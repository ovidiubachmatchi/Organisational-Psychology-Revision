# 9.1 NEED THEORIES

---

## Maslow (1943) -- Hierarchy of Needs

> TLDR
> - 5-level pyramid: physiology -> safety -> belonging -> esteem -> self-actualisation
> - Must satisfy lower needs before moving up
> - Bottom 4 = **deficiency needs**; top = **growth need**
> - Applied to work: pay & safety first, then belonging, then challenge/autonomy

### The 5 Levels (bottom to top)

| Level | Need | Workplace example |
|-------|------|-------------------|
| 1 | **Physiological** -- food, water, warmth, sleep | Adequate wages to buy food & stay warm |
| 2 | **Safety** -- security, stability, health | Pension, safe work environment |
| 3 | **Belongingness** -- friendship, connection, group | Feeling accepted, social interaction |
| 4 | **Esteem** -- respect, recognition, achievement | Promotion, better job title, new skills |
| 5 | **Self-actualisation** -- fulfilling potential | Autonomy, challenge, freedom from supervision |

### Key concepts

- **Deficiency needs** = levels 1-4; motivated by lacking something; drive gets stronger the longer unmet
- **Growth needs** = level 5; motivated by personal growth; motivation *increases* as you grow
- People can **slide back down** the hierarchy (e.g. job loss, divorce)
- Maslow believed everyone *can* reach self-actualisation but many never do

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Holistic** -- considers physiological AND psychological needs (more complete than cognitive theories) | **Validity issue** -- assumes lower needs must be met first, but people in poverty still show love & belonging (e.g. Mother Teresa) |
| **Useful application** -- employers can use it to improve wages, safety, belonging -> increases motivation | **Cultural bias** -- self-actualisation may mean different things in **collectivist** vs **individualistic** cultures |
| Humanistic = supports **free will** (you can choose to change jobs if needs unmet) | Behaviour still partly **determined** by external forces (e.g. can't find another job) |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Examiners love asking you to *apply* Maslow to a workplace scenario. Practice linking each level to a specific workplace action.

### Supporting study: Saeednia (2011)

- **Aim:** Develop a reliable scale (BNSS) to measure basic needs satisfaction in children
- **Method:** 13 children + parents & educators interviewed first (open-ended Qs -> qualitative data); themes coded using Maslow's theory; used to construct the **Basic Needs Satisfaction Scale (BNSS)**; after a pilot study, BNSS given to **300 participants** randomly chosen from 457
- **Result:** High reliability (0.83)
- **Conclusion:** Qualitative methods may be more appropriate for investigating Maslow -- children tend to "unconsciously pretend" a need is being met, especially if the environment won't help meet it
- **Finding:** Life satisfaction varies by culture -- poorer nations focus on safety; wealthier on love/esteem (consistent with Maslow)
- **Weakness 1:** Sample only from **wealthy areas of Tehran** -- low generalisability to less wealthy areas or nations
- **Weakness 2:** Children have **limited ability to express themselves fully** -- may affect **validity** of results (even though parents/educators were also interviewed)
- **Strength 1:** Shows Maslow's theory *can* be measured **quantitatively** -> overcomes criticism that the theory is unscientific; increases reliability
- **Strength 2:** One of the **first attempts** to produce a questionnaire for children's basic needs -- provides a basis for future research to refine the tool

## McClelland (1961) -- Achievement Motivation Theory

> TLDR
> - 3 work needs: **Achievement**, **Affiliation**, **Power**
> - Everyone has all 3 but one is **dominant** (shaped by life experience & culture)
> - Measured using the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** -- a projective test
> - Useful for placing employees in roles that suit their dominant need

### The 3 Work Needs

**1. Need for Achievement**
- Urge to master tasks and succeed
- Avoid *too easy* (no real achievement) AND *too hard* (success = luck)
- Prefer moderate challenge, regular feedback, working alone or with other high achievers
- McClelland said high achievers make the **best leaders**
- Low achievers: avoid responsibility, fear failure

**2. Need for Affiliation**
- Want social interaction, to be liked and accepted
- Conform to group norms, avoid conflict, fear rejection
- Prefer collaboration over competition
- Don't like being singled out (even for praise!)
- Suited to: customer service, team activities
- Low affiliators: distant, avoid social interaction

**3. Need for Power**
- Desire control, authority, influence over decisions
- Want prestige and status
- Two types:
  - **Personal power** = control over *others*
  - **Institutional (social) power** = organise a group to meet business goals
- Best managers have **institutional** power, not personal power
- Low power: dependent on others, won't lead

> ⚡ **Memory trick:** **A-A-P** = Achievement, Affiliation, Power. Think "AAP" like an app that matches people to jobs.

### Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

- **Projective test** -- shown ~20 ambiguous pictures
- Asked to make up a story about each one
- Stories reveal **unconscious motives/needs**
- McClelland devised a scoring system for power, affiliation, achievement

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Practical application** -- helps organisations place people in best-fit roles; improves productivity & retention | **Cultural/gender bias** -- constructed using Western males; may not apply to women or collectivist cultures (e.g. Japan) |
| Scoring system for TAT is relatively reliable & can be analysed statistically | **Only considers individual needs** -- ignores situational factors (e.g. power needs require others to be submissive) |
| | TAT measures **unobservable** characteristics from preconscious mind -- results can't be disproven |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare Maslow and McClelland -- both are need theories but Maslow = hierarchy (universal order), McClelland = individual dominant need (no fixed order). McClelland's needs are *learned*, Maslow's are *innate*.

# 9.1.2 COGNITIVE THEORIES OF MOTIVATION

---

## Locke & Latham -- Goal-Setting Theory

> TLDR
> - **Locke et al. (1968)** first proposed SMART goals; **Locke & Latham (1990)** expanded with 5 characteristics
> - Specific, challenging goals motivate better than vague ones
> - Use **SMART** goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound
> - 5 characteristics of effective goals: Clarity, Challenge, Commitment, Feedback, Task complexity
> - Based on 50+ years of research -- high reliability

### SMART Goals (Locke et al., 1968)

| Letter | Meaning | Key question |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **S** | Specific | What exactly do I want to achieve? |
| **M** | Measurable | How will I track progress? |
| **A** | Achievable | Is it realistic? Do I have the resources? |
| **R** | Realistic/Relevant | What's the benefit? |
| **T** | Time-bound | What's the deadline? |

- Best goals = ones you're **not 100% sure** you'll achieve, but realistic with hard work
- "Increase sales of fluffy pink slippers by 50% in 28 days" > "Let's get more slippers sold soon"

### 5 Characteristics of Effective Goal Setting (Locke & Latham, 1990)

1. **Clarity** -- specific, unambiguous, measurable
2. **Challenge** -- not too easy (demotivating), not unrealistic; linked to rewards
3. **Commitment** -- workforce must understand and be invested
4. **Feedback** -- regular feedback keeps people on track
5. **Task complexity** -- realistic timescales, sub-goals, regular reviews

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Evidence-based** -- built on 50+ years of research, still used today; high reliability | **Reductionist** -- only considers cognitive factors (goal-setting), ignores physiological needs & past experiences |
| **Useful application** -- helps managers plan, monitor progress, spot problems early; increases worker motivation | **Cultural bias** -- based on individualistic Western values (USA); may not generalise to collectivist cultures |
| Ethically sound -- improves workplace for both workers & managers | |

> ⚡ **Memory trick for 5 characteristics:** "**C**lever **C**ats **C**an **F**ind **T**una" = Clarity, Challenge, Commitment, Feedback, Task complexity

## Vroom (1964) -- VIE / Expectancy Theory

> TLDR
> - Motivation = **Valence x Instrumentality x Expectancy**
> - If ANY of the 3 is low/zero, motivation = low/zero
> - People make **rational, conscious** choices to maximise pleasure and minimise pain
> - Individual factors (personality, skills, experience) affect performance

### The 3 Beliefs (V-I-E)

| Factor | What it means | Workplace example |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Valence** | Strength/value of the expected reward (intrinsic or extrinsic) | Big bonus vs small bonus; does the employee actually *want* the reward? |
| **Instrumentality** | Belief that good performance WILL lead to the promised reward | Trust in decision-makers; clear, transparent link between performance and outcome |
| **Expectancy** | Belief that effort WILL lead to good performance | Having the right skills, resources, support; training & mentoring raise expectancy |

### The Formula

**Motivation = V x I x E**

- Multiplied, not added -- so if *any one* = 0, total motivation = 0
- Common theme: **reward** -- if reward is small, motivation tends to be low overall

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Predictive** -- Redmond (2009) found V, I, and E all correlated highly with predicting choices; useful theory | **Doesn't always apply** -- in real workplaces, rewards often aren't directly linked to performance |
| Considers **individual factors** (personality, skills) unlike Maslow who assumes everyone is the same | **Assumes rational choices** -- ignores short-term needs, personality quirks, unconscious processes |
| | **Nomothetic** -- establishes a formula for ALL workers; doesn't look at what uniquely motivates individuals |
| | **Environmentally deterministic** -- says motivation is determined by expected rewards/costs; ignores free will |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare Vroom with Maslow -- Vroom = individual differences matter (idiographic element); Maslow = everyone follows the same hierarchy (nomothetic). But Vroom's formula itself is nomothetic (applies to all).

> ⚡ **Memory trick:** **VIE** = "**V**ery **I**mportant **E**xpectations" -- all 3 must be high or motivation dies.

# 9.1.3 MOTIVATORS AT WORK

---

## Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation

> TLDR
> - **Extrinsic** = external rewards (pay, bonuses, profit sharing) -- driven by **operant conditioning**; short-term, can cause burnout
> - **Intrinsic** = internal feelings (recognition, praise, respect, empowerment, belonging) -- longer lasting
> - Over-reliance on extrinsic rewards can **decrease** intrinsic motivation
> - Best approach: combine both, but frame rewards supportively

### Extrinsic Motivators

| Type | What it is | Watch out for |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| **Pay** | Salary to cover bills and enjoy life | Basic motivator; expected, not bonus |
| **Bonuses** | Extra rewards based on targets (individual or company) | Can be monetary or non-monetary; motivates short-term effort |
| **Profit sharing** | % of company profits awarded to employees | Only works if done fairly; can boost morale & productivity |
| **Performance-related pay** | Reward when work meets a standard; reviewed via **appraisal** | Targets must be realistic or motivation *decreases* |

### Intrinsic Motivators

| Type | What it is | Key detail |
|------|-----------|------------|
| **Recognition** | Verbal appreciation for performance | Formal (appraisal) or informal; must be linked to job performance, not just targets |
| **Praise** | Saying "well done" | Over-praising *reduces* effect; must be meaningful and targeted |
| **Respect** | Active listening, not insulting or dismissing ideas | Direct impact on motivation and productivity |
| **Empowerment** | Letting workers make decisions about their own work; giving **autonomy** | Shows trust; gives sense of ownership; mastery of tasks |
| **Sense of belonging** | Feeling valued, trusted, proud to be part of the team | Links to Maslow's belongingness need |

## Deci & Ryan (1985) -- Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

> TLDR
> - People have a **need for growth** -- mastery over challenges is key
> - **Autonomous** (self-determined) motivation = intrinsic = best kind
> - 3 basic needs: **Autonomy**, **Relatedness**, **Competence** (ARC)
> - Too many extrinsic rewards can *kill* intrinsic motivation

### 2 Key Assumptions

1. **Need for growth drives behaviour** -- people want to improve and master challenges (linked to intrinsic motivation)
2. **Autonomous motivation is important** -- purely self-determined behaviour = intrinsic, done for pleasure; non-self-determined = no pleasure, no choice

### 3 Basic Psychological Needs

| Need | Definition | Increases when... | Decreases when... |
|------|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Autonomy** | Self-governance; control over own behaviour | Workers have choice over how they work | Controlled by others; constant deadlines; too many extrinsic rewards for already-intrinsic behaviour |
| **Relatedness** | Building relationships; feeling attached and belonging | Respected by others; people care about your feelings | Criticised; excluded from groups; isolated |
| **Competence** | Having the right skills to perform a task; mastery | Task matches skill level; positive/constructive feedback | Task too hard; too much negative feedback |

> ⚡ **Memory trick:** **ARC** = Autonomy, Relatedness, Competence. Think: "building an ARC of motivation to stay afloat at work."

### 🔑 Critical point: Extrinsic rewards can UNDERMINE intrinsic motivation

- If extrinsic rewards are given constantly for behaviour that was already intrinsically motivated...
- ...the worker starts caring ONLY about the reward, not the satisfaction
- This is supported by **Deci & Ryan (1999)**: reviewed 128 experiments -- task-dependent/performance-related rewards *decreased* intrinsic motivation; positive feedback *enhanced* it
- **Kohn (1994)**: using rewards to control behaviour leads to more surveillance and competition -> further decreases intrinsic motivation

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Research support** -- Deci & Ryan (1999) review of 128 experiments supports the theory | **Deterministic** -- suggests motivation is determined by type of reward received; ignores individual personality differences |
| **Practical application** -- managers can avoid controlling rewards and use collaborative atmosphere instead | **Ethnocentric** -- constructed using Western ideas of motivation; collectivist cultures may value autonomy less |
| Useful for real-life -- increases worker satisfaction & productivity | **Haggar et al. (2013)**: individualistic groups preferred personal choice; collectivist groups preferred being told by a manager -> different cultures, different motivators |

# KEY STUDY: Landry et al. (2019) -- Study 1

> TLDR
> - Tested whether HOW you frame a monetary reward affects motivation and performance
> - Key concept: **functional meaning** -- the meaning employees give to a reward (informational vs controlling)
> - **Autonomy-supportive framing** (informational) -> higher intrinsic motivation -> better performance
> - **Controlling framing** -> higher extrinsic motivation -> NO performance boost
> - Supports self-determination theory: it's not *whether* you reward, but *how*

### Hypotheses

- **H1:** Presenting rewards in an autonomy-supportive way (informational) leads to **greater performance** than presenting them in a controlling way
- **H2:** Informational rewards -> greater needs satisfaction -> higher **intrinsic** motivation -> better performance; Controlling rewards -> greater needs frustration -> higher **extrinsic** motivation

### The Study at a Glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Aim** | Does the way monetary rewards are presented affect motivation & performance? |
| **Method** | Lab experiment + self-report questionnaires (quantitative) |
| **Sample** | 123 student volunteers, mean age 23, Canadian university; **60% female**; randomly assigned |
| **IV** | Autonomy-supportive (informational) vs autonomy-threatening (controlling) instruction paragraph |
| **Task** | Solve 25 four-letter anagrams in 2 minutes |
| **Reward** | $10 gift card for local coffee shop |
| **DV** | Performance score, intrinsic/extrinsic motivation, needs satisfaction/frustration (measured via 7-point Likert scales: **Basic Psychological Needs Satisfaction Scale**, **Psychological Needs Thwarting Scale**, **Situational Motivation Scale**) |

### Two Conditions

| Autonomy-Supportive (n=65) | Controlling (n=58) |
|----------------------------|-------------------|
| Supportive, encouraging language | Pressurising language |
| Reward = "token of appreciation for contribution" | Reward = "to reinforce performance standards" |
| **Informational** meaning | **Controlling** meaning |

### Key Results

| Measure | Autonomy-supportive | Controlling |
|---------|-------------------|-------------|
| Performance | **0.76** | 0.54 |
| Intrinsic motivation | **5.31** | 4.56 |
| Extrinsic motivation | 3.10 | **3.95** |
| Needs satisfaction | **5.79** | 5.39 |
| Needs frustration | 2.41 | **2.83** |

- Autonomy-supportive group = **higher performance, higher intrinsic motivation, higher needs satisfaction**
- Controlling group = higher extrinsic motivation, higher needs frustration
- Extrinsic motivation did **NOT** predict performance

### Mediation (important for Hypothesis 2)

- **Needs satisfaction** acted as a **mediator** between condition and intrinsic motivation, AND between condition and performance
- **Needs frustration** mediated -> extrinsic motivation (significant)
- BUT: extrinsic motivation as a mediator between needs satisfaction and performance was **NOT significant**

### Conclusions

1. Rewards framed in an **autonomy-supportive** way -> increased performance
2. Autonomy-supportive rewards -> increased needs satisfaction -> intrinsic motivation -> better performance
3. Controlling rewards -> needs frustration -> extrinsic motivation -> NO performance benefit

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Good controls** -- measured perceived value of reward & affect (emotions) to control for individual differences; increases validity | **Sampling bias** -- all from one course at one Canadian university; hard to generalise to other cultures/workplaces (textbook notes "153" participants in evaluation section -- likely a typo; methodology says 123) |
| **Quantitative data** -- Likert scales + performance scores allow statistical comparison; increases reliability | **Low ecological validity** -- lab experiment, not a real workplace; artificial environment |
| **Ethical** -- informed consent, no deception, confidentiality maintained, no distress | **Reductionist** -- only considers self-determination; ignores personality, experience, age, pre-existing competence |
| **Practical application** -- shows organisations how to frame rewards to boost motivation & performance | Results may not transfer to real-life workplace situations |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Landry et al. is the **compulsory key study** for Motivation at Work. Be ready to describe the procedure, results AND evaluate it. Examiners especially like asking about: controls, ecological validity, sampling bias, and application to real life.

# QUICK-FIRE Q&A

### Likely exam questions with model answer bullet points

---

**Q1: Describe Maslow's hierarchy of needs. [6]**
- 5 levels: physiological, safety, belongingness, esteem, self-actualisation
- Lower needs must be satisfied before progressing upward
- Levels 1-4 = **deficiency needs** (motivated by what you lack)
- Level 5 = **growth need** (motivation increases as you grow)
- People can regress down the hierarchy (job loss, divorce)
- Applied to work: employers ensure wages, safety, belonging, recognition, then autonomy

---

**Q2: Evaluate Maslow's hierarchy of needs, including application to real life. [10]**
- **Strength -- application:** employers use it to ensure pay, safety, social needs met -> improves motivation & retention
- **Strength -- holistic:** considers both physiological AND psychological factors (unlike purely cognitive theories)
- **Weakness -- validity:** assumes fixed order but people in poverty can show love/belonging (Mother Teresa example)
- **Weakness -- cultural bias:** self-actualisation defined in Western/individualistic terms; collectivist cultures may differ
- **Debate -- free will vs determinism:** humanistic = supports free will, but external forces (e.g. no jobs available) can determine behaviour
- **Supporting evidence:** Saeednia (2011) -- BNSS scale shows needs differ by culture (poorer nations = safety; wealthier = love/esteem) -- supports Maslow but also shows cultural limitations

---

**Q3: Explain two characteristics of a person with high affiliation needs (McClelland). [4]**
- They want to be **liked and accepted** -- they conform to group norms and won't challenge the group because they fear rejection
- They **prefer collaboration over competition** -- they dislike being singled out (even for praise) and perform well in team activities

---

**Q4: Explain what is meant by a SMART target. [2]**
- A goal that is **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic, **T**ime-bound
- More effective than vague goals because progress can be tracked and outcomes are clear

---

**Q5: Outline what is meant by valence in Vroom's expectancy theory. [2]**
- **Valence** = the perceived strength or value of an expected reward
- It can be intrinsic (sense of achievement) or extrinsic (money/bonus); higher valence = higher motivation

---

**Q6: Explain why Locke and Latham's theory could be said to be reductionist. [2]**
- It only considers **cognitive factors** (setting specific, achievable goals)
- It ignores other factors like **physiological needs** (Maslow) or the **role of past experiences**, making it an incomplete explanation of motivation

---

**Q7: Explain one difference between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. [2]**
- **Extrinsic** motivation is driven by **external** factors such as pay, bonuses, or avoiding punishment
- **Intrinsic** motivation is driven by **internal** feelings such as satisfaction, pride, or a sense of achievement from completing a task

---

**Q8: Suggest two ways organisations could use self-determination theory to increase motivation. [4]**
- Increase **autonomy** by giving workers choice over how and when they complete tasks, which increases their sense of ownership and intrinsic motivation
- Increase **competence** by providing constructive feedback and matching task difficulty to workers' skill levels, so workers feel capable and can achieve mastery

---

**Q9: Describe the procedure and results of Landry et al. (2019). [6]**
- 123 students randomly assigned to autonomy-supportive or controlling condition
- Read an instruction paragraph framing a $10 gift card reward as either informational ("token of appreciation") or controlling ("reinforce performance standards")
- Completed 25 anagrams in 2 minutes; then rated needs satisfaction, motivation on Likert scales
- **Results:** autonomy-supportive group scored higher on performance (0.76 vs 0.54), intrinsic motivation (5.31 vs 4.56), and needs satisfaction (5.79 vs 5.39)
- Controlling group had higher extrinsic motivation (3.95 vs 3.10) and needs frustration (2.83 vs 2.41)
- Extrinsic motivation did NOT predict better performance

---

**Q10: Evaluate Landry et al. (2019), including a discussion of application to real life. [10]**
- **Strength -- controls:** measured reward value and affect to control individual differences -> increases validity
- **Strength -- quantitative data:** Likert scales and performance scores allow statistical comparison -> reliable
- **Strength -- application:** shows organisations that framing rewards supportively (informational) boosts intrinsic motivation and performance; practical for managers
- **Weakness -- ecological validity:** lab experiment, not a real workplace -> results may not transfer to actual jobs
- **Weakness -- sampling bias:** all participants from one Canadian university course -> low generalisability, especially cross-culturally
- **Weakness -- reductionist:** only considers self-determination; ignores personality, age, experience
- **Ethical strength:** informed consent, confidentiality, no deception or distress

---

**Q11: Compare two need theories of motivation. [6+]**
- **Maslow:** universal hierarchy; 5 levels in fixed order; deficiency + growth needs; innate
- **McClelland:** 3 needs (achievement, affiliation, power); no fixed order; one is dominant; **learned** through experience and culture
- **Similarity:** both identify core human needs that drive workplace motivation
- **Difference:** Maslow = fixed progression; McClelland = dominant need varies per person
- **Difference:** Maslow = can be applied to all of life; McClelland = specifically designed for the workplace
- Both criticised for **cultural bias** (Western, individualistic origins)
