## STUDY 1

### Claypoole & Szalma (2019) — Electronic Performance Monitoring improves vigilance performance

**1. Why this study exists**

By 2019, over **70% of US organisations** were using **Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)** — webcams, keystroke loggers, screen recorders — to track worker productivity.

But here's the problem: almost all prior research on EPM focused on **simple, repetitive tasks** like data entry or typing speed.

Nobody had asked: *What happens when the job actually requires you to stare at something for a long time and catch rare errors?*
That is called a **vigilance (sustained attention) task** — think air-traffic control, medical imaging, quality control on a production line, or driving.

There was also a theoretical reason: **social facilitation** theory says that being watched **improves performance on easy tasks** but **impairs it on hard ones** (because arousal amplifies the dominant response). But prior EPM research hadn't cleanly tested whether an *electronic* observer works the same way as a human observer on these mentally demanding, boring-but-critical vigilance tasks.

> 💡 The gap: Does EPM act like a human audience on tasks that need sustained attention? Can watching someone via webcam actually help them pay attention longer?

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you are a university student. You sign up for a psychology study on a website to earn course credit. You arrive at the lab.

**Step 1 — Hand over your phone and watch.**
The experimenter takes them away immediately. Why? Because looking at a phone or watch would tell you how much time has passed, which would change how alert you stay. The researchers needed participants to experience the full tedium of the task without temporal anchors.

**Step 2 — You are randomly assigned to one of two rooms.**

> **Room A — Control condition (n = 53):**
> Plain room. No camera. No recording device. You are completely alone for the entire experiment. Nobody is watching. Ever.

> **Room B — Electronic Presence condition (n = 53):**
> There is a **webcam on top of the computer screen**. Before you start, the researcher tells you:
> *"The webcam sends live video feed to the office next door so that performance and engagement can be evaluated during the task."*
> Behind the screen there is also a **video recorder**, and you are told:
> *"This records your performance so it can be evaluated later."*
> 🧠 Key twist: **Nothing was actually recorded.** The cameras were props. But the participants believed they were being watched in real time AND recorded for later review.

**Step 3 — You fill in a demographics questionnaire.**
Then a research assistant reviews the instructions, answers your questions, and leaves the room.

**Step 4 — 3-minute practice session.**
You learn the task on the computer.

**Step 5 — The 24-minute vigilance task.**
A stream of **two-digit numbers** appears on screen, one at a time, each shown for **1000 milliseconds (1 second)**.

You have to press the **spacebar** every time you see a **critical signal** — defined as:
- Two digits that are the **same** (e.g., 77)
- Two digits that differ by **1** (e.g., 65 or 01)

Everything else you ignore.

There are only **20 critical signals** across the 24 minutes (5 per 6-minute block), so the vast majority of numbers are irrelevant. The timing of signals is randomised but kept consistent across all participants.

> 💡 Analogy: It is like watching 1,440 cars drive past and having to press a button every time you see a red car — but only 20 of the 1,440 cars are red. It sounds easy until minute 18, when your mind starts to wander.

**Step 6 — Debrief.**
All participants are told the truth about the cameras. Total time: under 1 hour.

**Sample:** 106 undergraduates (mean age 20.57; 65 female, 41 male) from a Southern US university, recruited via an experiment sign-up website. All received course credit.

---

**3. What they found**

| Measure | Electronic Presence (n=53) | Control (n=53) |
|---|---|---|
| **Correct detections (mean proportion)** | **0.6906** | 0.6038 |
| **False alarms (mean proportion)** | **0.0059** | 0.0201 |
| **Median response time (ms)** | **767.34 ms** | 802.02 ms |

**In plain English:**

- Monitored participants correctly spotted **more critical signals** (69% vs 60%).
- Monitored participants made **far fewer false alarms** — pressing spacebar when they shouldn't (0.6% vs 2%).
- Monitored participants were **faster** to respond (767 ms vs 802 ms).
- Both groups got **worse over time** as the task dragged on — consistent with classic vigilance decrement — but the monitored group declined less.

> 🧠 The electronic presence acted like having someone watching over your shoulder — it kept you sharper, longer.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Confirms that **EPM can be applied to vigilance tasks** — not just simple clerical work.
- Suggests organisations in **safety-critical industries** (aviation, healthcare, transport) could use webcam monitoring to reduce errors caused by attention lapses.
- Supports **social facilitation theory** extending to electronic observers — you don't need a human in the room; a camera is enough.
- Offers a **cost-effective tool**: webcams are cheap. If they boost vigilance performance, that has real economic and safety value.

> ⚡ But also raises a darker question: is it ethical to keep workers under constant surveillance? The study itself notes that persistent monitoring could cause stress and be counterproductive in the long run.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

| | Point | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ | **Laboratory experiment with standardised procedure** | Controls all variables except the IV (electronic presence); same task, same instructions, same timing for both groups | High **internal validity** — confident that any differences are caused by the camera, not by chance |
| ✅ | **Quantitative data** (detection rates, response times, false alarms) | Objective, not subject to experimenter bias; can be compared statistically | High **reliability**; results can be replicated |
| ✅ | **Random assignment** to conditions | Reduces participant variables biasing one group | Controls for pre-existing differences in alertness or ability |
| ❌ | **Unrepresentative sample** — university undergraduates from one Southern US university | Students are younger, more educated, and may be less experienced with vigilance work than real workers | Low **population validity** — cannot generalise to air-traffic controllers, truck drivers, radiologists |
| ❌ | **Low ecological validity** of the task | Watching digits and pressing a spacebar is nothing like real-world vigilance tasks; no real consequences for failing | Results may not transfer to actual workplaces where stakes and context differ |
| ❌ | **Deception** — cameras were not actually recording | Participants believed they were monitored when they were not | Ethical concern (though debrief was given); results depend on belief, not actual monitoring — raises question of whether real EPM would work differently |
| ❌ | **Student sample = response bias** | Only certain types of students sign up to experiment websites | Sample may be more conscientious or motivated than average workers |

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- IV: Electronic presence (webcam + recorder) vs. no presence (control)
- DV: Correct signal detections, false alarms, response time
- Key finding: EPM **improved** vigilance performance
- Theoretical link: **social facilitation** + **evaluation apprehension**
- Key weakness: low ecological validity + unrepresentative sample
- Key strength: standardised lab procedure = high internal validity

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**

> Claypoole & Szalma (2019) aimed to test whether EPM could improve performance on a sustained attention (vigilance) task using social facilitation effects. 106 university undergraduates were randomly assigned to either a control condition (no monitoring) or an electronic presence condition (webcam + video recorder). Both groups completed a 24-minute task watching two-digit numbers and pressing a spacebar when a critical signal appeared. Results showed the monitored group detected significantly more critical signals (M=0.6906 vs M=0.6038), made fewer false alarms, and had faster response times. They concluded that EPM can improve vigilance performance and that video-based monitoring is an effective and less intrusive form of EPM.

## STUDY 2

### Einarsen (1999) — The nature and causes of bullying at work: a literature review

**1. Why this study exists**

Workplace bullying was, by the late 1990s, known to be damaging — but poorly understood. Most research focused on **school bullying** (the playground kind). Einarsen wanted to map out what workplace bullying actually *looks like*, how it *escalates*, and *why* it starts.

Rather than running a new experiment, he synthesised what was already known — a **literature review** (also called a review article).

> 💡 Think of it like a documentary made from other people's footage: Einarsen gathered and organised the best available studies on workplace bullying to give a clear, complete picture.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

There are no participants here in the traditional sense. Einarsen sat down with the existing body of research — Norwegian studies, Irish interview studies, questionnaire data — and read it carefully, looking for patterns.

He asked three questions:
1. **What types of bullying happen at work?**
2. **How does bullying progress over time (phases)?**
3. **What causes bullying in the first place?**

He drew on multiple prior studies including:
- **Einarsen et al. (1994)** — Norwegian survey of victims (types and phases)
- **Seign (1996)** — interviews with 30 Irish bullying victims (causes)
- **Bjorkqvist et al. (1994)** — causes from the bully's perspective

> 💡 A literature review is like being a judge who reads all the witness statements and then summarises what probably happened, rather than interviewing witnesses yourself.

---

**3. What they found**

**Types of bullying (5 types):**

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Work-related** | Changing or making tasks harder | Given impossible deadlines, moved to worse shifts |
| **Social isolation** | Excluding the victim | Excluded from team lunches, ignored in meetings |
| **Personal ridicule** | Insults, gossip about private life | Mocked in front of colleagues, rumours spread |
| **Verbal threats** | Yelled at, criticised, humiliated | Shouted at in public, publicly blamed for mistakes |
| **Physical violence** | Threats or acts of physical harm | Only ~2.4% of Norwegian cases — the rarest form |

> 🧠 Most workplace bullying is **verbal and social**, not physical. This surprises many people who associate bullying with hitting.

**Phases of bullying (4 stages — escalation model):**

| Phase | Name | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Aggressive behaviour** | Subtle, indirect aggression — snide comments, small exclusions. Victim may not even realise it is bullying yet |
| 2 | **Bullying** | Aggression becomes more open, direct, and frequent. Victim starts to lose the ability to defend themselves |
| 3 | **Stigmatisation** | Victim is labelled and set apart. Others in the workplace adopt a negative view of the victim ("they brought it on themselves") |
| 4 | **Trauma** | Physical and mental health severely damaged. Depression, anxiety, PTSD-like symptoms |

> 💡 Think of it like a fire: Phase 1 is a spark. By Phase 4, the whole building is burning. The key is catching it at Phase 1.

**Causes of bullying (3 sources):**

| Source | What the research found |
|---|---|
| **The bully** (Bjorkqvist et al., 1994) | Competition for status/jobs; **envy** of the victim; bully's own **insecurity and self-doubt** |
| **The victim** | Victims described as overachievers, highly rigid, overly honest/punctual — traits that can irritate colleagues |
| **The workplace** | Poor work design; weak/absent leadership; socially exposed positions; low moral standards in the department |

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Gives organisations a **framework to detect bullying early** — if managers know Phase 1 exists, they can act before Phase 4 destroys someone.
- Identifies that bullying is **not one person's fault** — it involves personality (bully + victim) AND the work environment.
- The 5 types help HR departments create **specific policies** and training ("this behaviour counts as bullying, this does not").
- The 4-phase model is now widely used in **anti-bullying programmes** across Europe.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

| | Point | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ | **Holistic approach** — considers bully, victim, AND workplace factors | Does not reduce bullying to just one person's fault | More **complete explanation** that can inform multi-level organisational interventions |
| ✅ | **Application to real life** — 4 phases give early-warning system | Organisations can train managers to spot Phase 1 | **Reduces harm** — catching it early means less damage to victim and organisation |
| ✅ | **Draws on multiple studies** across different countries (Norway, Ireland) | Cross-cultural basis | **Greater generalisability** than a single study |
| ❌ | **Literature review methodology** — uses only secondary sources | Cannot verify the quality or methodology of the original studies | **Lower reliability** — flawed original studies contaminate the conclusions |
| ❌ | **Blaming the victim** — says victims are "overachievers" who "annoy others" | Implies personality causes victimisation | **Ethically problematic** — can cause further psychological harm to victims; shifts responsibility away from the bully |
| ❌ | **Self-report data** in many original studies | Victims may exaggerate; bullies may minimise | **Social desirability bias** reduces validity of underlying data |
| ❌ | **Cultural bias** — heavy reliance on Norwegian and Irish studies | Workplace cultures differ internationally (especially collectivist vs individualist cultures) | Limited **cross-cultural validity** |

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Methodology: **literature review** (secondary sources, NOT primary data)
- 5 types of bullying: work-related, social isolation, ridicule, verbal threats, physical violence
- 4 phases: aggressive behaviour → bullying → stigmatisation → trauma
- 3 causes: the bully (envy, insecurity), the victim (overachiever, rigid), the workplace (poor leadership, work design)
- Key strength: holistic (individual + situational)
- Key weakness: blaming the victim is ethically problematic; review methodology has reliability issues

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**

> Einarsen (1999) used a literature review methodology to summarise existing research on the nature and causes of bullying at work. He identified five types of bullying including work-related bullying, social isolation, personal ridicule, verbal threats, and physical violence — noting that physical violence occurs in only around 2.4% of cases. He also outlined four phases through which bullying escalates: aggressive behaviour, overt bullying, stigmatisation, and trauma. Regarding causes, research highlighted three sources: characteristics of the bully (e.g., envy, insecurity), characteristics of the victim (e.g., overachieving, rigidity), and workplace factors (e.g., poor leadership, weak work design). He concluded that more empirical research was needed, particularly into the subjective experience of victims.

## STUDY 3

### Thomas & Kilmann (1970s) — Five conflict-handling modes (TKI model)

**1. Why this study exists**

Conflict at work is inevitable. But different people deal with it in completely different ways — some fight, some give in, some hide. Thomas and Kilmann wanted to **measure** these differences systematically and give organisations a tool to understand how their employees naturally respond to conflict.

> 💡 The goal was not to find the "best" style — it was to map the full range of styles, so people could become **aware of their own tendencies** and choose more deliberately.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Thomas and Kilmann started with a theoretical insight: when you are in conflict with someone at work, your response can be described along **two dimensions**:

1. **How much are you trying to satisfy YOUR OWN concerns?** (Assertiveness: low → high)
2. **How much are you trying to satisfy THE OTHER PERSON'S concerns?** (Cooperativeness: low → high)

Plot those two axes on a grid. Every possible conflict response falls somewhere on that grid. Five distinct regions emerge — five **modes**:

> Think of it like a map of conflict responses, with your ego-drive on the vertical axis and your empathy on the horizontal axis.

They then built the **Thomas-Kilmann Instrument (TKI)** — a **30-item forced-choice questionnaire**.

Each question gives you **two statements** (A and B) about how you respond to conflict. You must pick one. There is no "neither" option. This forces you off the fence.

Example (paraphrased):
> A: "I try to find a compromise solution."
> B: "I try to deal with all of the other person's concerns as well as my own."

Your pattern of choices across 30 pairs places you in one of the five modes.

---

**3. What they found**

The model produces five conflict-handling modes:

| Mode | Assertiveness | Cooperativeness | In plain English | Best used when... |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Competing** | HIGH | LOW | "I will win. I don't care if you lose." | Emergency decisions; when you know you are right and stakes are high |
| **Accommodating** | LOW | HIGH | "Fine, have it your way." | Preserving the relationship matters more than the outcome; or when you realise you are wrong |
| **Avoiding** | LOW | LOW | "I'm not dealing with this." | Issue is trivial; you need time to cool down |
| **Collaborating** | HIGH | HIGH | "Let's both win. We'll solve this together." | Complex issues where both parties' needs matter; long-term relationships |
| **Compromising** | MEDIUM | MEDIUM | "We'll both give a little and both get a little." | Quick resolution needed; both parties equal in power |

> 💡 Most people have a **default** style. But every mode has a time and place. The TKI helps you see which ones you overuse or underuse.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Widely used in **HR training and organisational development** worldwide.
- Helps managers understand why the same person handles conflict well in one situation but badly in another.
- If you know your team includes one "Competitor" and two "Avoiders," you can predict where conflict will get stuck.
- Conflict resolution training can use TKI results to **coach people toward more adaptive styles**.

> 🧠 The TKI is now published by Myers-Briggs Company and is one of the most-used conflict assessment tools globally.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

| | Point | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ | **Practical application** — gives individuals and organisations a clear, actionable framework | People can identify their style and consciously adjust | **Real-world impact** on conflict management training |
| ✅ | **Nomothetic approach** — produces normative data across many people | Can compare individuals to population norms | Allows organisations to assess team conflict dynamics systematically |
| ✅ | **Quantitative** — forced-choice format produces scores that can be compared | Reduces ambiguity in classification | Objective, replicable measurement |
| ❌ | **Social desirability bias** — forced-choice questionnaire | People may pick the answer that makes them look best, not the one that is truest | **Lower validity** — scores may not reflect real behaviour |
| ❌ | **Forced-choice format** | Respondent may disagree with BOTH options but must pick one | Scores may misclassify people; reduces **face validity** |
| ❌ | **Idiographic limitation** — assumes all behaviour fits two dimensions and five modes | Ignores individual complexity and context | May oversimplify responses; misses **unique conflict situations** |
| ❌ | **Self-report only** — does not observe actual conflict behaviour | People may not know how they really act under pressure | Gap between **self-perception and actual behaviour** |
| ❌ | **Style assumed to be learnable** — implies people can change modes | May underestimate how fixed personality-based styles are | May create **unrealistic expectations** in training programmes |

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Two underlying dimensions: **assertiveness** (own concerns) and **cooperativeness** (others' concerns)
- Five modes: Competing, Accommodating, Avoiding, Collaborating, Compromising
- Instrument: **TKI** — 30 forced-choice pairs of statements
- Key strength: practical, widely applicable
- Key weakness: **social desirability bias**; forced-choice lowers validity
- Debate link: **nomothetic vs idiographic** (TKI is nomothetic — assumes universal dimensions)

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe the modes, 6 marks):**

> Thomas and Kilmann proposed five conflict-handling modes based on two dimensions: assertiveness (satisfying one's own concerns) and cooperativeness (satisfying others' concerns). Competing is high assertiveness/low cooperativeness — pursuing your own goals at others' expense. Accommodating is the opposite — low assertiveness/high cooperativeness — giving up your own needs to satisfy others. Avoiding is low on both — neither party's concerns are addressed. Collaborating is high on both — working together for a mutually beneficial solution. Compromising is moderate on both — a middle-ground solution. Each person's style is measured using the Thomas-Kilmann Instrument (TKI), which presents 30 forced-choice pairs of statements.

## SUPPORTING STUDY A

### Sherif (1962) — Robbers Cave Experiment: competition for scarce resources creates conflict

> This is cited in the textbook (page 711) as supporting evidence for **realistic conflict theory** as a cause of inter-group conflict at work.

**1. Why this study exists**

Muzafer Sherif wanted to test whether **competition for limited resources** was enough on its own to generate hostility and conflict between groups — even groups of perfectly normal people who had no history of conflict.

> 💡 The idea: you don't need bad people to create conflict. You just need two groups chasing the same prize.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

The study took place at **Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma** — a 200-acre summer camp. The participants were **22 eleven-to-twelve-year-old boys**, all similar backgrounds: white, Protestant, middle-class, two-parent homes, average intelligence. None had met before.

The researchers posed as camp staff. The boys had no idea they were in a psychology experiment.

**Stage 1 — In-group formation (approx. 1 week):**
The boys were split into two groups of 11 and kept completely separate. Each group bonded through camping activities, chose a name (the **Eagles** and the **Rattlers**), and developed their own group identity.

**Stage 2 — Competition (approx. 4–6 days):**
The researchers introduced a **tournament** with prizes. The groups competed in baseball, tug-of-war, and other activities. Only ONE group could win.

> What happened? Almost immediately, hostility erupted. The groups raided each other's cabins, burned each other's flags, name-called, and refused to eat in the same canteen. The researchers had to physically separate them. The study was terminated before the third phase was fully complete due to the level of aggression.

**Stage 3 — Resolution (Superordinate goals):**
Sherif tried to reduce conflict by getting the groups to work together on shared goals (e.g., a broken-down water truck that both groups needed to fix). Cooperation on superordinate goals gradually reduced hostility.

---

**3. What they found**

- **Competition for a scarce resource** (the prize) was sufficient to generate **serious inter-group conflict** within days.
- Hostility included verbal abuse, physical confrontation, and deliberate sabotage.
- **Superordinate goals** — shared challenges requiring both groups — gradually reduced conflict.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides **direct experimental evidence** for **realistic conflict theory** — competition creates hostility.
- Applies directly to workplaces: departments competing for budget, workers competing for promotion, teams competing for company resources.
- Superordinate goals suggest a **conflict resolution strategy**: give competing workplace groups a shared target that both need each other to achieve.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

| | Point | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ | **High ecological validity** — real camp, real activities, real children | Behaviour was natural, not artificially constructed | Findings likely reflect genuine group dynamics |
| ✅ | **Controlled variables** — boys were matched on background | Reduces alternative explanations for conflict | Confident that competition (not pre-existing differences) caused hostility |
| ❌ | **Serious ethical issues** | Boys did not consent; were deceived; exposed to real aggression and psychological harm | Violates modern ethical guidelines — could not be repeated |
| ❌ | **Biased sample** — only white, middle-class, Protestant American boys | Completely unrepresentative of the global population | **Cannot generalise** to diverse workplaces or non-Western cultures |
| ❌ | **Demand characteristics** were possible | Researchers posed as staff — could have inadvertently encouraged aggression | May have inflated the level of conflict observed |
| ❌ | **Temporal validity** — conducted in 1954/1961 | Social norms, children's behaviour, and camp culture have changed significantly | Results may not apply to modern organisations |

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Used to support: **inter-group conflict** caused by competition for **scarce resources**
- Type of study: **field experiment** (quasi-experiment — no full random assignment to natural groups)
- Key finding: competition → immediate hostility; superordinate goals → reduced conflict
- Key weakness: ethics (no consent, deception, harm to children)
- Workplace application: departments competing for budget = same dynamic as Rattlers vs Eagles

## SUPPORTING STUDY B

### Aiello & Svec (1993) — EPM impairs performance on complex tasks

> Cited in the textbook (page 705) as **background context** for Claypoole & Szalma (2019). Understanding it helps explain what Claypoole & Szalma were responding to.

**1. Why this study exists**

Before Claypoole & Szalma, Aiello & Svec (1993) tested whether **electronic monitoring** (computer-based) could replicate the effects of a human audience under **social facilitation theory**.

Social facilitation predicts: an audience helps performance on **easy tasks** but hurts it on **difficult ones** (because it raises arousal, which amplifies your strongest/most practised response — good if the task is simple, bad if it's hard).

Aiello & Svec asked: does an *electronic* audience create the same effect?

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Participants completed **anagram tasks** (rearranging letters to make words) — which is a **cognitively complex** task. They were assigned to one of three conditions:
1. **Alone** — no monitoring
2. **Monitored electronically** — computer tracked their performance
3. **Monitored by a person** — human observer present

---

**3. What they found**

- Performance on the **complex anagram task was significantly impaired** for both the electronically monitored AND the personally monitored groups compared to the alone group.
- Electronic monitoring produced the **same effect as human monitoring** — it created evaluation apprehension even though no human was physically present.

> 💡 This was important: it confirmed that an *electronic* "audience" triggers the same psychological response as a real human one. You feel watched. Your arousal increases. Hard tasks suffer.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Established the **theoretical foundation** for understanding EPM as a form of social facilitation.
- Set the scene for Claypoole & Szalma — who then showed that the story is more complex: EPM impairs *complex* tasks but *improves* vigilance (boring but demanding attention) tasks.
- Has direct implications: organisations using EPM on workers doing cognitively complex work (e.g., programming, design, analysis) may inadvertently **reduce performance**.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

| | Point | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ | **Established key theoretical link** between EPM and social facilitation | Had not been done before at this scale | Opened an entire research field |
| ✅ | **Laboratory control** — standardised conditions | Can attribute results to monitoring condition | High internal validity |
| ❌ | **Anagram task** — not a real workplace task | Lacks ecological validity | Results may not transfer to actual office or factory environments |
| ❌ | **Sample** — likely undergraduate students | Unrepresentative | Low generalisability |
| ❌ | **Only tested complex tasks** | Did not test easy tasks or sustained attention tasks | Left the EPM story incomplete — Claypoole & Szalma later had to fill this gap |

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- EPM **impaired** complex task performance (same as human monitoring)
- Theoretical basis: **social facilitation** + **evaluation apprehension**
- Key contribution: proved electronic observer = social presence psychologically
- Key limitation: only complex tasks; low ecological validity
- Used as **contrast** to Claypoole & Szalma: different task type, opposite finding on vigilance

## Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)** | Use of technology (webcams, software, keystroke loggers) to track worker performance |
| **Vigilance / sustained attention** | Maintaining focus over a long period to detect rare, critical signals |
| **Evaluation apprehension** | Anxiety caused by knowing you are being assessed; the psychological mechanism behind social facilitation effects |
| **Social facilitation** | The tendency for performance to improve on easy tasks and worsen on difficult tasks when others are present |
| **Intra-individual conflict** | Conflict within a single person (e.g., competing goals or values) |
| **Inter-individual conflict** | Conflict between two individuals (behavioural, cognitive, or affective components) |
| **Intra-group conflict** | Conflict between members of the same group (process, task, or relationship conflict) |
| **Inter-group conflict** | Conflict between different groups; linked to social identity theory (in-group vs out-group) |
| **Realistic conflict theory** | Theory that competition for scarce resources causes inter-group hostility |
| **Social identity theory** | Tajfel & Turner — we derive self-esteem from group membership; leads to in-group favouritism and out-group derogation |
| **Psychosocial factors** | Psychological and social workplace conditions that affect behaviour (e.g., leadership, work design) |
| **Social stigma** | A mark of disgrace that sets a person apart — applied to bullying victims in Phase 3 |
| **Literature review** | A summary and synthesis of existing published research on a topic (secondary, not primary data) |
| **Thomas-Kilmann Instrument (TKI)** | 30-item forced-choice questionnaire measuring conflict-handling mode |
| **Forced-choice questionnaire** | Respondent must choose between two options with no neutral response available |
