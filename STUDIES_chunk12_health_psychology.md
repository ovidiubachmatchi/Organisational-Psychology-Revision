# Studies: Health Psychology — Individual Factors (8.5.3)

> Chapter 8.5.3 covers how **individual psychological factors** — particularly **unrealistic optimism** and **positive psychology** — shape whether people follow health advice or change their health beliefs.
> Three key studies: **Weinstein (1980)**, **Seligman (2004)**, and **Shoshani & Steinmetz (2014)**.

---

## What is Unrealistic Optimism? (Core Concept)

**Unrealistic optimism** = the cognitive belief that:
- **Negative events** are *less* likely to happen to **you** than to others
- **Positive events** are *more* likely to happen to **you** than to others

> 💡 Think of it as: "Cancer? That's something that happens to *other people*, not me."

This matters for health psychology because if someone thinks a health risk doesn't apply to them, they won't change their behaviour — they won't quit smoking, get screened, or take a vaccine seriously.

---

## Study 1: Weinstein (1980) — "It won't happen to me"

### Weinstein (1980) — People systematically believe bad things will happen to others, not themselves

**1. Why this study exists**

Before Weinstein, psychologists knew people could be optimistic — but was this optimism *realistic* or *systematically distorted*?
Weinstein wanted to test whether this bias was a **genuine, measurable pattern** across the population, and find out *what drives it* — is it just wishful thinking, or does it depend on how controllable the event feels?

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you are a student at an American university in 1980. You walk into your psychology lecture and the researcher hands you a rating form.

**Study 1 — The Rating Task (main group)**

There are 258 college students. Each gets a form listing **21 life events** — a random mix of positive things (like *owning your own home*) and negative things (like *being fired from a job*).

The form asks one simple question for each event:

> *"Compared to other students of the same sex as you — what do you think are the chances this will happen to YOU?"*

You answer on a scale from "100% less than average" (no chance at all) to "100% more than average".

💡 **Key trick:** Because everyone in the room is comparing themselves to "their classmates," if the class as a whole is biased, you can *measure* the direction and size of that bias — because mathematically, the average for the group should be zero (you can't all be below average).

The 42 events were split randomly into **two different forms of 21 events each**, handed to alternate students — so no one form was overloaded with one type.

---

**Study 2 — The Event Characteristics Group**

A *different* group of 120 female undergraduates rated the **same 42 events** on three scales:

| Scale | What it measured | Range |
|---|---|---|
| **Controllability** | Can a person's actions change whether this happens? | 1 (nothing you can do) → 5 (completely controllable) |
| **Desirability** | How good or bad is this event? | 1 (extremely undesirable) → 5 (extremely desirable) |
| **Personal experience** | Has this happened to you or people you know? | 1 (no one I know) → 5 (happened to me more than once) |

💡 **Why measure these?** Weinstein had a theory: the amount of unrealistic optimism for any given event would depend on these three factors. This group's ratings let him test that theory statistically.

---

**3. What they found**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Direction of bias | On **37 out of 42 events**, participants showed the predicted pattern (rating themselves as more likely to have good things happen, less likely to have bad things happen) |
| Exceptions | Participants did NOT rate themselves significantly less likely to be a *victim of burglary*, nor more likely to be *not ill all winter* |
| Positive events | Correlated with both how **desirable** the event was AND how likely participants thought it was |
| Negative events | Correlated with **perceived controllability** AND **personal experience** — not with desirability |
| The "stereotype" mechanism | For controllable negative events, people had a mental image of the "type of person" this happens to. They compared themselves to that stereotype — and concluded *they* were different/better. Hence: below-average risk. |

> **Key conclusion:** Unrealistic optimism is strongest when an event feels **controllable** AND people have some **emotional investment** in the outcome. Desirability alone is not enough — especially for negative events.

---

**4. Why it matters**

This study proved that optimism bias is **not just vague wishfulness** — it is a **systematic, measurable cognitive distortion** that affects how people evaluate health risks.

If someone believes that getting lung cancer from smoking is something that happens to *other types of people* (careless people, unhealthy people), they will feel their own risk is below average — and they will **ignore health warnings**.

This is why health campaigns that say "smoking kills" may fail: the smoker thinks *yes, but not me.*

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

| Strength | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Nomothetic approach** | By testing a *group*, Weinstein could statistically show the bias was a genuine, population-level pattern | Findings can be generalised — it is not just one person's quirk |
| **Controlled methodology** | Randomly dividing events between two forms, standardised instructions | Reduces order effects and experimenter bias |
| **Multiple event types** | 18 positive + 24 negative events = broad coverage | More reliable conclusions about what *types* of events trigger bias |

**Weaknesses:**

| Weakness | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Student sample only** | All participants were American college students | Older adults may have more realistic views — findings may not generalise |
| **Individualistic culture** | US students are from an individualistic culture where personal control is valued | Research (Heine & Lehman, 1995) shows people from collectivistic cultures (e.g., Japan) show less unrealistic optimism — so findings are **culturally specific** |
| **Social desirability / demand characteristics** | Participants might have *guessed* the purpose and adjusted responses | Could inflate or deflate the measured bias |
| **Self-report** | Ratings are subjective estimates, not objective probabilities | Low validity — how do you *really* know your risk of burglary? |

---

**6. Exam tips**

**Must-knows:**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What was the IV?** | Type of life event (positive vs negative) + characteristics (controllability, desirability, personal experience) |
| **What was the DV?** | Self-rated comparative probability |
| **Key finding (1 sentence)** | People rated themselves as more likely to experience positive events and less likely to experience negative events than their classmates — especially when events were perceived as controllable |
| **Key mechanism** | Stereotype comparison: "the type of person that happens to" → "that's not me" |
| **Key limitation** | Only American students → cultural and age bias, limited generalisability |

**Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**

> Weinstein (1980) investigated whether people show unrealistic optimism by asking 258 American college students to compare their own chances of experiencing 42 life events (18 positive, 24 negative) to the chances of their classmates. A separate group of 120 students rated the same events for controllability, desirability, and personal experience. Results showed that on 37 of 42 events, students rated themselves as more likely to have positive events and less likely to have negative events happen to them than their peers. This effect was strongest for events perceived as controllable, as people formed a stereotype of the type of person to whom the event would occur and compared themselves favourably to that stereotype.

## Study 2: Seligman (2004) — Positive Psychology Goes to University

### Seligman (2004) — Teaching students to live a pleasant, good, and meaningful life

**1. Why this study exists**

Psychology in the 20th century focused almost entirely on what goes *wrong* — mental illness, disorders, deficits. In 1998, Martin Seligman became President of the American Psychological Association and made a radical argument: **why aren't we studying what goes right?**

His 2004 article is not a traditional experiment — it is an **overview of a seminar course** he taught at the University of Pennsylvania. Its purpose was to show that positive psychology principles could be *actively taught and practised*, not just theorised.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Picture yourself as an undergraduate at Penn. You sign up for a 4-week seminar with Seligman himself. This is not a normal lecture. Every week, you go away and *do things* — real-world homework designed to make you measurably happier.

**The course structure:**

Students reviewed scientific literature across three time frames:

| Time frame | Focus |
|---|---|
| **The past** | Contentment, satisfaction, gratitude |
| **The present** | Joy, comfort, pleasure, mindfulness |
| **The future** | Optimism, hope, trust, faith |

They also learned a sobering fact — called **depressive realism**: *unhappy people are sometimes MORE accurate than happy people.* And that even lottery winners return to their pre-existing happiness level within a year (Brickman et al., 1978) — because about 50% of our capacity for pleasure is inherited.

**The homework assignments:**

**Assignment 1 — Gratitude Night**

> Research shows that consciously counting what you're grateful for raises happiness.

Students organised a "Gratitude Night" — they invited family members or important people in their lives, then stood up and **read a testimonial** aloud, explaining why that person mattered and thanking them. Face to face. Out loud. In public.

💡 This is not journaling privately — it is *performed* gratitude, and the social act of saying it aloud deepens the emotion.

---

**Assignment 2 — Pleasurable Day**

Students learned three skills:
- **Savouring**: sharing experiences, taking mental photographs, keeping physical mementos (ticket stubs, photos)
- **Mindfulness**: being fully present in the moment, slowing down
- **Perspective shifting**: seeing familiar things from a new angle

Their homework: plan and carry out an entire **Pleasurable Day** using these skills.

---

**Assignment 3 — Signature Strengths**

Students completed the **Values in Action (VIA) Questionnaire** — a self-report psychometric tool that identifies their top **5 signature strengths** out of 24 possibilities (e.g., creativity, curiosity, kindness, bravery, fairness, humour, spirituality).

Then they chose a *boring, tedious task* they had been avoiding and figured out how to complete it using one of their signature strengths.

> 🧠 Example: A student with "curiosity" as a strength had to do data entry. Instead of grinding through it, he looked for patterns and themes in the data — turning a dull chore into a detective puzzle.

---

**The Meaningful Life (final phase):**

Students pushed beyond personal gain into contributing to something larger:
- Created a **family tree of strengths** — had family members take the VIA test and interviewed parents about deceased relatives
- **Mentored a younger student** who faced difficulties they themselves had overcome
- Wrote a **positive vision for humanity's future** and their own role in it
- Wrote their **obituary from the viewpoint of their future grandchildren**

---

**3. What they found**

This is not a controlled experiment — there is no control group, no statistical analysis. It is a **course description and theoretical overview**.

Key claims from the article:
- Practising optimism **increases productivity, improves physical health, and lowers vulnerability to depression**
- The three components of a happy life are the **pleasant life, the good life, and the meaningful life**
- People whose happiness comes from *meaningful engagement* (using strengths for others) report deeper, more lasting wellbeing than those pursuing only pleasure

| Component | Focus | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Pleasant life** | Positive emotions | Enjoying good food, time with friends |
| **Good life** | Signature strengths | Using creativity or humour to improve your work |
| **Meaningful life** | Contributing to something bigger | Volunteering, mentoring, civic engagement |

---

**4. Why it matters**

Seligman's work shifts the entire focus of health psychology — from "how do we fix what's broken" to "how do we help people flourish."

The practical takeaway: **positive psychology can be taught, practised, and applied in schools, workplaces, and clinics** — not just studied in labs.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

| Strength | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Real-world application** | Students practised techniques in their actual lives, not just in a lab | High ecological validity — findings relate to genuine behaviour change |
| **Broad research base** | Seligman draws on multiple supporting studies | Conclusions are not based on one experiment alone |
| **Replicable structure** | The programme can be adopted by other universities and schools | High practical utility |

**Weaknesses:**

| Weakness | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Not a controlled experiment** | No control group, no random assignment | Cannot establish causality — we don't know if the activities *caused* improvements |
| **Self-report (VIA)** | Students may choose flattering strengths rather than genuine ones | Social desirability bias — reduces validity of strength identification |
| **Nomothetic assumption** | Assumes everyone benefits from positive thinking | Ignores individuals for whom acknowledging negative emotions is important (e.g., people with chronic illness) |
| **Limited sample** | Ivy League undergraduates at Penn | Not representative of people from different SES, cultural backgrounds, or age groups |

---

**6. Exam tips**

**Must-knows:**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What type of study is this?** | Overview/seminar description — NOT an experiment |
| **Three happy life components** | Pleasant (emotions), Good (strengths), Meaningful (institutions/purpose) |
| **VIA questionnaire** | Identifies top 5 of 24 signature strengths via self-report |
| **Key limitation** | No control group → cannot prove causation |
| **Key strength** | High real-world application — activities designed for everyday life |

**Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**

> Seligman (2004) described a four-week positive psychology seminar at the University of Pennsylvania designed to help students live a pleasant, good, and meaningful life. Students completed homework tasks including a Gratitude Night (reading testimonials to important people), a Pleasurable Day (using mindfulness and savouring), and a Signature Strengths exercise (using the VIA questionnaire to identify their top 5 strengths and applying them to a boring task). The final phase involved contributing to something larger than themselves — such as mentoring younger students and writing their personal vision for humanity's future. Seligman concluded that positive psychology can be actively practised and that doing so increases wellbeing, optimism, and physical health.

## Study 3: Shoshani and Steinmetz (2014) — Can Positive Psychology Protect Teenagers' Mental Health?

### Shoshani & Steinmetz (2014) — A school-based positive psychology programme reduced depression and anxiety in Israeli adolescents over two years

**1. Why this study exists**

The transition into middle school (ages 11–15) is one of the most psychologically turbulent periods of life. Academic pressure rises, friendships shift, self-identity develops — and **mental health problems often first appear** at this age.

Positive psychology interventions (like gratitude diaries) had been tested before, but most only looked at **short-term effects**. Shoshani and Steinmetz wanted to know: does it last? And does it work for *all* students, including the most vulnerable?

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Two large middle schools in central Israel. Over **1,000 students**, aged 11 to 15.

**The setup:**

- **School 1 (537 students):** The **intervention group** — they received the positive psychology programme
- **School 2 (501 students):** The **wait-list control group** — they continued normal lessons; they would receive the programme later

> 💡 A **wait-list control group** is smarter than a plain control group. Instead of just denying the programme to some students forever (which raises ethical issues), the control school is told: "You're next in line." This keeps things ethical AND gives the researchers a comparison.

**Measuring everything — four times:**

Students completed a battery of self-report questionnaires **before the intervention, after the intervention, and twice during the follow-up year** — giving a two-year picture of change.

| Questionnaire | What it measured |
|---|---|
| Brief Symptoms Inventory (BSI) | Depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety |
| Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale | Self-worth |
| General Self-Efficacy Scale | Confidence in coping with life |
| Satisfaction with Life Scale | Overall life satisfaction (1–7 Likert scale) |
| Life Orientation Test-Revised | Optimism vs pessimism |
| Socio-demographic questionnaire | Age, gender, SES, country of birth |

**Training the teachers first:**

The researchers could not run the programme themselves in every classroom — so they trained the **schoolteachers** instead.

15 training sessions, each two hours long, every two weeks across the school year — led by **clinical psychologists**. Teachers received a full textbook with lesson plans. School psychologists randomly spot-checked classrooms to make sure sessions were delivered consistently.

**What the students actually did in class:**

The programme used activities, discussions, poems, stories, and video clips. The central component was **gratitude**:
- Every week: students discussed **five or more things they were grateful for**
- Over the year: students **wrote gratitude letters** to people who had positively impacted their lives

The control school continued their normal social science lessons on adolescence-related topics.

---

**3. What they found**

| Outcome | Intervention Group | Wait-list Control Group |
|---|---|---|
| General psychological distress | Decreased significantly (mean change = −0.22) | **Increased** |
| Depression symptoms | Decreased significantly (mean change = −0.10) | **Increased** |
| Optimism | Increased significantly | No change / decreased |
| Anxiety | Decreased | Increased |
| Interpersonal sensitivity | Decreased | Increased |

> Key finding: It was not just that the intervention group improved — the **control group got worse**. This makes the difference between groups even more striking.

**Pre-existing risk factors** (poverty, single-parent households) were associated with worse baseline mental health — but the intervention still worked for these high-risk students too.

**Gender differences:** Males started with lower self-efficacy and depressive symptoms than females, but higher initial anxiety.

---

**4. Why it matters**

This study provides **real-world evidence** that positive psychology is not just philosophical talk — it can be delivered through ordinary teachers in ordinary schools and produce **measurable improvements** in adolescent mental health.

Given the pressure on child mental health services, a proactive school programme that **prevents** decline (rather than treating illness after it develops) has enormous public health value.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

| Strength | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Longitudinal design** | Students followed for 2 years (not just the intervention year) | Can assess long-term effects — much stronger evidence than a one-shot study |
| **Wait-list control group** | Provides a comparison condition without permanently denying the programme | Increases validity of causal conclusions AND is more ethical |
| **Large sample** | 1,038 participants from two schools | Greater statistical power and more reliable findings |
| **Teacher delivery** | Programme delivered by existing teachers (not researchers) | More ecologically valid — shows the programme can work in real conditions, not just with specialist researchers |
| **Mixed design** | Both independent measures AND repeated measures | Can detect change over time AND compare groups |

**Weaknesses:**

| Weakness | Why | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand characteristics** | Students knew they were in an intervention; they completed the same questionnaires 4 times | May have given answers they thought teachers/researchers wanted → inflates apparent effectiveness |
| **Limited generalisability** | Only two Israeli schools, ages 11–15 | Cannot assume the programme works the same way in different countries, age groups, or cultural settings |
| **Self-report measures only** | All outcomes measured via questionnaires | No objective behavioural evidence (e.g., attendance, academic performance, clinical assessment) |
| **Schools not individuals randomised** | One whole school = intervention, one whole school = control | Can't rule out pre-existing differences between the two schools (different teachers, culture, leadership) |
| **Ethics: withholding programme** | The control group was denied the programme for a full year | Some students in the control school left for high school before they ever received the intervention — they never benefited |

---

**6. Exam tips**

**Must-knows:**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sample** | 1,038 Israeli adolescents, ages 11.8–14.7, from two middle schools |
| **Design** | Longitudinal field experiment, mixed design (independent + repeated measures) |
| **Key finding (positive)** | Intervention group showed significant decreases in distress and depression; control group symptoms increased |
| **Key limitation** | Demand characteristics from completing the same questionnaires 4 times |
| **Key ethical issue** | Wait-list controls may have missed their chance to benefit before moving to high school |
| **Why "wait-list" matters** | More ethical than plain control — control school was promised the programme later |

**Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**

> Shoshani and Steinmetz (2014) investigated whether a school-based positive psychology intervention could improve the mental health of adolescents. 537 students from one Israeli middle school received the intervention (involving gratitude activities, strengths identification, and positive psychology exercises delivered by trained teachers) while 501 students from a second school acted as a wait-list control. Over a two-year longitudinal design, students completed self-report questionnaires including the Brief Symptoms Inventory and Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale at four time points. Results showed that the intervention group had significantly lower levels of depression, anxiety, and general distress by the end of the study, while the control group's symptoms increased. The researchers concluded that positive psychology interventions can be effectively embedded in school curricula to protect adolescent mental health.

## Quick Comparison Table — All Three Studies

| Feature | Weinstein (1980) | Seligman (2004) | Shoshani & Steinmetz (2014) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Type** | Survey / correlational | Course description (not an experiment) | Longitudinal field experiment |
| **Sample** | 258 + 120 US college students | University of Pennsylvania undergraduates | 1,038 Israeli adolescents (11–15) |
| **Key concept** | Unrealistic optimism | Pleasant / good / meaningful life | Positive psychology intervention |
| **Method** | Comparative ratings + event characteristic ratings | Homework tasks (gratitude, savouring, VIA) | Self-report questionnaires x4, mixed design |
| **Key finding** | People systematically underestimate their risk of negative events | Positive psychology can be actively taught and practised | PP intervention reduced depression/anxiety; control group got worse |
| **Key weakness** | US student sample only — culture and age limits | No control group — cannot establish causation | Demand characteristics; only 2 Israeli schools |
| **Real-world relevance** | Explains why people ignore health warnings | Provides tools for increasing wellbeing | Shows PP can be scaled in schools |

## Core Concept Connections

> 🧠 **Unrealistic optimism (Weinstein)** explains WHY people don't follow health advice.
> 🧠 **Positive psychology (Seligman)** offers a framework for how to make people healthier and happier.
> 🧠 **School intervention (Shoshani & Steinmetz)** provides empirical evidence that Seligman's ideas actually work in the real world.

These three fit together as a narrative:
1. We have a cognitive bias that makes us underestimate health risks (Weinstein)
2. A new approach — positive psychology — suggests building strengths, not just treating deficits (Seligman)
3. When we apply that approach in schools, we get measurable mental health improvements (Shoshani & Steinmetz)
