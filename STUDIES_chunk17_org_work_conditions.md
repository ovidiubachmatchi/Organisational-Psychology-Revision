## Study 1 — Mayo et al. (1920s–1930s) — The Hawthorne Studies

### [Mayo et al. (1920s)] — Being watched at work makes people work harder

---

**1. Why this study exists**

In the 1920s, managers at the Hawthorne plant of the **Western Electric Company** in Chicago wanted a practical answer to a simple question: *does better lighting make workers more productive?*

Nobody had done a rigorous experiment on this before.
The answer they got was not the one they expected — and it changed psychology forever.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you are a factory worker in 1920s Chicago, assembling telephone relays all day.*

**Phase 1 — The Illumination Experiment**

Researchers split workers into two groups:
- **Experimental group:** lighting was **systematically lowered** over time.
- **Control group:** lighting stayed the same.

Both groups got more productive — even as the lights dimmed.
The only time productivity fell was when the lights were so low workers could "barely see."

> "Wait — we made it darker, and they worked *harder*?" — the researchers, baffled.

**Phase 2 — The Relay Test Room Experiment**

Now researchers took **five women** and changed one thing at a time:
- More rest breaks → productivity went up.
- Fewer rest breaks → productivity went up.
- Longer working day → productivity went up.
- Shorter working day → productivity went up.

It didn't matter *what* they changed.
Whatever the researchers touched, the women worked harder and more efficiently.

**The conclusion:** It wasn't the lighting or the breaks that mattered.
It was the fact that the workers *knew they were being watched and paid attention to.*
This became known as the **Hawthorne Effect**.

---

**3. What they found**

| Condition | Productivity |
|---|---|
| Lights decreased (experimental) | Went UP |
| Lights stayed same (control) | Also went UP |
| Any change to working conditions | Workers consistently worked harder |

- **Hawthorne Effect** = performance improves when people know they are being observed.
- Being noticed by researchers (an authority figure) acted like a social motivator.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Set the foundation for all future **field experiments** in organisational psychology.
- Showed that **social factors** (being watched, paid attention to) can matter more than physical conditions.
- Changed how researchers think about **observer effects** — any study where participants know they are being watched is at risk of the Hawthorne Effect distorting results.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Mixed methods (quantitative + qualitative)**
State → Used observation, interviews, blood pressure and heart rate data.
Why → Multiple data types cross-check each other.
Consequence → Results are more **valid** than a single-method study.

✅ **Strength: High ecological validity**
State → Conducted in a real factory, real workers, real shifts.
Why → Nothing was artificially staged.
Consequence → Results **apply to real workplaces** more readily than lab studies.

✅ **Strength: Pioneering collaboration**
State → First major university–industry research partnership.
Why → Opened the door to decades of applied organisational research.
Consequence → Methodology still used today.

❌ **Weakness: Very low generalisability**
State → One electrical plant, one city (Chicago), one culture (USA).
Why → Results may only apply to Western, individualist, factory settings.
Consequence → Cannot safely claim the effect exists universally.

❌ **Weakness: Deterministic**
State → Implies workers *had no choice* but to work harder when watched.
Why → Kompier (2006) showed that some workers complained, dissented, and productivity sometimes *fell*.
Consequence → The Hawthorne Effect does **not** explain everyone's behaviour.

❌ **Weakness: Poor scientific quality**
State → Many uncontrolled variables; no random assignment; tiny samples.
Why → Cannot isolate what actually caused productivity changes.
Consequence → Causal claims are not valid — correlation only.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- The two phases: illumination experiment + relay test room.
- Definition: **Hawthorne Effect** = performance improves when observed.
- Key evaluation: ecological validity ✅ vs. generalisability ❌ vs. determinism ❌.
- Kompier (2006) is the direct challenge to this study — link them.

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
1. Mayo conducted field experiments at the Hawthorne plant in Chicago (1920s).
2. In the illumination study, lighting was decreased for the experimental group and kept constant for the control.
3. Both groups showed increased productivity — even as lights dimmed.
4. In the relay test room study, five women had working conditions changed repeatedly.
5. Whatever was changed, productivity rose, suggesting workers worked harder because they were being observed.
6. This led to the concept of the **Hawthorne Effect** — behaviour changes when people know they are being watched.

## Study 2 — Kompier (2006) — "The Hawthorne Effect Is a Myth"

### [Kompier (2006)] — Critiquing the Hawthorne legend

---

**1. Why this study exists**

By 2006, the Hawthorne Effect had become textbook gospel — taught in every psychology course.
**Michiel Kompier**, a Dutch work psychologist, looked at the original data again and said: *"Wait. This doesn't hold up."*

He wrote a paper arguing the Hawthorne Effect is not a valid scientific finding — it is a **myth** that became a legend because it was a good story.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you're a detective re-reading old case files that everyone else has accepted as solved.*

Kompier did not run a new study.
He did a **critical re-analysis** of the original Hawthorne studies and identified **five myths** that the original researchers created or ignored:

| Myth # | What the original researchers claimed | What Kompier found |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The studies were scientifically rigorous | Poor methodology, no controls, tiny samples — cannot establish causation |
| 2 | Productivity always improved | Productivity **fell** in many periods — these were downplayed or ignored |
| 3 | Social attention mattered more than pay | When incentive pay was introduced, productivity jumped; when removed, it dropped — pay mattered hugely |
| 4 | Everyone cooperated happily | Workers showed resistance, annoyance, disciplinary incidents; one study ended early due to **worker conflict** |
| 5 | Conflict was due to neurotic/unstable workers | Management blamed workers' mental health or family problems — dismissed legitimate workplace dissatisfaction |

---

**3. What they found**

Kompier concluded: the Hawthorne Effect is not supported by the original data.
Social factors *may* play a role, but they are **not the primary driver** of productivity.
Pay, supervision quality, physical conditions, and individual differences all matter too.

Why does the myth persist? Kompier suggests:
- It tells a compelling story that fits social psychology theories (social influence, observer effects).
- Social scientists are "too far removed from the workplace."
- It validates the idea that workers have feelings — which felt revolutionary at the time.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Forces researchers to think critically before citing "classic" studies.
- Highlights the danger of **confirmation bias** in interpreting data.
- Supports the view that productivity has **multiple causes** — not just observation.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Evidence-based critique**
State → Kompier examined the actual data and records from the original studies.
Why → Not just theoretical argument.
Consequence → More credible than opinion-based dismissals.

✅ **Strength: Identifies methodological flaws clearly**
State → Lists specific, named problems (no controls, ignored negative results, etc.).
Why → Gives future researchers a checklist of what to avoid.
Consequence → Increases scientific rigour in the field.

❌ **Weakness: This is a review, not a new experiment**
State → Kompier did not collect new data.
Why → Cannot test whether better-designed research would show a Hawthorne Effect.
Consequence → Does not definitively disprove the effect — only questions the original evidence.

❌ **Weakness: Retrospective analysis is limited**
State → The original data from the 1920s may be incomplete or poorly recorded.
Why → Kompier is working with whatever survived from 80+ years ago.
Consequence → His conclusions are only as good as the data available.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Kompier's **5 myths** (scientific worth, continuous improvement, social > pay, wholehearted cooperation, neurotic worker).
- Kompier says pay is a key motivator — contradicts the original conclusion directly.
- Use this to evaluate Mayo: "However, Kompier (2006) argued that the Hawthorne Effect is a myth because..."

📋 **Skeleton answer (evaluation use):**
> "One weakness of the Hawthorne studies is the low scientific rigour. Kompier (2006) identified five myths in the original research, including that productivity did not always improve — periods of decline were ignored. This means the studies lack validity and causal conclusions cannot be drawn."

## Study 3 — Oldham & Brass (1979) — Open Plan Offices

### [Oldham & Brass (1979)] — Moving to open plan offices made workers more miserable, not more productive

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Open plan offices were being promoted as the future of work in the 1970s — more communication, more collaboration, more creativity.

**Oldham and Brass** asked the obvious question that nobody had tested properly:
*What actually happens to workers' motivation and satisfaction when you rip out the walls?*

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you have your own cosy office with your own filing cabinet, your own door. Then one day the company moves everyone into one giant open room. No walls. No privacy. Just... everyone.*

**The setting:** 76 employees at an American newspaper company in the Midwest USA.

**The design:** A longitudinal quasi-experiment with three groups:

| Group | Who | Office type before | Office type after | When surveyed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Experimental (n=76) | Non-supervisory staff (e.g. copy editors, receptionists) | Conventional (private) | New open plan | 8 weeks before, 9 weeks after, 18 weeks after |
| Non-equivalent control (n=5) | Press room staff | Conventional | New conventional (private cubicles) | 8 weeks before, 9 weeks after, 18 weeks after |
| Quasi-control (n=26) | Randomly selected | Conventional | New open plan | Only 9 weeks and 18 weeks after (no baseline) |

**The measures:** A questionnaire with **7-point rating scales** measuring:
- **Work satisfaction** — how happy and satisfied workers feel.
- **Interpersonal satisfaction** — satisfaction with colleagues and supervisors.
- **Internal work motivation** — feeling positive about doing the job well.

Informal interviews were also conducted with managers and employees to gather qualitative data.

---

**3. What they found**

**Quantitative:**
- Experimental group showed a **sharp decline** in work satisfaction, interpersonal satisfaction, and internal motivation after the move.
- The quasi-control group (who had no pre-move baseline) showed the same decline — ruling out questionnaire order effects.
- The non-equivalent control group (who stayed in conventional offices) showed **no change** — confirming the open plan office was the cause.

**Qualitative:**
Workers described the new office as a:
- "Fishbowl"
- "Cage"
- "Warehouse"
- "Grand Central Station"

They reported inability to concentrate, constant noise, and complete loss of privacy.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- One of the earliest empirical studies to show that open plan offices have **negative** psychological consequences.
- Still relevant today: James et al. (2021), reviewing 31 papers, found the same negative outcomes on health, satisfaction, and stress.
- Provides evidence that **physical work environment design** directly affects worker wellbeing.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Longitudinal design**
State → Three data collection points (8 weeks before, 9 weeks after, 18 weeks after).
Why → Tracks changes over time, not just a snapshot.
Consequence → Shows that satisfaction declined *and stayed low* — not just a short-term reaction.

✅ **Strength: Standardised procedure**
State → Same questionnaire used across all groups at each time point.
Why → Allows direct comparisons across groups and time.
Consequence → Increases **reliability** — can be replicated.

✅ **Strength: Mixed methods (quant + qual)**
State → Questionnaire scales + informal interviews.
Why → Qualitative data supports and explains the quantitative findings.
Consequence → Increases **validity** — we know both *what* happened and *why* workers felt that way.

✅ **Strength: Control groups used**
State → Both a non-equivalent control and a quasi-control group were included.
Why → Rules out alternative explanations (e.g., order effects, novelty effects).
Consequence → Strengthens causal claim that the open plan office caused the decline.

❌ **Weakness: Very low generalisability**
State → Only one company, one city (Midwest USA), one industry (newspaper).
Why → That company or culture may have had specific characteristics making them especially resistant to open plan.
Consequence → Cannot confidently apply findings to all workplaces globally.

❌ **Weakness: Low temporal validity**
State → Conducted in 1979, when open plan offices were new and rare.
Why → Workers in 2024 have grown up with open plan — attitudes and adaptations have changed.
Consequence → Results may not reflect modern workers' experiences accurately.
> Note: However, James et al. (2021) found similar negative outcomes — suggesting the findings *may* still hold.

❌ **Weakness: Social desirability bias**
State → Questionnaires are self-report measures.
Why → Workers may have exaggerated negative feelings about the new office (especially if they were resistant to change) or downplayed them (not wanting to seem difficult).
Consequence → Lowers validity of the questionnaire data.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Three groups: experimental (open plan), non-equivalent control (stayed conventional), quasi-control (open plan, no baseline).
- Three outcome measures: work satisfaction, interpersonal satisfaction, internal work motivation — all declined.
- Qualitative quotes: "fishbowl," "cage," "Grand Central Station."
- Temporal validity weakness — but counteract with James et al. (2021).

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
1. 76 employees at an American newspaper moved from conventional to open plan offices.
2. Questionnaires measured work satisfaction, interpersonal satisfaction, and internal motivation.
3. These were completed 8 weeks before the move, and 9 and 18 weeks after.
4. Two control groups were used — one that stayed in conventional offices, one in the open plan without a pre-move baseline.
5. Results showed sharp declines in all three measures for the experimental group.
6. Interviews revealed workers called it a "fishbowl" and "cage" — loss of privacy and noise were key issues.

## Study 4 — Gold et al. (1992) — Shift Work, Sleep & Accidents in Nurses

### [Gold et al. (1992)] — Rotating shift nurses lost the most sleep and had the most accidents

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Hospitals never close. Nurses must cover nights, days, and everything in between.
But what actually happens to a nurse's sleep — and safety — depending on *which* shift pattern they work?

**Gold et al.** wanted real data, not assumptions, on whether rotating shifts were the most dangerous.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you are a nurse at Massachusetts General Hospital. Some weeks you work 7am–3pm. Some weeks 3pm–11pm. Some weeks 11pm–7am. You never quite know when your next sleep will be — or for how long.*

**The setting:** A hospital in Massachusetts, USA.

**The sample:** 878 nurses and auxiliary hospital staff (mean age 33.9).

**The method:** A **questionnaire** was sent out.

Nurses recorded:
- Which shift category they worked (for the current week, next week, and the previous two weeks).
- Sleep-wake times.
- Sleep quality.
- Alcohol and medication use.
- Whether they had **fallen asleep at work**.
- Whether they had **fallen asleep while driving** to or from work.
- **Accidents, errors, and near-miss accidents** in the past year.

The five shift categories were:

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| Rotator | 4 days/evenings, then 4 nights |
| Day/evening only | No nights |
| Night only | 8+ nights/month, no days or evenings |
| Day/evening with occasional nights | |
| Night with occasional days/evenings | |

Researchers also measured **anchor sleep** — defined as at least 4 hours of sleep during both work days and days off.

---

**3. What they found**

| Outcome | Rotators | Night nurses | Day/evening nurses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fell asleep at work (≥1/week) | **35%** | 32.4% | 20.7% |
| Odds of falling asleep while driving | **3.9x higher** than day/evening | 3.6x higher | Baseline |
| Odds of any accident or error | **2x higher** than day/evening | — | Baseline |
| Odds of near-miss accidents | **2.5x higher** than day/evening | — | Baseline |

> **Key finding:** Rotators were the most at risk — more so than permanent night nurses, who at least had a stable sleep pattern.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides evidence that **rotating shift work** is the most harmful shift type for sleep and safety.
- Has direct application: hospitals can redesign shift patterns (e.g., forward rotation, slow rotation) to reduce accidents.
- Shows that **sleep deprivation** is not just a personal problem — it is a workplace safety issue.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Large sample (n=878)**
State → Over 800 nurses participated.
Why → Larger samples reduce the effect of outliers and increase statistical power.
Consequence → Results are more **generalisable** than a small-sample study.

✅ **Strength: Quantitative data**
State → Numbers of accidents, near-misses, sleep hours, and response rates were recorded.
Why → Objective data is less subject to experimenter bias.
Consequence → Statistical comparisons across shift types are valid and **reliable**.

✅ **Strength: Real-world application**
State → Results directly inform hospital staffing decisions.
Why → If rotating shifts cause the most harm, policies can be changed.
Consequence → Study has high **practical utility**.

❌ **Weakness: Self-report questionnaire — social desirability bias**
State → Nurses self-reported accidents, near-misses, and falling asleep at work.
Why → Admitting to falling asleep at work or having accidents could have professional consequences.
Consequence → Underreporting is likely — actual accident rates may be *higher* than recorded, reducing **validity**.

❌ **Weakness: Low generalisability**
State → Only nurses from one hospital in Massachusetts, USA.
Why → Nursing culture, shift patterns, and hospital protocols differ across countries.
Consequence → Results may not apply to nurses in the UK, other healthcare roles, or other industries.

❌ **Weakness: Correlational — cannot prove causation**
State → The study shows that rotating shifts are *associated* with more accidents.
Why → Confounding variables (e.g., personal stress, diet, commute time) are not controlled.
Consequence → Cannot definitively claim that rotating shifts *cause* accidents.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Rotators had 3.9x the odds of falling asleep while driving (vs. day/evening nurses).
- Rotators had 2x the odds of accidents and 2.5x the odds of near-miss accidents.
- Key weakness: social desirability — nurses may not report accidents honestly.
- Link to: circadian rhythms, sleep deprivation, Challenger disaster (1986) as real-world consequence.

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
1. Gold et al. surveyed 878 nurses and hospital staff in Massachusetts using questionnaires.
2. Nurses recorded shift category, sleep-wake times, sleep quality, accidents, near-misses, and whether they fell asleep at work or while driving.
3. Five shift types were compared, including rotators, day/evening only, and night only nurses.
4. Rotators reported the fewest hours of sleep, the most accidents, and the most near-misses.
5. 35% of rotators fell asleep at work at least once a week, and 3.9x the odds of falling asleep while driving.
6. Concluded that rotating shifts disrupt circadian rhythms and cause dangerous levels of sleep deprivation.

## Study 5 — Fox et al. (1987) — Token Economy to Reduce Mining Accidents

### [Fox et al. (1987)] — Rewarding safe behaviour with trading stamps cut accidents in mines — for over a decade

---

**1. Why this study exists**

In 1985, US mines recorded **500 accidents** and **40,000 work-related injuries** per year.
Previous safety interventions were short-term — nobody knew if behavioural programmes could work *long-term*.

**Fox et al.** wanted to test whether operant conditioning principles — specifically a **token economy** — could sustainably reduce accidents.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you are a miner in Wyoming in the mid-1980s. Every month, if you had zero lost-time injuries, your pay packet arrives with a booklet of trading stamps — like supermarket loyalty stamps. Collect enough and trade them in at local stores for goods.*

**The setting:** Two open-pit mines — one in Wyoming, one in Arizona.

**The participants:** All employees at both mines (similar mining procedures, similar existing health and safety policies).

**Baseline:** Workers were divided into **four hazard groups**:

| Group | Job type | Hazard level |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Office-based (75%+ time in office) | Lowest |
| 2 | Mixed | Low-moderate |
| 3 | Mixed | Moderate-high |
| 4 | Electricians, scrapers, operators | Highest |

**The token economy system:**
- End of each month, workers with **zero lost-time injuries or compensation injuries** received **trading stamps** with their wages.
- More hazardous jobs = more stamps (acknowledging the greater risk).
- **Extra stamps** were awarded for safety suggestions that prevented accidents, injuries, or property damage.
- Accidents led to **loss of stamps**: no stamps for that month (1–2 days off), or for two months (3–4 days off).
- **Failure to report** an accident = loss of tokens.
- Trading stamps could be exchanged at local stores for hundreds of different items.

The programme ran for **over a decade** at both mines.

---

**3. What they found**

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Days lost from work | Substantial decrease in the first years |
| Lost-time injuries | Significant downward trend by Year 2 (not Year 1) |
| Costs of injuries/accidents | Fell significantly |
| Cost-benefit ratio | Money saved > cost of trading stamps |

> The programme was economically self-sustaining — the savings from reduced accidents outweighed the cost of the stamps.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Proves that **operant conditioning** (reward for safe behaviour) can work in a real industrial setting.
- Long-term effectiveness (12+ years) is rare in organisational interventions — most studies only follow up for weeks or months.
- Demonstrates that token economies are **not just for classrooms** — they have industrial safety applications.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Longitudinal — ran for over a decade**
State → One mine ran the programme for 12 years.
Why → Allows researchers to track long-term effectiveness, not just initial novelty effects.
Consequence → Results show sustained, genuine behavioural change — not just a short-term response.

✅ **Strength: Real-world application**
State → Reduced accidents and costs in an actual mining workplace.
Why → This was not a simulated or lab environment.
Consequence → High **ecological validity** and immediate practical usefulness.

✅ **Strength: Cost-benefit evidence**
State → Savings from fewer accidents outweighed the cost of stamps.
Why → Makes the intervention financially viable and attractive to organisations.
Consequence → More likely to be adopted by other industries.

❌ **Weakness: Low generalisability**
State → Both mines were in the USA, both were mining operations.
Why → Mining culture may make workers uniquely responsive to risk-based reward (they already work in physically dangerous environments).
Consequence → Results may not generalise to office workers, healthcare, or other industries.

❌ **Weakness: Validity threatened — under-reporting of accidents**
State → Receiving a reward for having no accidents incentivises hiding accidents.
Why → A worker who has a minor injury might not report it to keep their stamps.
Consequence → The recorded decline in accidents may partly reflect **lower reporting**, not fewer actual accidents.

> However: serious accidents are hard to conceal — so this concern mainly affects minor incidents.

❌ **Weakness: Pre-existing trend**
State → At one mine, accidents were already declining before the programme started.
Why → There may have been other changes (management, equipment, training) occurring simultaneously.
Consequence → The token economy may not have been the sole — or even primary — cause of improvement.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Token economy = operant conditioning. Stamps = secondary reinforcers. Goods from stores = primary reinforcers.
- 12-year programme = key strength (longitudinal).
- Key weakness: incentive to *not report* accidents (validity).
- Link to: Fagen et al. (2014) used secondary reinforcement with elephants — same learning principles.

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
1. Fox et al. investigated whether a token economy could reduce accidents at two open-pit mines in the USA.
2. Workers were divided into hazard groups — more dangerous jobs earned more stamps.
3. At the end of each month, injury-free workers received trading stamps with their wages.
4. Workers who had accidents lost stamps for that month (or longer, depending on severity).
5. Stamps could be exchanged at local stores for goods.
6. Results showed significant decreases in lost time, injury costs, and accident rates — and the programme ran for over a decade.

## Study 6 — Swat (1997) — Monitoring Accidents at Work

### [Swat (1997)] — 95% of workplace incidents are never reported — and Poland's monitoring system was missing almost all of them

---

**1. Why this study exists**

**Krzysztof Swat**, a Polish researcher at the Nofer Institute of Occupational Medicine, had a core problem to solve:

> *If accidents are not being recorded, how can you prevent them?*

Poland's system only counted accidents that resulted in **sick leave or death**.
Minor injuries, near-misses, small incidents — invisible. Not recorded. Not analysed. Not prevented.

Swat set out to develop a better system.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you manage a meat factory in Lodz, Poland. You believe your workplace is fairly safe — your accident records for the year show 23 incidents. But what if the real number was closer to 520?*

**Design:** A **longitudinal case study** running from **1994 to 1997**.

**Sample:** Four industrial plants in Lodz, Poland:
- Foundry
- Machinery plant
- Meat processing plant
- Furniture factory

Total workforce across all plants: **2,964 workers** (as of 1993).
All plants were described as "rather old" with older equipment.

**Three sources of data:**

| Source | What it covered |
|---|---|
| Collective accident reports from safety supervisors | Officially recorded accidents 1993 |
| Researchers' own investigation of accident protocols | Deeper analysis of 83 accidents |
| Interviews with line managers, safety supervisors, and employees | Qualitative understanding of causes and culture |

**Definitions used:**
- **Accident** = "a sudden undesired event connected with the workplace resulting in health impairment" — in Polish law, this meant *only* injuries causing sick leave or death.
- **Accident frequency** = number of injury accidents per 100 employees per year.
- **Accident severity** = number of sick days per accident.

**Phase 2 — Minor Incidents at the Meat Plant (1994):**
- All 96 meat plant employees were interviewed and told to report even **tiny injuries**.
- First-aid cases were recorded.
- An incident was defined as "any sudden event resulting in personal injury" — much broader than the legal definition.

---

**3. What they found**

**Accident frequency (per 100 employees):**

| Plant | Frequency rate |
|---|---|
| Foundry | 5.9 (highest) |
| Meat processing | 2.8 |
| Furniture | 2.3 |
| Machinery | 2.1 |
| **Average** | **2.8** |

**Accident severity (sick days per accident):**

| Plant | Severity rate |
|---|---|
| Machinery plant | 61.2 (highest) |
| Meat processing | 41.4 |
| Furniture | 41.2 |
| Foundry | 38.8 |
| **Average** | **44.6** |

**Five accident types identified:**
1. Falls and slips
2. Accidents connected with manual work
3. Accidents connected with working parts of machinery
4. Accidents connected with sources of energy
5. Others

**Four causes of accidents:**
1. Insufficient supervision (present in **89%** of accidents)
2. Poor workplace organisation
3. Technical factors (only **11%**)
4. Individual error

**Housekeeping as a cause:**
- Foundry: 65% of accidents linked to poor housekeeping
- Meat processing: 47.8%
- Furniture: 37.5%
- Machinery: 33.3%

**The headline finding — incidents in the meat plant:**
- Officially reported: **23** incidents with sick leave.
- Injuries requiring any medical treatment: **254**.
- Employee interviews suggested real total: up to **520 incidents**.
- **95% of incidents were never reported.**

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Proved that official accident records are a **massive undercount**.
- Created a practical framework for monitoring that includes minor incidents, near-misses, and housekeeping factors — not just serious reportable accidents.
- Applicable beyond Poland: any country that only counts "serious" accidents is flying blind on workplace safety.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Multiple data sources (triangulation)**
State → Used official reports, researcher-led accident protocol analysis, and interviews.
Why → Different sources cross-check each other.
Consequence → Discovering that official data missed 95% of incidents was only possible through triangulation — increasing **validity**.

✅ **Strength: Longitudinal design (3 years)**
State → Data collected from 1994 to 1997; the meat plant was tracked across two years.
Why → Trends over time are visible; not just a single snapshot.
Consequence → Allows researchers to track whether accident rates are improving or worsening — more comprehensive.

✅ **Strength: Covers multiple industries**
State → Foundry, machinery, meat processing, furniture — all different industries.
Why → Allows comparison across sectors.
Consequence → Findings about housekeeping, supervision, and monitoring apply across different workplace types.

❌ **Weakness: Subjectivity and bias in data**
State → Official accident records are compiled by line managers who may not record everything.
Why → Managers may suppress minor incident reports to avoid scrutiny from above.
Consequence → The data Swat *analysed* is itself biased — the researcher is building on incomplete information, lowering **validity**.

❌ **Weakness: Low generalisability (case study)**
State → Four plants, all in Lodz, Poland, all described as "old" with outdated equipment.
Why → Older factories with older equipment likely have higher accident rates than modern ones.
Consequence → Findings may not reflect accident rates or monitoring gaps in modern, better-equipped workplaces — **cannot generalise** freely.

❌ **Weakness: Researcher bias in analysis**
State → Swat personally analysed accident protocols and categorised causes.
Why → There are no inter-rater reliability checks mentioned.
Consequence → The categorisation of causes (e.g., "poor housekeeping") may reflect Swat's interpretations rather than objective reality.

**Ethics:**
- No specific businesses were named → **confidentiality maintained**.
- Workers were not harmed by participation.
- Results were beneficial — improved monitoring = better accident prevention.
- Minor concern: the meat plant may be identifiable (only one such plant in the area) — some risk of **identification**.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- 95% of incidents in the meat plant were never reported — the headline number.
- Two types of monitoring: **active** (checking for hazards before) vs. **reactive** (analysing accidents after).
- Insufficient supervision = 89% of accidents.
- Housekeeping (maintenance/tidiness) = 33–65% of accidents depending on plant.
- Four causes: supervision, organisation, technical, individual error.
- Key weakness: generalisability — four old Polish factories ≠ all workplaces.

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
1. Swat conducted a longitudinal case study (1994–1997) in four industrial plants in Poland.
2. Secondary data on 83 accidents from 1993 was analysed alongside interviews with managers and employees.
3. Accidents were defined as incidents resulting in sick leave or death — a narrow definition.
4. In the meat processing plant, only 23 incidents had been officially reported, but interviews revealed up to 520 had actually occurred — meaning 95% were unreported.
5. The main cause of accidents was insufficient supervision (89%), with poor housekeeping also common.
6. Swat concluded that monitoring must include minor incidents and first-aid cases, not just serious accidents.

## Study 7 — Claypoole & Szalma (2019) — Electronic Performance Monitoring & Sustained Attention

### [Claypoole & Szalma (2019)] — Being watched by a webcam made people better at boring, attention-demanding tasks

---

**1. Why this study exists**

By 2019, over **70% of US organisations** were using electronic systems to monitor worker productivity.
But the research on whether this actually *helps* performance was contradictory:

- Some studies: EPM improves performance.
- Other studies: EPM *impairs* performance — especially on **difficult tasks** (because of evaluation anxiety).

Crucially, all previous research used simple, quick tasks.
**Nobody had tested EPM on tasks requiring sustained attention (vigilance)** — the kind of boring, prolonged focus needed for driving, medical screening, or security monitoring.

**Claypoole and Szalma** filled that gap.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

💡 *Imagine you sit down at a computer. A webcam is perched on top of the screen. You are told: "This sends live video to the room next door where your performance is being evaluated right now." A video recorder hums behind the screen. "This records everything for later review."*

*Nothing is actually being recorded. But you don't know that.*

**Design:** A laboratory experiment, **independent measures** (two conditions).

**Sample:**
- 106 undergraduate students from a university in the southern USA.
- Mean age: 20.57 (range 18–37).
- 65 female, 41 male.
- Recruited via a psychology experiment website.
- Received course credits for participation.
- Randomly assigned to conditions.

**The two conditions:**

| Condition | Setup |
|---|---|
| Control (n=53) | No webcam, no recorder. Sat alone in a room. |
| Electronic presence (n=53) | Webcam on top of screen (told it streams live to next room). Video recorder behind screen (told it records for later review). Both had audio and visual cues to maintain sense of being watched. |

**Procedure steps:**
1. Participants surrendered phones and watches on arrival.
2. Completed a demographics questionnaire.
3. Research assistant reviewed task instructions and left.
4. 3-minute practice session.
5. 24-minute **sustained attention (vigilance) task**.

**The vigilance task:**
- Watched a screen displaying **two-digit numbers** appearing every 1000 milliseconds.
- Had to press the **spacebar** when a "critical signal" appeared.
- Critical signal = two numbers differing by 0 or ±1 (e.g., 77, 65, 01).
- 20 critical signals total, spread across four 6-minute blocks.
- Timing was randomised but consistent across participants.

**Measures recorded:**
- **Correct detections** (hits)
- **False alarms** (pressing spacebar when not a critical signal)
- **Response time** (milliseconds)

---

**3. What they found**

| Measure | Electronic Presence (M) | Control (M) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Correct detections | **0.6906** | 0.6038 | EP significantly better ✅ |
| False alarms | **0.0059** | 0.0201 | EP significantly fewer ✅ |
| Response time (ms) | **767.34** | 802.02 | EP significantly faster ✅ |

> Across all three measures, the electronically monitored group performed **better**.

Additional finding: In *both* groups, correct detections decreased over the 24-minute session — consistent with the known **vigilance decrement** (attention fades over time during boring tasks).

But the electronic presence group's performance was still **significantly better** throughout.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Shows that **social facilitation effects** — usually studied with a live observer — can be replicated with an *electronic* observer (webcam).
- Practical implication: webcams and video recorders are cheap, non-intrusive tools that may improve performance on vigilance-dependent tasks (security, medical screening, driving monitoring).
- Counters the idea that EPM always causes stress and impairs performance — for vigilance tasks, it actually *helps*.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength: Laboratory experiment — standardised procedure**
State → Same task, same timing, same instructions, same room setup for all participants.
Why → Eliminates confounding variables; only the electronic presence differs between conditions.
Consequence → High **internal validity** — can be confident the electronic presence caused the performance difference.

✅ **Strength: Random allocation to conditions**
State → Participants were randomly assigned to electronic presence or control.
Why → Controls for pre-existing individual differences (e.g., some people naturally more vigilant).
Consequence → Reduces participant variables as a confound — increases **reliability**.

✅ **Strength: Quantitative data**
State → Correct detections, false alarms, and response times were all objectively measured.
Why → No subjectivity — not based on self-report.
Consequence → Data is **reliable** and can be statistically compared and replicated.

❌ **Weakness: Low ecological validity — artificial task**
State → Watching two-digit numbers and pressing a spacebar is not a real job.
Why → Real vigilance tasks (driving, security screening) have genuine consequences for errors.
Consequence → Results may not reflect how workers actually perform under EPM in real workplaces — low **ecological validity**.

❌ **Weakness: Unrepresentative sample**
State → 106 undergraduates from one southern US university, recruited via an experiment website.
Why → Students may have different attention capabilities, motivation, and comfort with technology than real workers.
Consequence → Results may not **generalise** to older workers, non-students, or workers in other countries or cultures.

❌ **Weakness: Deception used**
State → Participants were told they were being filmed when they actually were not.
Why → Necessary to create a convincing electronic presence.
Consequence → Raises ethical concerns — participants could not give fully informed consent; however, all were fully debriefed afterwards. The APA ethics committee approved the study.

❌ **Weakness: Short task duration**
State → The vigilance task lasted only 24 minutes.
Why → Real vigilance tasks (e.g., security monitoring, long-haul driving) can last hours.
Consequence → The effects of EPM on attention over a full working shift are unknown — results may not scale up.

**Ethics summary:**
- ✅ Informed consent obtained.
- ✅ Confidentiality maintained (responses anonymous).
- ✅ APA ethics approval gained.
- ✅ Full debrief given.
- ⚠️ Deception used (fake recording equipment) — justified by debrief.
- ⚠️ Credit incentive may reduce right to withdraw (felt obligated to complete to earn credits).
- ⚠️ Long-term EPM in real workplaces may cause stress — study's implications need careful handling.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- EPM = Electronic Performance Monitoring.
- The electronic presence group had more correct detections, fewer false alarms, and faster response times.
- Social facilitation: being observed (even electronically) increases performance on tasks that require sustained attention.
- Key weakness: ecological validity — the task was artificial (two-digit numbers, spacebar).
- Deception was used — webcam was on but nothing was actually recorded.
- Link to: Bateson et al. (2006) — even images of eyes increase prosocial behaviour.
- Link to: Hawthorne Effect — workers perform better when observed.

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
1. Claypoole and Szalma (2019) aimed to determine whether electronic monitoring could improve performance on a sustained attention task.
2. 106 undergraduates were randomly assigned to either a control condition (no monitoring) or an electronic presence condition (webcam + video recorder).
3. Participants surrendered phones and watches; the research assistant left before the task began.
4. The vigilance task required watching a screen of two-digit numbers and pressing a spacebar when a critical signal appeared, over 24 minutes.
5. The electronically monitored group had significantly more correct detections, fewer false alarms, and faster response times.
6. This suggests that electronic monitoring, like a live observer, can improve vigilance performance through social facilitation effects.

## Cross-Study Comparisons

> Use these for evaluation and "issues and debates" questions.

### Ecological validity comparison

| Study | Setting | Ecological validity |
|---|---|---|
| Mayo et al. | Real factory, real workers | ✅ Very high |
| Oldham & Brass | Real newspaper office | ✅ High |
| Gold et al. | Real hospital | ✅ High |
| Fox et al. | Real mines | ✅ Very high |
| Swat (1997) | Real industrial plants | ✅ High |
| Claypoole & Szalma | University lab, artificial task | ❌ Low |

### Generalisability comparison

| Study | Why limited |
|---|---|
| Mayo et al. | One US factory, one culture |
| Oldham & Brass | One US company, 1979 |
| Gold et al. | One US hospital |
| Fox et al. | Two US mines, mining culture only |
| Swat (1997) | Four old Polish plants |
| Claypoole & Szalma | US undergraduates, artificial task |

### Determinism vs. Free Will

- **Mayo / Hawthorne:** Deterministic — implies workers *had to* respond to observation. Kompier disproves this (some resisted).
- **Gold et al. / shift work research:** Deterministic — implies shift patterns determine health outcomes. But individuals can manage diet, sleep hygiene, etc.
- **Claypoole & Szalma:** Deterministic — implies electronic monitoring *will* improve performance. But cultural differences (comfort with surveillance) may change this.

### Individual vs. Situational explanations

| Study | Situational factors | Individual factors |
|---|---|---|
| Hawthorne | Being watched → performance up | Not everyone responded the same |
| Open plan offices | Noise, lack of privacy → motivation down | Some workers adapt better |
| Shift work | Rotating shifts → sleep loss | Individual chronotypes differ |
| Token economy | Rewards → safer behaviour | Risk appetite and responsiveness to rewards vary |
| Swat (1997) | Poor supervision, housekeeping | Individual errors also a cause |
| Claypoole & Szalma | Electronic presence → better attention | Individual anxiety about surveillance varies |
