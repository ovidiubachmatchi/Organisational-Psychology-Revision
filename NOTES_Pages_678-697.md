## KOUZES & POSNER - Leadership Practices Inventory (LPI)

> **TLDR**
> - **Kouzes & Posner** studied leadership over 30 years using case studies, interviews & surveys
> - They identified **5 practices** of exemplary leadership
> - The **LPI** is a psychometric questionnaire (30 items, 10-point scale) measuring those 5 practices
> - Has both a **self** version and an **observer** version (up to 10 raters)
> - Weakness: social desirability bias; may not apply across cultures

### The Five Practices

| # | Practice | What it means (1 line) |
|---|----------|----------------------|
| 1 | **Model the Way** | Lead by example, be honest, stay humble |
| 2 | **Inspire a Shared Vision** | Paint a compelling future; credibility is key |
| 3 | **Challenge the Process** | Seek change, take risks, learn from trial & error |
| 4 | **Enable Others to Act** | Foster collaboration, empower workers to decide |
| 5 | **Encourage the Heart** | Recognise contributions, praise publicly & privately |

**Key details per practice (exam depth):**
- **Model the Way:** Be true to yourself, always tell the truth, do what you say. Remain humble - let workers get praise. "Becoming a leader begins when you come to understand who you are" (K&P 1997)
- **Inspire a Shared Vision:** **Credibility = single most important asset** a leader can have ("if you don't believe in the messenger you won't believe the message"). Be forward-looking.
- **Challenge the Process:** "Only challenge provides the opportunity for greatness" (K&P 1997). Leaders don't wait for change - they make it happen. Don't fear failure - trial & error. Can't do it without workers who turn ideas into action.
- **Encourage the Heart:** Recognise contributions both **publicly and privately**. Verbal praise costs nothing but means everything to the worker.

🔑 **Memory trick: "MICEE"** - Model, Inspire, Challenge, Enable, Encourage

### The LPI (Leadership Practices Inventory)

- **Psychometric** questionnaire, 30 forced-choice items
- **10-point rating scale** (1 = almost never, 5 = occasionally, 10 = almost always)
- 6 questions per practice
- **Two versions:** LPI Self (leader rates themselves) + LPI Observer (up to 10 others rate the leader)
- Results compared against average ratings of other people

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| Quantitative data = more objective & reliable | Psychometric = social desirability bias, lying, exaggeration |
| **Kouzes & Posner (1993):** 2876 managers, no gender bias, no social desirability, no significant org differences | Based on **Western** ideal of leadership |
| Self & observer rank order was identical | Significant cultural differences found in scores |
| Applicable across organisations | Statements may not consider language/culture differences |
| **Application to everyday life:** LPI can identify which practices a leader IS or ISN'T using, then improve (e.g. not enough "encourage the heart" = give more verbal praise) | Leader may know what test is looking for; observer may dislike leader and deliberately lie |

⚡ **Idiographic vs Nomothetic:** The LPI itself is **nomothetic** (psychometric questionnaire), BUT the five practices were originally formulated using **idiographic** methods (case studies + in-depth interviews). So the research used BOTH approaches.

📝 **Exam Bait:** Examiners love asking you to evaluate the LPI as a measurement tool. Always mention: social desirability, cultural differences, and the strength of having both self + observer versions.

## KELLEY (1988) - Followership Styles

> **TLDR**
> - **Kelley (1988)** shifted focus from leaders to **followers**
> - Two dimensions: **critical thinking** (independent vs dependent) and **participation** (active vs passive)
> - These create **5 followership styles**
> - **Exemplary** followers = best performers (high critical thinking + active)
> - Cultural limitation: may not generalise beyond Western cultures

### What is Followership?

- **Crossman & Crossman (2011):** "the ability to effectively follow the directives and support the efforts of a leader to maximise a structured organisation"
- **Wortman (cited in Crossman & Crossman, 2011):** more person-centred - about attaining individual goals by being influenced by a leader
- **General definition:** willingness to cooperate towards defined goals, showing teamwork, following a leader but playing an **active not passive** role

### Two Dimensions

1. **Independent critical thinking** vs dependent non-critical thinking - Do they question and analyse, or just accept?
2. **Active participation** vs passive participation - Do they act, or wait for others?

### Five Followership Styles

| Style | Critical Thinking | Participation | Key Feature |
|-------|:-:|:-:|-------------|
| **Passive** ("Sheep") | Low | Low | Do tasks then stop, no initiative |
| **Alienated** | High | Low | Cynical, negative, whisper on sidelines (often passed over for promotion) |
| **Pragmatic Survivor** | Mid | Mid | "Better safe than sorry", shifts style, early warning system |
| **Conformist** ("Yes People") | Low | High | Obedient, never question, bosses with low confidence like them |
| **Exemplary** | High | High | Most effective, risk-takers, problem-solvers, will challenge leaders |

🔑 **Memory trick: "SPACE"** - Sheep, Pragmatic, Alienated, Conformist, Exemplary

⚡ **Exemplary followers** can succeed without strong leadership. They don't need to become leaders and find fulfilment as followers. They don't see the leader as a hero, but as **a person who manages change**. Effective followers **manage themselves well, are committed to the organisation, build up their skills, and are courageous and honest.**

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Favara (2009):** 175 workers, 3 questionnaires - significant positive correlation between exemplary style and job satisfaction + performance | **Reductionist** - breaks followership into 5 types on 2 dimensions, ignores situational factors |
| First theory to focus on followers, not leaders = useful for organisations | **Oyetunji (2013):** Botswana lecturers self-reported - passive followers rated themselves as best performers (opposite of theory!) = cultural differences |
| **Application to everyday life:** Orgs can shift focus from seeking effective leaders to also seeking effective followers = increase morale + productivity | Followers may be a mix of types, not just one - doesn't consider situational aspects of a task or organisation |

📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare Kelley's followership with leadership theories. Examiners love the individual vs situational debate here - Kelley is more on the individual side (types of people) but ignores situational factors like Milgram showed.

## TUCKMAN & JENSEN (2010) - Stages of Group Development

> **TLDR**
> - **Tuckman (1965)** reviewed 55 articles and proposed 4 stages of group development
> - **Tuckman & Jensen (2010)** added a 5th stage (adjourning) after reviewing 22 more articles
> - Stages are **linear**: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning
> - Weakness: lacks empirical testing; stages may overlap in reality

### The Five Stages

| Stage | What Happens |
|-------|-------------|
| 1. **Forming** | Getting to know each other, uncertainty, seeking guidance, worrying about fitting in |
| 2. **Storming** | Most critical stage, conflict, personalities clash, subgroups form (led by strongest personalities), performance may drop. Must "storm through" conflicting ideas or **resentment may follow** |
| 3. **Norming** | Conflict resolves, unity, roles allocated, leaders confirmed. Risk: **dominant members take over**, quieter members reluctant to challenge. **Groupthink** if storming was skipped |
| 4. **Performing** | Team is organised & mature, cooperation strong, creativity & innovation high |
| 5. **Adjourning** | Goals accomplished, group disperses, may cause regret, formal "ceremony" may happen |

🔑 **Memory trick: "Furious Storms Never Prevent Awesome-ness"** - Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Dynamic model** - updated with new evidence (added 5th stage) | Lacks **empirical evidence** - Tuckman admitted more research needed |
| 22 articles broadly supported the model | Only **Runkel (1971)** specifically set out to empirically test it - mainly supported it but methodology was prone to **observer bias** |
| Two studies + a review identified **termination** as final stage = led to adding adjourning | Amended version based on mainly **theoretical articles** rather than empirical studies |
| Widely applicable to real teams | Linear stages may not reflect reality - stages can **overlap and be revisited** |

📝 **Exam Bait:** Validity is a key eval point here. The model is dynamic (strength) but splitting group behaviour into distinct linear stages may not be realistic (weakness).

## BELBIN - Nine Team Roles

> **TLDR**
> - **Belbin** observed teams in the 1970s and identified **9 team roles**
> - Found behaviour matters more than intelligence in teams
> - Measured using the **Belbin Self Perception Inventory** (forced-choice + observer statements)
> - Weakness: Western-based, self-reports prone to bias

### The Nine Roles

| Role | Strengths | Allowable Weaknesses |
|------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Plant** | Creative, innovative, solves complex problems | Focuses on own ideas, not group's |
| **Resource Investigator** | Enthusiastic, great communicator, develops contacts | Enthusiasm fades quickly |
| **Co-ordinator** | Spots talent, delegates well, trusting | May manipulate others |
| **Shaper** | Driven, competitive, decisive, overcomes obstacles | Argumentative, aggressive |
| **Monitor Evaluator** | Careful thinker, high critical thinking, deals in facts | Slow, overly critical, boring |
| **Implementer** | Reliable, efficient, systematic, disciplined | Rigid, resists new ways |
| **Teamworker** | Supportive, diplomatic, flexible, good listener | Indecisive in conflict |
| **Completer Finisher** | Attention to detail, high standards, accurate | Anxious, reluctant to trust others |
| **Specialist** | Expert knowledge, respected authority | Not a natural team player, stubborn |

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Nomothetic** - uses reliable, valid, scientific methods | Self-reports = social desirability bias |
| Uses both self-perception + observer statements (triangulation). Observer statements are **not fixed choices** and 6 responses are correlated to check validity | Forced-choice may not reflect real-life actions, don't allow responders to give reasons |
| Quantitative = objective & reliable data | May not apply to **collectivist** cultures that favour teamwork over individual roles |
| Observer statements check validity | Observer reports affected by external conflicts |

## GROUPTHINK - Janis (1971)

> **TLDR**
> - **Janis (1971):** Groupthink = desire for agreement overrides objective decision-making
> - Caused by: group norms, social identity, cohesiveness, group isolation, stress
> - **8 symptoms** of groupthink (invulnerability, rationale, morality, stereotypes, pressure, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, mindguards)
> - Real examples: **Challenger Disaster (1986)**, **Bay of Pigs (1961)**
> - 4 strategies to avoid it

### What Causes Groupthink?

- **Group norms** + strong **social identity** + **cohesiveness** suppress critical thinking
- **Group isolation** - no outside information
- **Conformity** increases as cohesiveness increases (counterintuitive!)
- **Stress** - crisis situations make it worse
- **Golkar (2013):** High stress + low self-esteem (from past failures) + low self-efficacy = more groupthink
- Also more likely if the decision creates a **moral dilemma**

### The 8 Symptoms

| Symptom | What it means |
|---------|--------------|
| **Invulnerability** | Illusion group can't fail, over-optimism, risk-taking |
| **Rationale** | Ignoring warnings, rationalising beliefs (e.g. before Pearl Harbor, warnings of Japanese attack were ignored) |
| **Morality** | Belief group has high morals, ignoring moral consequences |
| **Stereotypes** | Stereotypical views of outgroups |
| **Pressure** | Pressuring members who express doubt |
| **Self-censorship** | Avoiding expressing opinions that deviate from group |
| **Illusion of unanimity** | Assuming silence = agreement |
| **Mindguards** | People who guard the group from outside dissent |

🔑 **Memory trick: "I Run My Stuff Past Some Intelligent Minds"** - Invulnerability, Rationale, Morality, Stereotypes, Pressure, Self-censorship, Illusion of unanimity, Mindguards

### Real-Life Examples

**Challenger Disaster (1986):** Exploded 73 seconds after take-off
- Thiokol engineers had doubts about **temperature** at launch and said abort, but NASA managers claimed everyone agreed (illusion of unanimity)
- Engineers pressured to reverse doubts - already 3 failed launch attempts + their own company worried about losing NASA contracts (pressure)
- Experts not consulted for final decision-maker Jesse Moore (mindguards)
- Cohesive group who had worked together for years, engineers isolated from decisions (group isolation)

**Bay of Pigs (1961):** American soldiers tried to overthrow Cuban Government (Fidel Castro)
- Group made decisions to please President Kennedy (knew what he wanted)
- Used negative stereotypes of the "enemy"
- No openness to new information
- Result: disaster, nearly started a war with Russia

### 4 Strategies to Avoid Groupthink

| Strategy | But... (limitation) |
|----------|-------------------|
| Invite outside experts to challenge ideas | People may act differently, not be honest |
| Assign a "devil's advocate" role | Only works if group believes the person is genuine |
| Senior managers tell group they share responsibility for consequences | May lead to "safe" decisions rather than correct ones |
| Every member = critical evaluator, must air doubts | Decision-making takes longer, could cause dysfunctional conflict |

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| Applied to real life (Bay of Pigs, Challenger, Iraq War) = useful | **Aldag & Fuller (1993):** Support comes from retrospective case studies = bias + faulty memory |
| **Holistic** - considers both personality AND situation | Case studies = political decisions at highest level, not typical workplaces |
| Janis: conformity, cohesiveness, stress (situational) + Golkar: low self-esteem (individual) | All case studies from **individualist cultures** (USA) - may not apply to collectivist nations (decision-making may differ in collectivist cultures where group > individual) |
| Strategies can be implemented to prevent it | **Fuller & Aldag (1998):** Groupthink has become a populist model, talked about in news/social media - every faulty decision gets labelled groupthink, other factors ignored |

⚡ **Counter-argument on culture:** The process of group formation may be **universal**, and groupthink is still a popular and widely used theory across the world - so concerns about cultural generalisability may be misplaced.

📝 **Exam Bait:** Examiners LOVE groupthink. Know: the 8 symptoms, at least one real-life example with specific symptoms, AND strategies + their limitations. Also be ready to discuss individual vs situational debate.

## FORSYTH (2006) - Cognitive Limitations & Errors

> **TLDR**
> - **Forsyth (2006)** / **Kerr et al. (1992):** Group decisions go wrong due to cognitive biases
> - Three categories: **sins of commission**, **sins of omission**, **sins of imprecision**
> - **Confirmation bias** is stronger in groups than in individuals
> - Strategy: diverse group membership reduces confirmation bias
> - Weakness: reductionist, ignores stress/sleep deprivation

### Three Categories of Decisional Sin

**1. Sins of Commission** (using wrong/irrelevant info)
- **Belief perseverance** - relying on info already shown to be inaccurate
- **Sunk cost bias** - won't change course because of prior investment
- **Extra-evidentiary bias** - using info you were told to ignore
- **Hindsight bias** - overestimating accuracy of your prior knowledge

**2. Sins of Omission** (missing useful info)
- **Base rate bias** - failing to consider general trends
- **Fundamental attribution error** - blaming personality, ignoring situation (seen in individualist cultures, not collectivist)

**3. Sins of Imprecision** (oversimplifying)
- **Availability heuristic** - basing decisions on readily available info
- **Conjunction bias** - failing to recognise two events together are less probable than one alone
- **Representativeness heuristic** - relying on factors that seem logical but are misleading

⚡ **Confirmation bias** = not seeking info that contradicts your conclusion, only seeking info that confirms it. **Kerr et al. (1992):** This is STRONGER in groups than individuals.

🔑 **Memory trick for 3 sins: "COI"** - Commission (wrong info), Omission (missing info), Imprecision (sloppy shortcuts)

### Strategies to Avoid Cognitive Errors

- **Educate** yourself on cognitive errors so you can spot them
- To fix **fundamental attribution error:** actively think of situational factors, ask "would I have acted the same way?"
- Ensure group has **diversity** of opinion + leader who won't suppress challenge

### Strategies to Avoid Confirmation Bias

- Unanimous groups show strongest confirmation bias
- Groups with **at least 1 minority member** showed less bias
- Groups with **2 minority members** were better than individuals at overcoming it

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Osmani (2016):** 86% recommended project with sunk cost vs 26% without = supports cognitive bias in groups | **Reductionist** - ignores stress, sleep deprivation, other factors |
| Applicable to everyday life - helps form diverse groups + train members | Fundamental attribution error only in individualist cultures |
| Strategies can be implemented in real orgs | Not a complete theory of decision-making |

📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare groupthink vs cognitive errors. Both explain faulty decision-making but groupthink is more holistic (includes stress, personality, situation) while Forsyth is more reductionist (just cognitive biases).

## SOCIAL FACILITATION & SOCIAL LOAFING

> **TLDR**
> - **Social facilitation** = performing BETTER in presence of others (simple tasks)
> - **Social inhibition** = performing WORSE in presence of others (complex tasks)
> - Explained by **Drive Theory (Zajonc, 1966)** and **Evaluation Apprehension**
> - **Social loafing** = putting in less effort in a group
> - Explained by **Social Impact Theory (Latane, 1981)** and **diffusion of responsibility**

### Social Facilitation

- **Definition:** Tendency to perform better/faster in the presence of others
- **Triplett (1898):** Cyclists faster racing others than alone; 40 children turned reel quicker in pairs

**Two types of "presence":**
- **Co-action effect** - people working alongside you
- **Audience effect** - people watching you

### Drive Theory (Zajonc, 1966)

- Presence of others increases **arousal**
- Simple/well-known task + arousal = **better performance** (social facilitation)
- Complex/novel task + arousal = **worse performance** (social inhibition)
- The **dominant response** (habitual behaviour) kicks in - for easy tasks that's correct, for hard tasks that's often wrong
- Supported by **Yerkes-Dodson Law:** optimal arousal = peak performance; too much or too little = poor performance
- Individual differences matter: socially anxious people have higher baseline arousal = audience effects are greater

### Evaluation Apprehension

- Anxiety from worrying about being **judged negatively**
- We learn audiences may evaluate us, not just passively watch
- If confident + simple task = performance enhanced
- If not confident + complex task = performance inhibited
- More influential/experienced observers = higher arousal + apprehension

⚡ **Schmitt et al. (1986):** 45 undergraduates in 3 conditions (alone / blindfolded+headphones observer / evaluating experimenter). Tasks began **before participants realised the experiment had started** (reduces demand characteristics). Simple task (typing name) = faster with audience. Complex task (typing name backwards with ascending numbers between letters) = slower with audience. Most pronounced in evaluating condition. Supports BOTH evaluation apprehension AND mere presence effects.

### Social Loafing

- **Definition:** People put in less individual effort in a group
- **Ringelmann (1913):** Rope-pulling study (rope attached to pressure gauge) - more people = further below individual potential. Due to **coordination loss** (main reason) + **motivation loss**
- Groups still outperform individuals, but NOT at the rate expected if everyone gave 100%
- Social loafing becomes more likely if person is **not being individually evaluated** = more anonymous

### Social Impact Theory (Latane, 1981)

- Social influence depends on: **strength**, **immediacy**, and **number** of others
- In groups, influence is **diffused** across all members
- As group size increases, each person has less individual influence
- **Diffusion of responsibility** - less likely to take responsibility when others are present
- More anonymous = more loafing (no individual evaluation)

⚡ **Latane (1981):** Subjects stood in a **semi-circle**, clapped/shouted as loud as possible individually then with group. Noise increased with group size but **NOT proportionally**. Individuals made less noise as group got bigger.

📝 **Exam Bait:** Social facilitation vs social loafing is a classic comparison question. Facilitation = presence of others HELPS (simple tasks). Loafing = presence of others means you do LESS. Link drive theory to Yerkes-Dodson. Know Schmitt et al. and Ringelmann as evidence.

## Comparison Tables for Quick Revision

### Groupthink vs Cognitive Errors

| | Groupthink (Janis, 1971) | Cognitive Errors (Forsyth, 2006) |
|-|--------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Focus** | Social pressures in groups | Cognitive biases in individuals/groups |
| **Approach** | Holistic (personality + situation) | Reductionist (just cognition) |
| **Key causes** | Cohesiveness, stress, conformity, isolation | Heuristics, confirmation bias, attribution errors |
| **Evidence** | Retrospective case studies (political) | Experimental research (Osmani, 2016) |
| **Culture** | Based on individualist examples | Fundamental attribution error = individualist only |

### Social Facilitation vs Social Loafing

| | Social Facilitation | Social Loafing |
|-|--------------------|----|
| **Effect** | Performance improves with others | Performance decreases with others |
| **When** | Simple/well-practised tasks | Group tasks with no individual evaluation |
| **Why** | Increased arousal (drive theory) | Diffusion of responsibility |
| **Key study** | Schmitt et al. (1986) | Ringelmann (1913), Latane (1981) |

### Kouzes & Posner / LPI

**Q: Describe the LPI and how it measures leadership practices. [6]**
- 30-item psychometric questionnaire, 10-point rating scale
- 6 questions per practice across 5 practices
- Two versions: LPI Self (leader rates self) + LPI Observer (up to 10 others rate leader)
- Forced-choice statements about frequency of specific behaviours
- Results compared to average ratings of others
- Developed from 30 years of case studies, interviews, surveys

**Q: Evaluate the LPI, discussing validity. [10]**
- (+) Quantitative data = objective, reliable, not subject to experimenter bias
- (+) Kouzes & Posner (1993): 2876 managers, no gender bias, identical rank order self vs observer
- (-) Psychometrics = social desirability, exaggeration, lying
- (-) Leader may know what test is looking for; observer may dislike leader
- (-) Cultural differences: significant score differences due to culture/language, lowering validity
- (-) Western-based = limited generalisability
- Link to issues: idiographic vs nomothetic (LPI is nomothetic but built on idiographic methods)
