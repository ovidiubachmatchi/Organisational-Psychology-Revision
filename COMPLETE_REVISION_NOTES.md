# 9.1 NEED THEORIES

---

## Maslow (1943) -- Hierarchy of Needs

> TLDR
> - 5-level pyramid: physiology -> safety -> belonging -> esteem -> self-actualisation
> - Must satisfy lower needs before moving up
> - Bottom 4 = **deficiency needs**; top = **growth need**
> - Applied to work: pay & safety first, then belonging, then challenge/autonomy

### The 5 Levels (bottom to top)

| Level | Need | Workplace example |
|-------|------|-------------------|
| 1 | **Physiological** -- food, water, warmth, sleep | Adequate wages to buy food & stay warm |
| 2 | **Safety** -- security, stability, health | Pension, safe work environment |
| 3 | **Belongingness** -- friendship, connection, group | Feeling accepted, social interaction |
| 4 | **Esteem** -- respect, recognition, achievement | Promotion, better job title, new skills |
| 5 | **Self-actualisation** -- fulfilling potential | Autonomy, challenge, freedom from supervision |

### Key concepts

- **Deficiency needs** = levels 1-4; motivated by lacking something; drive gets stronger the longer unmet
- **Growth needs** = level 5; motivated by personal growth; motivation *increases* as you grow
- People can **slide back down** the hierarchy (e.g. job loss, divorce)
- Maslow believed everyone *can* reach self-actualisation but many never do

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Holistic** -- considers physiological AND psychological needs (more complete than cognitive theories) | **Validity issue** -- assumes lower needs must be met first, but people in poverty still show love & belonging (e.g. Mother Teresa) |
| **Useful application** -- employers can use it to improve wages, safety, belonging -> increases motivation | **Cultural bias** -- self-actualisation may mean different things in **collectivist** vs **individualistic** cultures |
| Humanistic = supports **free will** (you can choose to change jobs if needs unmet) | Behaviour still partly **determined** by external forces (e.g. can't find another job) |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Examiners love asking you to *apply* Maslow to a workplace scenario. Practice linking each level to a specific workplace action.

### Supporting study: Saeednia (2011)

- **Aim:** Develop a reliable scale (BNSS) to measure basic needs satisfaction in children
- **Method:** 13 children + parents & educators interviewed first (open-ended Qs -> qualitative data); themes coded using Maslow's theory; used to construct the **Basic Needs Satisfaction Scale (BNSS)**; after a pilot study, BNSS given to **300 participants** randomly chosen from 457
- **Result:** High reliability (0.83)
- **Conclusion:** Qualitative methods may be more appropriate for investigating Maslow -- children tend to "unconsciously pretend" a need is being met, especially if the environment won't help meet it
- **Finding:** Life satisfaction varies by culture -- poorer nations focus on safety; wealthier on love/esteem (consistent with Maslow)
- **Weakness 1:** Sample only from **wealthy areas of Tehran** -- low generalisability to less wealthy areas or nations
- **Weakness 2:** Children have **limited ability to express themselves fully** -- may affect **validity** of results (even though parents/educators were also interviewed)
- **Strength 1:** Shows Maslow's theory *can* be measured **quantitatively** -> overcomes criticism that the theory is unscientific; increases reliability
- **Strength 2:** One of the **first attempts** to produce a questionnaire for children's basic needs -- provides a basis for future research to refine the tool

## McClelland (1961) -- Achievement Motivation Theory

> TLDR
> - 3 work needs: **Achievement**, **Affiliation**, **Power**
> - Everyone has all 3 but one is **dominant** (shaped by life experience & culture)
> - Measured using the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** -- a projective test
> - Useful for placing employees in roles that suit their dominant need

### The 3 Work Needs

**1. Need for Achievement**
- Urge to master tasks and succeed
- Avoid *too easy* (no real achievement) AND *too hard* (success = luck)
- Prefer moderate challenge, regular feedback, working alone or with other high achievers
- McClelland said high achievers make the **best leaders**
- Low achievers: avoid responsibility, fear failure

**2. Need for Affiliation**
- Want social interaction, to be liked and accepted
- Conform to group norms, avoid conflict, fear rejection
- Prefer collaboration over competition
- Don't like being singled out (even for praise!)
- Suited to: customer service, team activities
- Low affiliators: distant, avoid social interaction

**3. Need for Power**
- Desire control, authority, influence over decisions
- Want prestige and status
- Two types:
  - **Personal power** = control over *others*
  - **Institutional (social) power** = organise a group to meet business goals
- Best managers have **institutional** power, not personal power
- Low power: dependent on others, won't lead

> ⚡ **Memory trick:** **A-A-P** = Achievement, Affiliation, Power. Think "AAP" like an app that matches people to jobs.

### Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

- **Projective test** -- shown ~20 ambiguous pictures
- Asked to make up a story about each one
- Stories reveal **unconscious motives/needs**
- McClelland devised a scoring system for power, affiliation, achievement

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Practical application** -- helps organisations place people in best-fit roles; improves productivity & retention | **Cultural/gender bias** -- constructed using Western males; may not apply to women or collectivist cultures (e.g. Japan) |
| Scoring system for TAT is relatively reliable & can be analysed statistically | **Only considers individual needs** -- ignores situational factors (e.g. power needs require others to be submissive) |
| | TAT measures **unobservable** characteristics from preconscious mind -- results can't be disproven |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare Maslow and McClelland -- both are need theories but Maslow = hierarchy (universal order), McClelland = individual dominant need (no fixed order). McClelland's needs are *learned*, Maslow's are *innate*.

## Locke & Latham -- Goal-Setting Theory

> TLDR
> - **Locke et al. (1968)** first proposed SMART goals; **Locke & Latham (1990)** expanded with 5 characteristics
> - Specific, challenging goals motivate better than vague ones
> - Use **SMART** goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound
> - 5 characteristics of effective goals: Clarity, Challenge, Commitment, Feedback, Task complexity
> - Based on 50+ years of research -- high reliability

### SMART Goals (Locke et al., 1968)

| Letter | Meaning | Key question |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **S** | Specific | What exactly do I want to achieve? |
| **M** | Measurable | How will I track progress? |
| **A** | Achievable | Is it realistic? Do I have the resources? |
| **R** | Realistic/Relevant | What's the benefit? |
| **T** | Time-bound | What's the deadline? |

- Best goals = ones you're **not 100% sure** you'll achieve, but realistic with hard work
- "Increase sales of fluffy pink slippers by 50% in 28 days" > "Let's get more slippers sold soon"

### 5 Characteristics of Effective Goal Setting (Locke & Latham, 1990)

1. **Clarity** -- specific, unambiguous, measurable
2. **Challenge** -- not too easy (demotivating), not unrealistic; linked to rewards
3. **Commitment** -- workforce must understand and be invested
4. **Feedback** -- regular feedback keeps people on track
5. **Task complexity** -- realistic timescales, sub-goals, regular reviews

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Evidence-based** -- built on 50+ years of research, still used today; high reliability | **Reductionist** -- only considers cognitive factors (goal-setting), ignores physiological needs & past experiences |
| **Useful application** -- helps managers plan, monitor progress, spot problems early; increases worker motivation | **Cultural bias** -- based on individualistic Western values (USA); may not generalise to collectivist cultures |
| Ethically sound -- improves workplace for both workers & managers | |

> ⚡ **Memory trick for 5 characteristics:** "**C**lever **C**ats **C**an **F**ind **T**una" = Clarity, Challenge, Commitment, Feedback, Task complexity

## Vroom (1964) -- VIE / Expectancy Theory

> TLDR
> - Motivation = **Valence x Instrumentality x Expectancy**
> - If ANY of the 3 is low/zero, motivation = low/zero
> - People make **rational, conscious** choices to maximise pleasure and minimise pain
> - Individual factors (personality, skills, experience) affect performance

### The 3 Beliefs (V-I-E)

| Factor | What it means | Workplace example |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Valence** | Strength/value of the expected reward (intrinsic or extrinsic) | Big bonus vs small bonus; does the employee actually *want* the reward? |
| **Instrumentality** | Belief that good performance WILL lead to the promised reward | Trust in decision-makers; clear, transparent link between performance and outcome |
| **Expectancy** | Belief that effort WILL lead to good performance | Having the right skills, resources, support; training & mentoring raise expectancy |

### The Formula

**Motivation = V x I x E**

- Multiplied, not added -- so if *any one* = 0, total motivation = 0
- Common theme: **reward** -- if reward is small, motivation tends to be low overall

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Predictive** -- Redmond (2009) found V, I, and E all correlated highly with predicting choices; useful theory | **Doesn't always apply** -- in real workplaces, rewards often aren't directly linked to performance |
| Considers **individual factors** (personality, skills) unlike Maslow who assumes everyone is the same | **Assumes rational choices** -- ignores short-term needs, personality quirks, unconscious processes |
| | **Nomothetic** -- establishes a formula for ALL workers; doesn't look at what uniquely motivates individuals |
| | **Environmentally deterministic** -- says motivation is determined by expected rewards/costs; ignores free will |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare Vroom with Maslow -- Vroom = individual differences matter (idiographic element); Maslow = everyone follows the same hierarchy (nomothetic). But Vroom's formula itself is nomothetic (applies to all).

> ⚡ **Memory trick:** **VIE** = "**V**ery **I**mportant **E**xpectations" -- all 3 must be high or motivation dies.

## Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation

> TLDR
> - **Extrinsic** = external rewards (pay, bonuses, profit sharing) -- driven by **operant conditioning**; short-term, can cause burnout
> - **Intrinsic** = internal feelings (recognition, praise, respect, empowerment, belonging) -- longer lasting
> - Over-reliance on extrinsic rewards can **decrease** intrinsic motivation
> - Best approach: combine both, but frame rewards supportively

### Extrinsic Motivators

| Type | What it is | Watch out for |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| **Pay** | Salary to cover bills and enjoy life | Basic motivator; expected, not bonus |
| **Bonuses** | Extra rewards based on targets (individual or company) | Can be monetary or non-monetary; motivates short-term effort |
| **Profit sharing** | % of company profits awarded to employees | Only works if done fairly; can boost morale & productivity |
| **Performance-related pay** | Reward when work meets a standard; reviewed via **appraisal** | Targets must be realistic or motivation *decreases* |

### Intrinsic Motivators

| Type | What it is | Key detail |
|------|-----------|------------|
| **Recognition** | Verbal appreciation for performance | Formal (appraisal) or informal; must be linked to job performance, not just targets |
| **Praise** | Saying "well done" | Over-praising *reduces* effect; must be meaningful and targeted |
| **Respect** | Active listening, not insulting or dismissing ideas | Direct impact on motivation and productivity |
| **Empowerment** | Letting workers make decisions about their own work; giving **autonomy** | Shows trust; gives sense of ownership; mastery of tasks |
| **Sense of belonging** | Feeling valued, trusted, proud to be part of the team | Links to Maslow's belongingness need |

## Deci & Ryan (1985) -- Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

> TLDR
> - People have a **need for growth** -- mastery over challenges is key
> - **Autonomous** (self-determined) motivation = intrinsic = best kind
> - 3 basic needs: **Autonomy**, **Relatedness**, **Competence** (ARC)
> - Too many extrinsic rewards can *kill* intrinsic motivation

### 2 Key Assumptions

1. **Need for growth drives behaviour** -- people want to improve and master challenges (linked to intrinsic motivation)
2. **Autonomous motivation is important** -- purely self-determined behaviour = intrinsic, done for pleasure; non-self-determined = no pleasure, no choice

### 3 Basic Psychological Needs

| Need | Definition | Increases when... | Decreases when... |
|------|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Autonomy** | Self-governance; control over own behaviour | Workers have choice over how they work | Controlled by others; constant deadlines; too many extrinsic rewards for already-intrinsic behaviour |
| **Relatedness** | Building relationships; feeling attached and belonging | Respected by others; people care about your feelings | Criticised; excluded from groups; isolated |
| **Competence** | Having the right skills to perform a task; mastery | Task matches skill level; positive/constructive feedback | Task too hard; too much negative feedback |

> ⚡ **Memory trick:** **ARC** = Autonomy, Relatedness, Competence. Think: "building an ARC of motivation to stay afloat at work."

### 🔑 Critical point: Extrinsic rewards can UNDERMINE intrinsic motivation

- If extrinsic rewards are given constantly for behaviour that was already intrinsically motivated...
- ...the worker starts caring ONLY about the reward, not the satisfaction
- This is supported by **Deci & Ryan (1999)**: reviewed 128 experiments -- task-dependent/performance-related rewards *decreased* intrinsic motivation; positive feedback *enhanced* it
- **Kohn (1994)**: using rewards to control behaviour leads to more surveillance and competition -> further decreases intrinsic motivation

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Research support** -- Deci & Ryan (1999) review of 128 experiments supports the theory | **Deterministic** -- suggests motivation is determined by type of reward received; ignores individual personality differences |
| **Practical application** -- managers can avoid controlling rewards and use collaborative atmosphere instead | **Ethnocentric** -- constructed using Western ideas of motivation; collectivist cultures may value autonomy less |
| Useful for real-life -- increases worker satisfaction & productivity | **Haggar et al. (2013)**: individualistic groups preferred personal choice; collectivist groups preferred being told by a manager -> different cultures, different motivators |

# KEY STUDY: Landry et al. (2019) -- Study 1

> TLDR
> - Tested whether HOW you frame a monetary reward affects motivation and performance
> - Key concept: **functional meaning** -- the meaning employees give to a reward (informational vs controlling)
> - **Autonomy-supportive framing** (informational) -> higher intrinsic motivation -> better performance
> - **Controlling framing** -> higher extrinsic motivation -> NO performance boost
> - Supports self-determination theory: it's not *whether* you reward, but *how*

### Hypotheses

- **H1:** Presenting rewards in an autonomy-supportive way (informational) leads to **greater performance** than presenting them in a controlling way
- **H2:** Informational rewards -> greater needs satisfaction -> higher **intrinsic** motivation -> better performance; Controlling rewards -> greater needs frustration -> higher **extrinsic** motivation

### The Study at a Glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Aim** | Does the way monetary rewards are presented affect motivation & performance? |
| **Method** | Lab experiment + self-report questionnaires (quantitative) |
| **Sample** | 123 student volunteers, mean age 23, Canadian university; **60% female**; randomly assigned |
| **IV** | Autonomy-supportive (informational) vs autonomy-threatening (controlling) instruction paragraph |
| **Task** | Solve 25 four-letter anagrams in 2 minutes |
| **Reward** | $10 gift card for local coffee shop |
| **DV** | Performance score, intrinsic/extrinsic motivation, needs satisfaction/frustration (measured via 7-point Likert scales: **Basic Psychological Needs Satisfaction Scale**, **Psychological Needs Thwarting Scale**, **Situational Motivation Scale**) |

### Two Conditions

| Autonomy-Supportive (n=65) | Controlling (n=58) |
|----------------------------|-------------------|
| Supportive, encouraging language | Pressurising language |
| Reward = "token of appreciation for contribution" | Reward = "to reinforce performance standards" |
| **Informational** meaning | **Controlling** meaning |

### Key Results

| Measure | Autonomy-supportive | Controlling |
|---------|-------------------|-------------|
| Performance | **0.76** | 0.54 |
| Intrinsic motivation | **5.31** | 4.56 |
| Extrinsic motivation | 3.10 | **3.95** |
| Needs satisfaction | **5.79** | 5.39 |
| Needs frustration | 2.41 | **2.83** |

- Autonomy-supportive group = **higher performance, higher intrinsic motivation, higher needs satisfaction**
- Controlling group = higher extrinsic motivation, higher needs frustration
- Extrinsic motivation did **NOT** predict performance

### Mediation (important for Hypothesis 2)

- **Needs satisfaction** acted as a **mediator** between condition and intrinsic motivation, AND between condition and performance
- **Needs frustration** mediated -> extrinsic motivation (significant)
- BUT: extrinsic motivation as a mediator between needs satisfaction and performance was **NOT significant**

### Conclusions

1. Rewards framed in an **autonomy-supportive** way -> increased performance
2. Autonomy-supportive rewards -> increased needs satisfaction -> intrinsic motivation -> better performance
3. Controlling rewards -> needs frustration -> extrinsic motivation -> NO performance benefit

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Good controls** -- measured perceived value of reward & affect (emotions) to control for individual differences; increases validity | **Sampling bias** -- all from one course at one Canadian university; hard to generalise to other cultures/workplaces (textbook notes "153" participants in evaluation section -- likely a typo; methodology says 123) |
| **Quantitative data** -- Likert scales + performance scores allow statistical comparison; increases reliability | **Low ecological validity** -- lab experiment, not a real workplace; artificial environment |
| **Ethical** -- informed consent, no deception, confidentiality maintained, no distress | **Reductionist** -- only considers self-determination; ignores personality, experience, age, pre-existing competence |
| **Practical application** -- shows organisations how to frame rewards to boost motivation & performance | Results may not transfer to real-life workplace situations |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Landry et al. is the **compulsory key study** for Motivation at Work. Be ready to describe the procedure, results AND evaluate it. Examiners especially like asking about: controls, ecological validity, sampling bias, and application to real life.

## PART 1 — QUICK-FIRE Q&A

**Q1: Describe Maslow's hierarchy of needs. [6]**
- 5 levels: physiological, safety, belongingness, esteem, self-actualisation
- Lower needs must be satisfied before progressing upward
- Levels 1-4 = **deficiency needs** (motivated by what you lack)
- Level 5 = **growth need** (motivation increases as you grow)
- People can regress down the hierarchy (job loss, divorce)
- Applied to work: employers ensure wages, safety, belonging, recognition, then autonomy

**Q2: Evaluate Maslow's hierarchy of needs, including application to real life. [10]**
- **Strength -- application:** employers use it to ensure pay, safety, social needs met -> improves motivation & retention
- **Strength -- holistic:** considers both physiological AND psychological factors (unlike purely cognitive theories)
- **Weakness -- validity:** assumes fixed order but people in poverty can show love/belonging (Mother Teresa example)
- **Weakness -- cultural bias:** self-actualisation defined in Western/individualistic terms; collectivist cultures may differ
- **Debate -- free will vs determinism:** humanistic = supports free will, but external forces (e.g. no jobs available) can determine behaviour
- **Supporting evidence:** Saeednia (2011) -- BNSS scale shows needs differ by culture (poorer nations = safety; wealthier = love/esteem) -- supports Maslow but also shows cultural limitations

**Q3: Explain two characteristics of a person with high affiliation needs (McClelland). [4]**
- They want to be **liked and accepted** -- they conform to group norms and won't challenge the group because they fear rejection
- They **prefer collaboration over competition** -- they dislike being singled out (even for praise) and perform well in team activities

**Q4: Explain what is meant by a SMART target. [2]**
- A goal that is **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic, **T**ime-bound
- More effective than vague goals because progress can be tracked and outcomes are clear

**Q5: Outline what is meant by valence in Vroom's expectancy theory. [2]**
- **Valence** = the perceived strength or value of an expected reward
- It can be intrinsic (sense of achievement) or extrinsic (money/bonus); higher valence = higher motivation

**Q6: Explain why Locke and Latham's theory could be said to be reductionist. [2]**
- It only considers **cognitive factors** (setting specific, achievable goals)
- It ignores other factors like **physiological needs** (Maslow) or the **role of past experiences**, making it an incomplete explanation of motivation

**Q7: Explain one difference between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. [2]**
- **Extrinsic** motivation is driven by **external** factors such as pay, bonuses, or avoiding punishment
- **Intrinsic** motivation is driven by **internal** feelings such as satisfaction, pride, or a sense of achievement from completing a task

**Q8: Suggest two ways organisations could use self-determination theory to increase motivation. [4]**
- Increase **autonomy** by giving workers choice over how and when they complete tasks, which increases their sense of ownership and intrinsic motivation
- Increase **competence** by providing constructive feedback and matching task difficulty to workers' skill levels, so workers feel capable and can achieve mastery

**Q9: Describe the procedure and results of Landry et al. (2019). [6]**
- 123 students randomly assigned to autonomy-supportive or controlling condition
- Read an instruction paragraph framing a $10 gift card reward as either informational ("token of appreciation") or controlling ("reinforce performance standards")
- Completed 25 anagrams in 2 minutes; then rated needs satisfaction, motivation on Likert scales
- **Results:** autonomy-supportive group scored higher on performance (0.76 vs 0.54), intrinsic motivation (5.31 vs 4.56), and needs satisfaction (5.79 vs 5.39)
- Controlling group had higher extrinsic motivation (3.95 vs 3.10) and needs frustration (2.83 vs 2.41)
- Extrinsic motivation did NOT predict better performance

**Q10: Evaluate Landry et al. (2019), including a discussion of application to real life. [10]**
- **Strength -- controls:** measured reward value and affect to control individual differences -> increases validity
- **Strength -- quantitative data:** Likert scales and performance scores allow statistical comparison -> reliable
- **Strength -- application:** shows organisations that framing rewards supportively (informational) boosts intrinsic motivation and performance; practical for managers
- **Weakness -- ecological validity:** lab experiment, not a real workplace -> results may not transfer to actual jobs
- **Weakness -- sampling bias:** all participants from one Canadian university course -> low generalisability, especially cross-culturally
- **Weakness -- reductionist:** only considers self-determination; ignores personality, age, experience
- **Ethical strength:** informed consent, confidentiality, no deception or distress

**Q11: Compare two need theories of motivation. [6+]**
- **Maslow:** universal hierarchy; 5 levels in fixed order; deficiency + growth needs; innate
- **McClelland:** 3 needs (achievement, affiliation, power); no fixed order; one is dominant; **learned** through experience and culture
- **Similarity:** both identify core human needs that drive workplace motivation
- **Difference:** Maslow = fixed progression; McClelland = dominant need varies per person
- **Difference:** Maslow = can be applied to all of life; McClelland = specifically designed for the workplace
- Both criticised for **cultural bias** (Western, individualistic origins)

## SECTION 1: Universalist Theories of Leadership

> **TLDR**
> - Universalist theories = leaders have fixed traits that work in ANY situation
> - Three types: Great Person, Charismatic, Transformational
> - All are **nature-based** (born not made) and **reductionist**
> - Weakness: they ignore situational factors
> - Strength: some real-life support + can use psychometric testing to find leaders

---

### Great Person Theory

- **Who:** Thomas Carlyle (1841)
- **What:** Leaders are **born, not made**. They have inherited traits/characteristics. Greatest leaders arise when there is the greatest need.
- **Why it matters:** Simplest universalist theory; good for contrasting with behavioural theories

**Strength:** Has real-life support -- great leaders often emerge without training and even without wanting to lead, suggesting a genetic basis is valid.

**Weakness:** Only considers **nature**, not nurture. Cannot explain why some people with leadership traits never become leaders.

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Nature vs nurture debate -- Great Person Theory is a classic example of a pure nature argument.

---

### Charismatic Leadership Theory

- **Who:** Universalist theorists (no single named creator in the text)
- **What:** Leaders share specific traits: **charisma, popularity, environmental sensitivity, good communication**. Followers **respect and adore** the charismatic leader. They set high standards for themselves AND others.

**Strength:** Can be studied **quantitatively**. **Cicero et al. (2007)** found a positive correlation between charismatic leadership and work productivity using a field study with questionnaires.

**Weakness:** Focuses purely on **individual factors**, ignores situational factors like environment or social class -- so it is not a complete theory.

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Application to everyday life -- employers can use **psychometric testing** to identify charismatic/great person traits during recruitment. This applies to BOTH charismatic and great person theory.

---

### Transformational Leadership Theory

- **Who:** **Warrilow (2012)** described four characteristics
- **What:** Leaders **inspire and stimulate** followers to achieve more. They become role models and tie followers' identity to the group/organisation.

**Warrilow's 4 characteristics (mnemonic: I-I-I-P):**

| # | Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Idealised influence** | Leader is admirable, has clear values, is a role model |
| 2 | **Inspirational motivation** | Has a compelling vision, optimistic about goals |
| 3 | **Intellectual stimulation** | Challenges followers, encourages creativity |
| 4 | **Personal/individual attention** | Mentors individuals, respects contributions, helps growth |

**Strength:** Research support -- **Rubin et al. (2005)** found transformational leaders were evaluated as highly effective with increased productivity. High validity.

**Weakness:** Assumes this leadership always works. **Stevens et al. (1985)** found situational variables can make it cause **emotional stress, burnout and work conflict**. Could be unethical if promoted despite harm.

> 🔑 **Key Evaluation Point for ALL Universalist Theories:**
> All three are **reductionist** -- they reduce leadership to traits/characteristics, ignoring the role of learning and situational factors like the workplace environment.

## SECTION 2: Behavioural Theories of Leadership

> **TLDR**
> - Behavioural theories = leadership is **learned** (nurture, not nature)
> - Two studies: **Ohio University** and **Michigan University** (both late 1940s)
> - Both used surveys of leaders AND followers
> - Ohio found: **initiating** vs **consideration** behaviours
> - Michigan found: **production orientation** vs **employee orientation**
> - Key difference from universalist: leaders can be TRAINED

---

### Ohio University Behavioural Explanations (1948)

- **Who:** Researchers at Ohio State University (1948)
- **What:** Started with **over 1800 items** describing potential leadership behaviours. From responses, created the **LBDQ** (Leader Behaviour Description Questionnaire) -- 150 statements, five-point rating scale (never to always). Completed by workers in military, education, and industry. Found **two clusters** of leadership behaviour.

| Behaviour Type | Focus | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating (structural)** | Task-related | Setting expectations, feedback, performance standards, targets, coordinating tasks |
| **Consideration (nurturing)** | People-related | Listening, treating as equals, being friendly/supportive, empathy, building trust, active listening, finding areas for growth |

- These are **independent** -- you can be high or low on BOTH
- Studies found **initiating behaviours** were more effective for leadership
- **Warning:** Extreme initiating behaviour can lead to the leader making ALL decisions, leaving workers with NO autonomy, and punishing those who don't meet expectations

**Strength:** Quantitative methodology (five-point scales) allows objective, reliable, scientific analysis.

**Weakness:** **Reductionist** -- only looks at learned behaviours, ignores inherited aspects. Leadership is likely a combination of both.

---

### Michigan University Behavioural Explanations

- **Who:** Researchers at Michigan University
- **What:** Used interviews then questionnaires. Found two types of leadership behaviour.

| Behaviour Type | Focus | Similar to (Ohio) |
|---|---|---|
| **Employee orientation** | Valuing people as individuals, personal needs | Consideration behaviours |
| **Production orientation** | Technical/production elements, workers as means to an end | Initiating behaviours |

- Initially saw these as **opposite ends of one spectrum** (unlike Ohio)
- Later agreed with Ohio: two **independent** dimensions
- Found **employee orientation** was MORE effective (opposite to Ohio's finding!)

**Strength:** Application to everyday life -- leaders can be **trained** to use more effective (employee-oriented) behaviours. Unlike universalist theories, this is practical and useful.

**Weakness:** Ignores **situational factors**. Which behaviour is effective may depend on task type, workforce personality, and worker experience.

> 🔑 **Nature vs Nurture (Issues & Debates for Behavioural Theories):**
> Both Michigan and Ohio concentrate on characteristics but believe they can be **learned** (nurture). The likely truth: **neither side alone is correct** -- aspects of personality (nature) make you more likely to be a good leader, but these can be enhanced through **training and the right environment** (nurture).

> ⚡ **Must-Remember Comparison:**

| Feature | Ohio University | Michigan University |
|---|---|---|
| Two clusters | Initiating vs Consideration | Production vs Employee orientation |
| Relationship between clusters | Always independent | Initially opposite ends, later independent |
| Which is more effective? | Initiating (task) | Employee orientation (people) |
| Common weakness | Both ignore situational factors and are reductionist |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Nature vs nurture contrast -- Universalist = nature, Behavioural = nurture. Likely answer is BOTH matter. Behavioural theories can be applied to real life (training leaders) which universalist cannot easily do.

---

## SECTION 3: Heifetz (1997) -- Adaptive Leadership

> **TLDR**
> - **Heifetz & Laurie (1997)** -- six principles for leading adaptive work
> - Adaptive challenges = when old values/practices stop working and change is needed
> - Leaders must mobilise the workforce, not solve everything alone
> - Strength: considers **situational factors** (unlike universalist theories)
> - Weakness: reductionist + may not generalise across cultures

---

### What Are Adaptive Challenges?

- When beliefs are challenged and old values no longer work
- Competing values and perspectives emerge
- E.g., a shift pattern becomes ineffective, company must restructure

**Two reasons adaptive work is hard for leaders:**
1. Leaders must accept that **the solution is in the workforce**, not just in them
2. Change is **distressing** -- workers want leaders to take all responsibility, but this must be unlearned

---

### Heifetz's 6 Principles (mnemonic: **BigIRMGP** -- "**B**alcony, **I**dentify, **R**egulate, **M**aintain, **G**ive back, **P**rotect")

| # | Principle | Key Idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Get on the balcony** | Step back for an objective overview; spot power struggles, avoidance, resistance |
| 2 | **Identify the adaptive challenge** | Spot threats fast, respond effectively (e.g., British Airways fixed "Bloody Awful" reputation) |
| 3 | **Regulate distress** | Change at a steady pace -- not too fast (overwhelm) or too slow (no incentive). Three sub-tasks: **(a)** Create a **holding environment** for workers to express views, **(b)** Shape norms, manage conflict, maintain rate of change -- don't change too much too soon, **(c)** Leader must have a **presence** among the workforce -- understand pain of change while maintaining the right amount of tension |
| 4 | **Maintain disciplined attention** | Confront diverse views, don't scapegoat, encourage debate leading to collaboration |
| 5 | **Give the work back to employees** | Workers must take responsibility; leaders support, not control |
| 6 | **Protect voices from below** | Protect **whistleblowers** and **creative deviants** -- their perspectives prevent serious conflicts and bring fresh approaches |

---

### Evaluating Heifetz

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| **Application to real life** -- British Airways used it to improve customer satisfaction and profitability | **Reductionist** -- breaks leadership into components, ignoring personalities of workers and type/size of business |
| **Takes situational factors into account** -- recognises workplaces are dynamic and leaders must adapt | **May not generalise across cultures** -- based on Western representation of the workforce |

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Heifetz is great for the individual vs situational debate -- it is one of the few leadership theories that emphasises situational factors. Contrast it with universalist theories.

## SECTION 4: Leadership Style

> **TLDR**
> - **Muczyk & Reimann (1987)** -- four styles based on direction x participation
> - **Scouller (2011)** -- Three Levels (3Ps): Public, Private, Personal
> - Both consider situational factors more than universalist theories
> - Muczyk & Reimann uniquely accounts for **cultural differences**

---

### Muczyk & Reimann (1987) -- Four Styles of Leader Behaviour

- **Who:** Muczyk & Reimann (1987)
- **What:** Five dimensions of leadership, but two are **situation-dependent**: **participation** (worker involvement in decisions) and **direction** (control over execution)

| Style | Participation | Direction | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Directive autocrat** | None | High monitoring | Crisis, inexperienced workers, broken relationships |
| **Permissive autocrat** | None | Low monitoring | Quick decisions needed + experienced workers or structured tasks; policies/procedures/incentives substitute for direct control |
| **Directive democrat** | High | High monitoring | Complex tasks, speed not critical |
| **Permissive democrat** | High | Low monitoring | Experienced, motivated workers with good policies in place |

- Organisations often move gradually from **directive autocrat to permissive democrat** as they mature
- Culture matters: autocratic/directive leadership may be more appropriate outside US/Western democracies

**Strength:** Takes **cultural differences** into account -- does not assume American-style democratic leadership is always best. Can be applied across cultures.

**Weakness:** **Reductionist** -- reduces leadership to just four styles. Other theories suggest a wider variety of behaviours exist, so it is not a complete theory.

**Individual vs Situational (Issues & Debates):** Leaders decide how much worker participation and how much direction to give **depending on the situation**. Different cultures may need different leadership at different times -- many aspects of leadership are based on the **situation** rather than individual characteristics.

> ⚡ **Must-Remember:** Muczyk & Reimann argue against a "one size fits all" universal theory. The RIGHT style depends on the SITUATION (crisis vs stable, experienced vs inexperienced workers, culture).

---

### Scouller (2011) -- Three Levels of Leadership (3Ps)

- **Who:** **Scouller (2011)**
- **What:** Leadership is **learned, not inherited**. Leaders must work on three levels simultaneously. It is an **integrated model** (combines trait, behavioural, situational, and functional theories).

**Four dimensions a leader must address:**
1. Motivating purpose
2. Task progress results
3. Group unity and spirit
4. Individual effectiveness

**The 3Ps:**

| Level | Focus | Key Behaviours |
|---|---|---|
| **Public** (outer) | Groups -- dimensions 1, 2, 3 | Setting vision, ensuring unity, achieving group task, building trust, creating peer pressure for high standards (34 behaviours in 5 categories) |
| **Private** (outer) | Individuals -- dimensions 2, 4 | One-on-one handling: appraisal, goal-setting, relationship building, assessing talents (14 behaviours in 2 categories) |
| **Personal** (inner) | Self -- all 4 dimensions | **Self-mastery**, emotional intelligence, technical skills, attitude towards others. Most influential level. |

**Strength:** More **holistic** than other theories -- includes elements of trait, behavioural, AND situational theories. A more complete theory of leadership.

**Weakness:** Based on a **Western point of view** -- concepts like self-mastery, appraisal, and goal-setting reflect Western values. May be **culturally biased** and not applicable to all cultures/types of work.

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** Scouller is the go-to theory for the **reductionism vs holism** debate -- it is more holistic than most other leadership theories. Compare it with universalist theories (reductionist).

## SECTION 5: Key Study -- Cuadrado et al. (2008)

> **TLDR**
> - Tested **role congruity theory** -- are women evaluated worse when using "male" leadership styles?
> - Result: NO -- female leaders were NOT rated worse for using male styles
> - Stereotypically **female styles** (democratic, relationship-oriented) were rated BETTER regardless of leader's sex
> - Male evaluators did NOT favour male leaders

---

### Context

- **Eagly & Johnson (1990):** Leadership studies let researchers see whether leadership is **gender-stereotypical** (males = autocratic/task-oriented, females = democratic/relationship-oriented)
- **Role congruity theory:** People are evaluated less favourably when performing tasks **incongruent** with their gender
- Leadership roles are typically male sex-typed, so women face prejudice
- **Eagly et al. (1992)** meta-analysis found:
  - Female leaders got **worse evaluations only from male evaluators** when adopting male styles
  - Men did **NOT** get worse evaluations when adopting female styles
  - Men seen as more effective at **male-typed tasks**; women more effective at **female-typed tasks**
  - Male evaluators favoured men more; **female evaluators showed no preference**

---

### The Study

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Aim** | Test role congruity theory: are women evaluated worse when using stereotypically male leadership styles? |
| **Design** | Experimental, 4 conditions |
| **Sample** | 136 second-year psychology students, Spain (53% women, 47% men, mean age 29) |
| **Method** | Participants read a **narrative** about a supervisor (on a trial period) in a hospital **emergency service** (chosen as a **gender-neutral** setting -- has doctors [masculine] and nurses [feminine]), then completed anonymous questionnaires |

**Four conditions:**
1. Female leader + male style (autocratic/task-oriented)
2. Male leader + male style
3. Female leader + female style (democratic/relationship-oriented)
4. Male leader + female style

**Three dependent variables (all 7-point rating scales):**
1. Adjective list (7 positive + 7 negative)
2. Leadership capacity
3. Leadership effectiveness

A **pilot study** (40 participants) was conducted first.

---

### Results

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Hypothesis 1** (female leaders rated worse for male styles) | **NOT supported** -- both sexes rated similarly regardless of style |
| **Hypothesis 2** (males NOT rated worse for female styles) | **Supported** -- no difference |
| **Hypothesis 3** (females rated worse by male evaluators) | **NOT supported** -- evaluations were virtually identical |
| **Hypothesis 4** (males get similar evaluations from both sexes) | **NOT supported** -- same as H3, no difference at all |

> 🔑 **Key finding:** Stereotypically **female leadership styles** were rated significantly MORE favourably than male styles, regardless of the leader's sex.

---

### Conclusions

- Female leaders are **NOT** evaluated less favourably than males when using male styles
- Males do **NOT** favour leaders of their own sex
- **Female leadership styles (democratic, relationship-oriented) are more valued** in modern organisations

---

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| **Quantitative data** from rating scales -- objective, reliable | **Artificial task** -- reading a narrative does not reflect real workplace dynamics, low validity |
| **Standardised procedure** -- same narrative, same measures, easy to replicate | **Sample not representative** -- psychology students from one Spanish university, may know theory; results may not replicate across cultures |
| **Application to everyday life** -- training can promote female leadership styles | **Nomothetic** -- no insight into WHY participants gave their ratings |
| Pilot study increased validity | **Reductionist** -- reduced leadership to rating scales and adjectives |

**Ethics:**
- Data remained **confidential** (but knowing they were all 2nd-year psych students could make them more identifiable)
- **No harm** -- narrative task, not real-life, no consequences for any actual manager/worker
- **Right to withdraw** concern: participants received credit for completion, so some may have felt **pressured to continue**

> 📝 **Exam Bait:** This study is perfect for discussing: (1) gender and leadership, (2) role congruity theory, (3) methodological issues (ecological validity of narratives), (4) generalisability (Spanish students only), (5) idiographic vs nomothetic debate.

## PART 2 — QUICK-FIRE Q&A

**Q1: What are universalist theories of leadership?**
- Theories that search for a set of traits/characteristics held by effective leaders
- Argue these traits make leaders effective in ANY situation
- Examples: Great Person, Charismatic, Transformational

**Q2: Outline one difference between great person and charismatic theories.**
- Great person theory says leaders are born with inherited, almost divine traits
- Charismatic theory identifies specific measurable traits (charisma, popularity, communication) that can be studied quantitatively

**Q3: Describe the two types of behaviour found in the Ohio University studies.**
- **Initiating (structural):** task-related -- setting expectations, performance standards, coordinating work
- **Consideration (nurturing):** people-related -- listening, treating as equals, empathy, building trust
- These are independent; a leader can be high or low on both

**Q4: How does Heifetz's model differ from universalist theories?**
- Heifetz takes **situational factors** into account (workplaces are dynamic, challenges change)
- Universalist theories only look at individual characteristics/traits
- Heifetz says the solution lies in the **workforce**, not just the leader

**Q5: Explain Muczyk & Reimann's directive autocrat style.**
- No employee participation in decision-making + high monitoring of execution
- Used in crises, with inexperienced workers, or when leader-worker relationships have broken down
- The leader must still treat workers with respect

**Q6: Why is Scouller's model considered more holistic?**
- It integrates elements of trait, behavioural, situational, AND functional theories
- Unlike reductionist theories that focus on one aspect, it addresses public, private, and personal levels simultaneously

**Q7: Describe the procedure of Cuadrado et al. (2008).**
- 136 Spanish psychology students randomly assigned to 4 conditions
- Read a narrative about a hospital supervisor (manipulated: leader sex x leadership style)
- Completed anonymous questionnaires measuring: adjective ratings, leadership capacity, leadership effectiveness (all 7-point scales)

**Q8: What did Cuadrado et al. (2008) find?**
- **Female leadership styles** (democratic, relationship-oriented) were rated more favourably than male styles
- This was true regardless of the leader's actual sex
- No evidence that male evaluators favoured male leaders

**Q9: Give one strength and one weakness of Cuadrado et al. (2008).**
- **Strength:** Standardised procedure (same narrative, same measures) makes it replicable and reliable
- **Weakness:** Low ecological validity -- reading a narrative is artificial and does not capture real workplace dynamics

**Q10: Evaluate behavioural theories using the nature vs nurture debate.**
- Behavioural theories are on the **nurture** side (leadership can be learned)
- This contrasts with universalist theories (nature side)
- The truth is likely a combination: innate qualities (nature) make someone more likely to lead, but training and environment (nurture) enhance effectiveness

## KOUZES & POSNER - Leadership Practices Inventory (LPI)

> **TLDR**
> - **Kouzes & Posner** studied leadership over 30 years using case studies, interviews & surveys
> - They identified **5 practices** of exemplary leadership
> - The **LPI** is a psychometric questionnaire (30 items, 10-point scale) measuring those 5 practices
> - Has both a **self** version and an **observer** version (up to 10 raters)
> - Weakness: social desirability bias; may not apply across cultures

### The Five Practices

| # | Practice | What it means (1 line) |
|---|----------|----------------------|
| 1 | **Model the Way** | Lead by example, be honest, stay humble |
| 2 | **Inspire a Shared Vision** | Paint a compelling future; credibility is key |
| 3 | **Challenge the Process** | Seek change, take risks, learn from trial & error |
| 4 | **Enable Others to Act** | Foster collaboration, empower workers to decide |
| 5 | **Encourage the Heart** | Recognise contributions, praise publicly & privately |

**Key details per practice (exam depth):**
- **Model the Way:** Be true to yourself, always tell the truth, do what you say. Remain humble - let workers get praise. "Becoming a leader begins when you come to understand who you are" (K&P 1997)
- **Inspire a Shared Vision:** **Credibility = single most important asset** a leader can have ("if you don't believe in the messenger you won't believe the message"). Be forward-looking.
- **Challenge the Process:** "Only challenge provides the opportunity for greatness" (K&P 1997). Leaders don't wait for change - they make it happen. Don't fear failure - trial & error. Can't do it without workers who turn ideas into action.
- **Encourage the Heart:** Recognise contributions both **publicly and privately**. Verbal praise costs nothing but means everything to the worker.

🔑 **Memory trick: "MICEE"** - Model, Inspire, Challenge, Enable, Encourage

### The LPI (Leadership Practices Inventory)

- **Psychometric** questionnaire, 30 forced-choice items
- **10-point rating scale** (1 = almost never, 5 = occasionally, 10 = almost always)
- 6 questions per practice
- **Two versions:** LPI Self (leader rates themselves) + LPI Observer (up to 10 others rate the leader)
- Results compared against average ratings of other people

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| Quantitative data = more objective & reliable | Psychometric = social desirability bias, lying, exaggeration |
| **Kouzes & Posner (1993):** 2876 managers, no gender bias, no social desirability, no significant org differences | Based on **Western** ideal of leadership |
| Self & observer rank order was identical | Significant cultural differences found in scores |
| Applicable across organisations | Statements may not consider language/culture differences |
| **Application to everyday life:** LPI can identify which practices a leader IS or ISN'T using, then improve (e.g. not enough "encourage the heart" = give more verbal praise) | Leader may know what test is looking for; observer may dislike leader and deliberately lie |

⚡ **Idiographic vs Nomothetic:** The LPI itself is **nomothetic** (psychometric questionnaire), BUT the five practices were originally formulated using **idiographic** methods (case studies + in-depth interviews). So the research used BOTH approaches.

📝 **Exam Bait:** Examiners love asking you to evaluate the LPI as a measurement tool. Always mention: social desirability, cultural differences, and the strength of having both self + observer versions.

## KELLEY (1988) - Followership Styles

> **TLDR**
> - **Kelley (1988)** shifted focus from leaders to **followers**
> - Two dimensions: **critical thinking** (independent vs dependent) and **participation** (active vs passive)
> - These create **5 followership styles**
> - **Exemplary** followers = best performers (high critical thinking + active)
> - Cultural limitation: may not generalise beyond Western cultures

### What is Followership?

- **Crossman & Crossman (2011):** "the ability to effectively follow the directives and support the efforts of a leader to maximise a structured organisation"
- **Wortman (cited in Crossman & Crossman, 2011):** more person-centred - about attaining individual goals by being influenced by a leader
- **General definition:** willingness to cooperate towards defined goals, showing teamwork, following a leader but playing an **active not passive** role

### Two Dimensions

1. **Independent critical thinking** vs dependent non-critical thinking - Do they question and analyse, or just accept?
2. **Active participation** vs passive participation - Do they act, or wait for others?

### Five Followership Styles

| Style | Critical Thinking | Participation | Key Feature |
|-------|:-:|:-:|-------------|
| **Passive** ("Sheep") | Low | Low | Do tasks then stop, no initiative |
| **Alienated** | High | Low | Cynical, negative, whisper on sidelines (often passed over for promotion) |
| **Pragmatic Survivor** | Mid | Mid | "Better safe than sorry", shifts style, early warning system |
| **Conformist** ("Yes People") | Low | High | Obedient, never question, bosses with low confidence like them |
| **Exemplary** | High | High | Most effective, risk-takers, problem-solvers, will challenge leaders |

🔑 **Memory trick: "SPACE"** - Sheep, Pragmatic, Alienated, Conformist, Exemplary

⚡ **Exemplary followers** can succeed without strong leadership. They don't need to become leaders and find fulfilment as followers. They don't see the leader as a hero, but as **a person who manages change**. Effective followers **manage themselves well, are committed to the organisation, build up their skills, and are courageous and honest.**

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Favara (2009):** 175 workers, 3 questionnaires - significant positive correlation between exemplary style and job satisfaction + performance | **Reductionist** - breaks followership into 5 types on 2 dimensions, ignores situational factors |
| First theory to focus on followers, not leaders = useful for organisations | **Oyetunji (2013):** Botswana lecturers self-reported - passive followers rated themselves as best performers (opposite of theory!) = cultural differences |
| **Application to everyday life:** Orgs can shift focus from seeking effective leaders to also seeking effective followers = increase morale + productivity | Followers may be a mix of types, not just one - doesn't consider situational aspects of a task or organisation |

📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare Kelley's followership with leadership theories. Examiners love the individual vs situational debate here - Kelley is more on the individual side (types of people) but ignores situational factors like Milgram showed.

## TUCKMAN & JENSEN (2010) - Stages of Group Development

> **TLDR**
> - **Tuckman (1965)** reviewed 55 articles and proposed 4 stages of group development
> - **Tuckman & Jensen (2010)** added a 5th stage (adjourning) after reviewing 22 more articles
> - Stages are **linear**: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning
> - Weakness: lacks empirical testing; stages may overlap in reality

### The Five Stages

| Stage | What Happens |
|-------|-------------|
| 1. **Forming** | Getting to know each other, uncertainty, seeking guidance, worrying about fitting in |
| 2. **Storming** | Most critical stage, conflict, personalities clash, subgroups form (led by strongest personalities), performance may drop. Must "storm through" conflicting ideas or **resentment may follow** |
| 3. **Norming** | Conflict resolves, unity, roles allocated, leaders confirmed. Risk: **dominant members take over**, quieter members reluctant to challenge. **Groupthink** if storming was skipped |
| 4. **Performing** | Team is organised & mature, cooperation strong, creativity & innovation high |
| 5. **Adjourning** | Goals accomplished, group disperses, may cause regret, formal "ceremony" may happen |

🔑 **Memory trick: "Furious Storms Never Prevent Awesome-ness"** - Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Dynamic model** - updated with new evidence (added 5th stage) | Lacks **empirical evidence** - Tuckman admitted more research needed |
| 22 articles broadly supported the model | Only **Runkel (1971)** specifically set out to empirically test it - mainly supported it but methodology was prone to **observer bias** |
| Two studies + a review identified **termination** as final stage = led to adding adjourning | Amended version based on mainly **theoretical articles** rather than empirical studies |
| Widely applicable to real teams | Linear stages may not reflect reality - stages can **overlap and be revisited** |

📝 **Exam Bait:** Validity is a key eval point here. The model is dynamic (strength) but splitting group behaviour into distinct linear stages may not be realistic (weakness).

## BELBIN - Nine Team Roles

> **TLDR**
> - **Belbin** observed teams in the 1970s and identified **9 team roles**
> - Found behaviour matters more than intelligence in teams
> - Measured using the **Belbin Self Perception Inventory** (forced-choice + observer statements)
> - Weakness: Western-based, self-reports prone to bias

### The Nine Roles

| Role | Strengths | Allowable Weaknesses |
|------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Plant** | Creative, innovative, solves complex problems | Focuses on own ideas, not group's |
| **Resource Investigator** | Enthusiastic, great communicator, develops contacts | Enthusiasm fades quickly |
| **Co-ordinator** | Spots talent, delegates well, trusting | May manipulate others |
| **Shaper** | Driven, competitive, decisive, overcomes obstacles | Argumentative, aggressive |
| **Monitor Evaluator** | Careful thinker, high critical thinking, deals in facts | Slow, overly critical, boring |
| **Implementer** | Reliable, efficient, systematic, disciplined | Rigid, resists new ways |
| **Teamworker** | Supportive, diplomatic, flexible, good listener | Indecisive in conflict |
| **Completer Finisher** | Attention to detail, high standards, accurate | Anxious, reluctant to trust others |
| **Specialist** | Expert knowledge, respected authority | Not a natural team player, stubborn |

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Nomothetic** - uses reliable, valid, scientific methods | Self-reports = social desirability bias |
| Uses both self-perception + observer statements (triangulation). Observer statements are **not fixed choices** and 6 responses are correlated to check validity | Forced-choice may not reflect real-life actions, don't allow responders to give reasons |
| Quantitative = objective & reliable data | May not apply to **collectivist** cultures that favour teamwork over individual roles |
| Observer statements check validity | Observer reports affected by external conflicts |

## GROUPTHINK - Janis (1971)

> **TLDR**
> - **Janis (1971):** Groupthink = desire for agreement overrides objective decision-making
> - Caused by: group norms, social identity, cohesiveness, group isolation, stress
> - **8 symptoms** of groupthink (invulnerability, rationale, morality, stereotypes, pressure, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, mindguards)
> - Real examples: **Challenger Disaster (1986)**, **Bay of Pigs (1961)**
> - 4 strategies to avoid it

### What Causes Groupthink?

- **Group norms** + strong **social identity** + **cohesiveness** suppress critical thinking
- **Group isolation** - no outside information
- **Conformity** increases as cohesiveness increases (counterintuitive!)
- **Stress** - crisis situations make it worse
- **Golkar (2013):** High stress + low self-esteem (from past failures) + low self-efficacy = more groupthink
- Also more likely if the decision creates a **moral dilemma**

### The 8 Symptoms

| Symptom | What it means |
|---------|--------------|
| **Invulnerability** | Illusion group can't fail, over-optimism, risk-taking |
| **Rationale** | Ignoring warnings, rationalising beliefs (e.g. before Pearl Harbor, warnings of Japanese attack were ignored) |
| **Morality** | Belief group has high morals, ignoring moral consequences |
| **Stereotypes** | Stereotypical views of outgroups |
| **Pressure** | Pressuring members who express doubt |
| **Self-censorship** | Avoiding expressing opinions that deviate from group |
| **Illusion of unanimity** | Assuming silence = agreement |
| **Mindguards** | People who guard the group from outside dissent |

🔑 **Memory trick: "I Run My Stuff Past Some Intelligent Minds"** - Invulnerability, Rationale, Morality, Stereotypes, Pressure, Self-censorship, Illusion of unanimity, Mindguards

### Real-Life Examples

**Challenger Disaster (1986):** Exploded 73 seconds after take-off
- Thiokol engineers had doubts about **temperature** at launch and said abort, but NASA managers claimed everyone agreed (illusion of unanimity)
- Engineers pressured to reverse doubts - already 3 failed launch attempts + their own company worried about losing NASA contracts (pressure)
- Experts not consulted for final decision-maker Jesse Moore (mindguards)
- Cohesive group who had worked together for years, engineers isolated from decisions (group isolation)

**Bay of Pigs (1961):** American soldiers tried to overthrow Cuban Government (Fidel Castro)
- Group made decisions to please President Kennedy (knew what he wanted)
- Used negative stereotypes of the "enemy"
- No openness to new information
- Result: disaster, nearly started a war with Russia

### 4 Strategies to Avoid Groupthink

| Strategy | But... (limitation) |
|----------|-------------------|
| Invite outside experts to challenge ideas | People may act differently, not be honest |
| Assign a "devil's advocate" role | Only works if group believes the person is genuine |
| Senior managers tell group they share responsibility for consequences | May lead to "safe" decisions rather than correct ones |
| Every member = critical evaluator, must air doubts | Decision-making takes longer, could cause dysfunctional conflict |

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| Applied to real life (Bay of Pigs, Challenger, Iraq War) = useful | **Aldag & Fuller (1993):** Support comes from retrospective case studies = bias + faulty memory |
| **Holistic** - considers both personality AND situation | Case studies = political decisions at highest level, not typical workplaces |
| Janis: conformity, cohesiveness, stress (situational) + Golkar: low self-esteem (individual) | All case studies from **individualist cultures** (USA) - may not apply to collectivist nations (decision-making may differ in collectivist cultures where group > individual) |
| Strategies can be implemented to prevent it | **Fuller & Aldag (1998):** Groupthink has become a populist model, talked about in news/social media - every faulty decision gets labelled groupthink, other factors ignored |

⚡ **Counter-argument on culture:** The process of group formation may be **universal**, and groupthink is still a popular and widely used theory across the world - so concerns about cultural generalisability may be misplaced.

📝 **Exam Bait:** Examiners LOVE groupthink. Know: the 8 symptoms, at least one real-life example with specific symptoms, AND strategies + their limitations. Also be ready to discuss individual vs situational debate.

## FORSYTH (2006) - Cognitive Limitations & Errors

> **TLDR**
> - **Forsyth (2006)** / **Kerr et al. (1992):** Group decisions go wrong due to cognitive biases
> - Three categories: **sins of commission**, **sins of omission**, **sins of imprecision**
> - **Confirmation bias** is stronger in groups than in individuals
> - Strategy: diverse group membership reduces confirmation bias
> - Weakness: reductionist, ignores stress/sleep deprivation

### Three Categories of Decisional Sin

**1. Sins of Commission** (using wrong/irrelevant info)
- **Belief perseverance** - relying on info already shown to be inaccurate
- **Sunk cost bias** - won't change course because of prior investment
- **Extra-evidentiary bias** - using info you were told to ignore
- **Hindsight bias** - overestimating accuracy of your prior knowledge

**2. Sins of Omission** (missing useful info)
- **Base rate bias** - failing to consider general trends
- **Fundamental attribution error** - blaming personality, ignoring situation (seen in individualist cultures, not collectivist)

**3. Sins of Imprecision** (oversimplifying)
- **Availability heuristic** - basing decisions on readily available info
- **Conjunction bias** - failing to recognise two events together are less probable than one alone
- **Representativeness heuristic** - relying on factors that seem logical but are misleading

⚡ **Confirmation bias** = not seeking info that contradicts your conclusion, only seeking info that confirms it. **Kerr et al. (1992):** This is STRONGER in groups than individuals.

🔑 **Memory trick for 3 sins: "COI"** - Commission (wrong info), Omission (missing info), Imprecision (sloppy shortcuts)

### Strategies to Avoid Cognitive Errors

- **Educate** yourself on cognitive errors so you can spot them
- To fix **fundamental attribution error:** actively think of situational factors, ask "would I have acted the same way?"
- Ensure group has **diversity** of opinion + leader who won't suppress challenge

### Strategies to Avoid Confirmation Bias

- Unanimous groups show strongest confirmation bias
- Groups with **at least 1 minority member** showed less bias
- Groups with **2 minority members** were better than individuals at overcoming it

### Evaluation

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|------------|
| **Osmani (2016):** 86% recommended project with sunk cost vs 26% without = supports cognitive bias in groups | **Reductionist** - ignores stress, sleep deprivation, other factors |
| Applicable to everyday life - helps form diverse groups + train members | Fundamental attribution error only in individualist cultures |
| Strategies can be implemented in real orgs | Not a complete theory of decision-making |

📝 **Exam Bait:** Compare groupthink vs cognitive errors. Both explain faulty decision-making but groupthink is more holistic (includes stress, personality, situation) while Forsyth is more reductionist (just cognitive biases).

## SOCIAL FACILITATION & SOCIAL LOAFING

> **TLDR**
> - **Social facilitation** = performing BETTER in presence of others (simple tasks)
> - **Social inhibition** = performing WORSE in presence of others (complex tasks)
> - Explained by **Drive Theory (Zajonc, 1966)** and **Evaluation Apprehension**
> - **Social loafing** = putting in less effort in a group
> - Explained by **Social Impact Theory (Latane, 1981)** and **diffusion of responsibility**

### Social Facilitation

- **Definition:** Tendency to perform better/faster in the presence of others
- **Triplett (1898):** Cyclists faster racing others than alone; 40 children turned reel quicker in pairs

**Two types of "presence":**
- **Co-action effect** - people working alongside you
- **Audience effect** - people watching you

### Drive Theory (Zajonc, 1966)

- Presence of others increases **arousal**
- Simple/well-known task + arousal = **better performance** (social facilitation)
- Complex/novel task + arousal = **worse performance** (social inhibition)
- The **dominant response** (habitual behaviour) kicks in - for easy tasks that's correct, for hard tasks that's often wrong
- Supported by **Yerkes-Dodson Law:** optimal arousal = peak performance; too much or too little = poor performance
- Individual differences matter: socially anxious people have higher baseline arousal = audience effects are greater

### Evaluation Apprehension

- Anxiety from worrying about being **judged negatively**
- We learn audiences may evaluate us, not just passively watch
- If confident + simple task = performance enhanced
- If not confident + complex task = performance inhibited
- More influential/experienced observers = higher arousal + apprehension

⚡ **Schmitt et al. (1986):** 45 undergraduates in 3 conditions (alone / blindfolded+headphones observer / evaluating experimenter). Tasks began **before participants realised the experiment had started** (reduces demand characteristics). Simple task (typing name) = faster with audience. Complex task (typing name backwards with ascending numbers between letters) = slower with audience. Most pronounced in evaluating condition. Supports BOTH evaluation apprehension AND mere presence effects.

### Social Loafing

- **Definition:** People put in less individual effort in a group
- **Ringelmann (1913):** Rope-pulling study (rope attached to pressure gauge) - more people = further below individual potential. Due to **coordination loss** (main reason) + **motivation loss**
- Groups still outperform individuals, but NOT at the rate expected if everyone gave 100%
- Social loafing becomes more likely if person is **not being individually evaluated** = more anonymous

### Social Impact Theory (Latane, 1981)

- Social influence depends on: **strength**, **immediacy**, and **number** of others
- In groups, influence is **diffused** across all members
- As group size increases, each person has less individual influence
- **Diffusion of responsibility** - less likely to take responsibility when others are present
- More anonymous = more loafing (no individual evaluation)

⚡ **Latane (1981):** Subjects stood in a **semi-circle**, clapped/shouted as loud as possible individually then with group. Noise increased with group size but **NOT proportionally**. Individuals made less noise as group got bigger.

📝 **Exam Bait:** Social facilitation vs social loafing is a classic comparison question. Facilitation = presence of others HELPS (simple tasks). Loafing = presence of others means you do LESS. Link drive theory to Yerkes-Dodson. Know Schmitt et al. and Ringelmann as evidence.

**Q: Describe Kelley's followership styles. [6]**

**Q: Describe the stages of group development. [6]**
- Forming: getting to know each other, uncertainty, seeking guidance
- Storming: conflict, personalities clash, most critical stage
- Norming: unity, roles allocated, risk of groupthink if storming skipped

- Adjourning: goals done, group disperses, may cause regret

**Q: Describe groupthink. [6]**
- Janis (1971): desire for agreement overrides objective appraisal
- Caused by: group norms, social identity, cohesiveness, group isolation, stress
- 8 symptoms: invulnerability, rationale, morality, stereotypes, pressure, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, mindguards

- Invite outside experts to challenge ideas (but people may not be honest with outsiders)
- Assign devil's advocate role (but only works if group believes person is genuine)

**Q: Explain social facilitation using drive theory. [4]**
- Zajonc (1966): presence of others increases arousal
- Simple task = dominant response is correct = performance improves
- Complex task = dominant response is often wrong = performance worsens
- Supported by Yerkes-Dodson optimal arousal theory

**Q: Explain social loafing using social impact theory. [4]**
- Latane (1981): influence diffused across group members
- Larger group = less individual influence = less effort per person
- Diffusion of responsibility: assume others "will get it done"
- More anonymous (no individual evaluation) = more loafing

# SECTION 1: Group Performance Across Cultures

> **TLDR**
> - Social loafing = doing less work in a group
> - **Earley (1989, 1993)**: collectivists DON'T loaf when in an **ingroup** but DO loaf with an **outgroup**
> - Individualists perform BETTER alone; collectivists perform BETTER with their ingroup
> - It's not just culture — it's about whether you **identify** with the group
> - Self-efficacy and anticipated rewards explain the difference

---

### Key Definitions

- **Social loafing** — reduced individual effort when placed in a group
- **Individualist culture** — values personal goals over group goals (e.g. USA)
- **Collectivist culture** — values group goals over personal goals (e.g. China)
- **Ingroup** — a group you identify with / feel part of
- **Outgroup** — a group you do NOT identify with
- **Efficacy** — belief in your ability to succeed
- **Cognitive bias** — assuming ingroup = similar to you, outgroup = different. Collectivists assume everyone in the ingroup will work better together; individualists assume everyone works better alone (because THEY prefer it)
- **Collective orientation** — placing group goals above self-interest
- **Self-interest motive** — believing group rewards should be based on individual contribution

---

### Earley (1989) — Culture and Social Loafing

- **WHO**: Earley
- **WHAT**: Studied US (individualist) and Chinese (collectivist) managers on a shared responsibility task
- **KEY FINDINGS**:
  - Social loafing WAS present in US managers but NOT in Chinese managers
  - Individualists performed worse in groups than alone
  - Collectivists performed better in groups than alone
  - Accountability did NOT affect loafing in collectivists — performance was HIGHEST in the highest shared responsibility groups
- **WHY it matters**: Shows social loafing is NOT universal — culture matters

#### Why the difference?

| Individualists | Collectivists |
|---|---|
| **Self-interest motive** | **Collective orientation** |
| Want rewards based on personal contribution | Place group goals above self-interest |
| If no one notices their work, they loaf | Take pride in group achievement |
| Group rewards feel unfair if not based on effort | High effort even without personal recognition |

---

### Earley's Process Model of Group Performance

- **Collectivists** feel more efficacy performing WITH a group (especially ingroup); **individualists** feel more efficacy performing ALONE
- Collectivists anticipate greater rewards + higher efficacy with an ingroup = perform better
- Individualists anticipate more rewards + higher efficacy alone = perform better alone than in ANY group
- The **cognitive bias** drives this: each assumes others share their preferences

---

### Earley (1993) — Testing the Process Model (Key Study)

- **WHO**: Earley
- **WHAT**: Extended the 1989 study — tested whether it's REALLY about culture or about ingroup vs outgroup

#### AIM

- Do collectivists perform worse with an **outgroup** than an **ingroup**?
- Do self-efficacy and anticipated rewards explain the effect?

#### METHOD

- **Sample**: 165 managers — 45 Israeli, 60 Chinese, 60 American (aged 25-40)
- **Design**: Lab experiment, 3 conditions:
  - Working **alone**
  - Working in a 10-person **outgroup** (differences emphasised)
  - Working in a 10-person **ingroup** (similarities emphasised)
- Participants were NOT in physical presence of their group — just TOLD they were part of one (they were in a room with other participants but told those were NOT their group members)
- **Task**: Simulated in-basket of 40 work items (memos, prioritising interviews, rating applications) in 60 minutes. Chosen because familiar to managers in their own workplaces.
- 5-minute practice trial first — output used to give participants an estimate of their expected performance
- **Measured**: Performance (correct items), individualism-collectivism scale (5-point), self-efficacy (0-100)

#### RESULTS

| | Alone | Outgroup | Ingroup |
|---|---|---|---|
| **China** (collectivist) | 18.49 | 18.37 | **23.83** |
| **USA** (individualist) | **24.55** | 16.21 | 16.57 |
| **Israel** (collectivist) | 20.79 | 18.63 | **23.18** |

- **Individualists** = best when ALONE
- **Collectivists** = best with INGROUP, worst with outgroup/alone
- Self-efficacy and anticipated performance **partially mediated** the effects of group condition and individualism-collectivism on performance
- Both group and individual self-efficacy were higher for collectivists (China, Israel) in ingroups than outgroups or alone

#### CONCLUSIONS

- Collectivists' efficacy increases with high group identification
- Collectivists CAN loaf — when working alone or with an outgroup
- **Type of group** (ingroup vs outgroup) may matter MORE than culture itself

📝 **Exam Bait**: Examiners love asking you to use the results table to explain findings. Practise reading the numbers.

---

### Evaluating Earley (1993)

**Strengths**:
- Used **quantitative data** (questionnaires, rating scales) — objective, replicable
- Extended 1989 study — showed "collectivists never loaf" was too simplistic
- More **holistic** — looked at interaction between culture, group type AND self-efficacy

**Weaknesses**:
- **Low ecological validity** — lab experiment, not a real workplace; strangers, not real ingroups
- **Nomothetic approach** — group averages hide individual reasons; an idiographic approach (case studies, interviews) would show WHY individuals behaved that way
- Earley himself questioned whether the artificial manipulation reflected real workplace dynamics

⚡ **Must-remember**: The key takeaway is that it's NOT just individualism vs collectivism — it's about **identification with the group**.

# SECTION 2: Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)

> **TLDR**
> - **Claypoole & Szalma (2019)**: Electronic monitoring IMPROVES performance on sustained attention tasks
> - Monitored participants detected more signals, made fewer errors, responded faster
> - Links to **social facilitation** — electronic "observer" works like a real one
> - BUT: constant monitoring could also cause stress and harm workers

---

### Key Definitions

- **Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)** — using technology (webcams, software) to track worker productivity
- **Vigilance** — sustained attention over an extended period
- **Evaluation apprehension** — anxiety caused by knowing you are being watched/judged

### Background Context

- Over 70% of US organisations use electronic systems to manage performance (APA, 2007)
- Research is mixed: EPM may improve OR impair performance depending on task difficulty — **Davidson & Henderson (2000)**
- **Aiello & Svec (1993)** found performance on a COMPLEX task was impaired by both human and electronic monitors vs working alone
- Gap in research: most EPM studies used simple tasks, NOT sustained attention tasks (driving, medical screening)

---

### Claypoole & Szalma (2019) — Key Study

- **WHO**: Claypoole & Szalma
- **WHAT**: Tested whether EPM produces social facilitation effects on a sustained attention task

#### AIM

- Does electronic presence improve performance on a vigilance task?

#### METHOD

- **Sample**: 106 undergraduates (65F, 41M, mean age 20.57, range 18-37) from a university in the south of the USA
- **Design**: Lab experiment, 2 conditions:
  - **Control** — no monitoring, no webcam, alone in room
  - **Electronic presence** — webcam on top of screen ("sends live feed to office next door") + video recorder behind screen ("records for later evaluation"). Nothing was actually recorded.
- Phones and watches surrendered at start
- 3-minute practice session first, then 24-minute vigilance task
- **Task**: Watch 2-digit numbers on screen (shown for 1000ms each). Press spacebar when numbers differ by 0 or +/-1 (e.g. 77, 65, 01). 5 critical signals per 6-minute period = 20 total. Task lasted no more than 1 hour.

#### RESULTS

| Measure | EPM Group | Control Group |
|---|---|---|
| Correct detections | **0.69** | 0.60 |
| False alarms | **0.006** (fewer = better) | 0.020 |
| Response time (ms) | **767** (faster) | 802 |

- EPM group was **better on ALL three measures**
- Performance declined over time in both groups (consistent with previous research)

#### CONCLUSIONS

- EPM can **improve** performance on boring but demanding sustained attention tasks
- Video-based monitoring is effective and less intrusive than other EPM forms

📝 **Exam Bait**: Examiners may ask you to link this to **evaluation apprehension** from social facilitation — the electronic monitor acts as an evaluator, improving simple/well-rehearsed task performance.

---

### Evaluating Claypoole & Szalma (2019)

**Strengths**:
- **Lab experiment** — standardised procedure, high control, only the monitor was manipulated = replicable
- **Quantitative data** — objective measures (response times, correct detections, false alarms), no experimenter bias

**Weaknesses**:
- **Unrepresentative sample** — undergrad students, not real workers; response bias from experiment website
- **Low task validity** — pressing spacebar for digit differences is NOT a real workplace task (driving, medical screening would be different)
- **Cultural differences** not considered — different countries have different levels of surveillance, different reactions to monitoring

**Ethics**:
- Confidentiality protected, informed consent gained, APA ethics approval
- **Deception** used — told recording was happening when it wasn't (but debriefed after)
- Credits for participation may have pressured students to stay
- Findings could lead to **constant workplace surveillance** = stress and harm

🔑 **Key Link**: **Bateson et al. (2006)** found even an image of EYES increased prosocial behaviour — people just need a CUE that they're being watched.

# SECTION 3: Conflict at Work

> **TLDR**
> - 4 levels: intra-individual, inter-individual, intra-group, inter-group
> - Causes = personality differences, competition for resources, poor communication, status differences
> - **Thomas-Kilmann**: 5 conflict-handling modes (competing, accommodating, avoiding, collaborating, compromising)
> - **Einarsen (1999)**: Bullying has 5 types, 4 phases, and 3 areas of causes
> - Conflict has BOTH individual and situational causes

---

### Why Conflict Happens

- Miscommunication between employees about goals and beliefs
- Belief that others are about to affect you negatively
- Three broad categories: **personal** (relationship breakdown), **task-related**, or **procedural** issues

---

### 4 Levels of Workplace Conflict

#### 1. Intra-individual conflict (WITHIN one person)

- Your goals/values clash with the organisation's
- Uncertainty about what's expected of you
- Feeling you're not good enough for the role
- Three types of goal conflict:
  - **Positive-positive**: choosing between two attractive options (two good job offers)
  - **Negative-negative**: choosing between two bad options (punishment OR hated task)
  - **Positive-negative**: one option has BOTH good and bad outcomes (great job, terrible location)

#### 2. Inter-individual conflict (BETWEEN two people)

- Disagreements between co-workers / managers
- Can be productive if handled well
- **Barki & Hartwick (2004)** — 3 components:
  - **Behavioural** (interference) — deliberately blocking someone's goals
  - **Cognitive** (disagreement) — different values/objectives
  - **Affective** (negative emotions) — anger, stress, frustration

#### 3. Intra-group conflict (WITHIN a single group)

- Between 2+ members of the SAME group
- Three types: **process** conflict (how to do it), **relationship** conflict (personality clashes), **task** conflict (what the task actually is)
- More likely with diverse backgrounds, ages, experience

#### 4. Inter-group conflict (BETWEEN different groups)

- Conflict between different teams/departments
- Can INCREASE group loyalty (positive) but also creates "us vs them" (negative)
- Linked to **social identity theory** — we exaggerate outgroup negatives, enhance ingroup positives
- Main cause: **competition for scarce resources** — linked to competitiveness, where the need for respect from others drives competition and conflict

⚡ **Memory trick for the 4 levels**: Think **"I-I-I-I"** = Intra-Individual, Inter-Individual, Intra-Group, Inter-Group. Goes from SMALLEST unit (inside one person) to LARGEST (between groups).

---

### Causes of Organisational Group Conflict — Miles (1980)

1. **Interdependence** — the more you HAVE to work together, the more conflict is likely
2. **Status inconsistencies** — unfair differences in time off, pay between groups
3. **Communication problems** — misunderstanding or misinterpreting each other
4. **Individual differences** — personality, skills, aggression, tolerance levels

**Other causes**:
- Lack of trust
- Scarce resources / budget competition
- Organisational changes (restructuring, redundancies, new team leaders)

### Causes of Interpersonal Conflict

- Competition for scarce resources (situational)
- Personality differences (individual)
- Clashes of values and interests
- Power and status differences (situational)
- Poor communication (most common cause)

📝 **Exam Bait**: The **individual vs situational debate** — conflict has BOTH types of causes. Changing the situation alone isn't enough; you also need to consider personality.

---

### Evaluating Conflict Explanations

- **Research support**: **Sherif (1962)** — boys at camp competing for prizes showed increased hostility, had to terminate study. Supports realistic conflict theory.
- **Application to real life**: Knowing causes helps organisations fix them (e.g. improve communication channels)
- **Cultural weakness**: Explanations are individualistic — status and power may not matter in collectivist cultures, reducing generalisability

---

### Thomas-Kilmann's 5 Conflict-Handling Modes

Two dimensions: **Assertiveness** (satisfying YOUR concerns) x **Cooperativeness** (satisfying THEIR concerns)

| Mode | Assertive? | Cooperative? | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competing** | High | Low | Win at other's expense; defend your position |
| **Accommodating** | Low | High | Give in; neglect own concerns for others |
| **Avoiding** | Low | Low | Dodge conflict entirely; don't deal with it |
| **Collaborating** | High | High | Work together to find a win-win solution |
| **Compromising** | Medium | Medium | Middle ground; both give something up |

- Measured using the **Thomas-Kilmann Instrument (TKI)** — forced-choice questionnaire

⚡ **Memory trick**: **"C-A-A-C-C"** or think of a **2x2 grid** with assertiveness on one axis, cooperativeness on the other. Compromising sits in the MIDDLE.

---

### Evaluating Thomas-Kilmann

**Weakness**:
- TKI uses **forced-choice** answers — subject to **social desirability bias**
- Person may not agree with either option but must pick one — lowers validity

**Strength — Application**:
- Helps people understand their default style AND consider adapting to different situations
- BUT: assumes style can be LEARNED — it may be a fixed personality feature that's much harder to change than just learning about it

**Point**: Each person can use all 5 modes, but many are better at some than others due to personality or situational pressures

**Weakness — Nomothetic**:
- Assumes ALL conflict behaviour fits on 2 dimensions and 5 categories
- An idiographic approach (case studies, interviews) might reveal more nuanced responses

---

### Einarsen (1999) — Bullying at Work (Key Study)

- **WHO**: Einarsen
- **WHAT**: Review article summarising research on the nature, phases and causes of workplace bullying

#### 5 Types of Bullying

1. **Work-related** — changing/making tasks harder
2. **Social isolation**
3. **Personal attacks / ridicule** — insults, gossip, attacking private life
4. **Verbal threats** — criticised, yelled at, humiliated publicly
5. **Physical violence** or threats (rare — only ~2.4% of cases in Norwegian studies)

🔑 **Key point**: Most bullying is VERBAL, not physical.

#### 4 Phases of Bullying

1. **Aggressive behaviour** — subtle, indirect aggression toward someone
2. **Bullying** — aggression becomes more open, direct, frequent; victim less able to defend themselves
3. **Stigmatisation** — social stigma placed on victim; focus on their perceived flaws (low confidence, anxiety)
4. **Trauma** — severe impact on victim's physical and mental health

⚡ **Memory trick**: **"A-B-S-T"** = Aggression, Bullying, Stigmatisation, Trauma. Think: "ABST-ract cruelty that gets worse."

#### 3 Areas of Causes

- **Seign (1996)**: Interview study of 30 Irish victims — ALL blamed the personality of the bully

| The Bully | The Victim | The Workplace |
|---|---|---|
| Competition for status | Overachievers with unrealistic self-beliefs | Deficiencies in work design |
| Feelings of envy | Highly rigid | Deficiencies in leadership |
| Uncertainty about themselves | See themselves as more honest/punctual (annoying to others) | Socially exposed position of victim |
| (Bjorkqvist et al., 1994) | | Low moral standards in department |

#### CONCLUSION

- More empirical research needed — must focus on feelings of the person being victimised
- Need to distinguish between different types of bullying: **predatory** vs **dispute-related** bullying

---

### Evaluating Einarsen (1999)

**Weaknesses**:
- **Review article** — uses secondary sources, so you don't know if original research was robust = lower reliability
- **Victim-blaming** — suggesting victim personality is partly at fault is ethically problematic and could cause further psychological harm

**Strengths**:
- **Holistic** — considers individual factors (personality of bully AND victim) AND situational factors (workplace leadership, design, morality)
- **Application to real life** — identifying the 4 phases means early signs can be spotted sooner, reducing damage

📝 **Exam Bait**: Examiners LOVE the **reductionism vs holism** debate here. Einarsen is HOLISTIC because he considers multiple factors (individual + situational).

## PART 4 — QUICK-FIRE Q&A

**Q: Outline one difference between an individualist and collectivist society. [2]**
- In an **individualist** society, people prioritise personal goals over group goals. In a **collectivist** society, people prioritise group goals and cooperation over self-interest.

**Q: Using Earley (1993), explain how culture affects group performance. [4]**
- **Earley (1993)** found that **collectivists** (China, Israel) performed significantly better in an **ingroup** (e.g. Chinese mean = 23.83) than alone (18.49) or in an outgroup (18.37).
- **Individualists** (USA) performed best **alone** (24.55) and worst in groups (outgroup 16.21, ingroup 16.57).
- This is because collectivists have a **collective orientation** where group goals are valued, while individualists have a **self-interest motive** and prefer rewards based on personal contribution.

- **Strength**: Quantitative data from structured questionnaires = objective, replicable

- **Weakness**: Lab experiment with strangers, not real workplaces — low ecological validity
- **Weakness**: Nomothetic approach — group averages hide individual differences; idiographic methods like case studies would show WHY people behaved as they did
- **Debate**: Cultural differences — most social loafing research uses individualist samples; Earley included collectivist cultures, making findings more generalisable

**Q: Describe the study by Claypoole & Szalma (2019). [6]**
- **Aim**: Test whether EPM improves performance on a sustained attention task
- **Method**: Lab experiment; 106 undergrads; control (alone) vs electronic presence (webcam + video recorder). 24-min vigilance task detecting critical 2-digit signals.
- **Results**: EPM group had more correct detections (0.69 vs 0.60), fewer false alarms (0.006 vs 0.020), and faster response times (767ms vs 802ms).

**Q: Describe the four levels of workplace conflict. [4-6]**
- **Intra-individual**: conflict within one person (e.g. your values clash with the organisation's goals)
- **Inter-individual**: conflict between two people (e.g. co-workers disagreeing over resources)
- **Intra-group**: conflict within the same group (e.g. disagreements on how to complete a task)
- **Inter-group**: conflict between different groups/teams (e.g. departments competing for budget), linked to social identity theory

**Q: Outline Thomas-Kilmann's five conflict-handling modes. [6]**
- **Competing**: assertive + uncooperative — pursue own goals at other's expense
- **Accommodating**: unassertive + cooperative — give up own concerns to satisfy others
- **Avoiding**: unassertive + uncooperative — dodge the conflict entirely
- **Collaborating**: assertive + cooperative — work together for a win-win solution
- **Compromising**: moderate on both — find a middle-ground solution

**Q: Describe Einarsen's (1999) findings on bullying. [6]**
- **Types**: mostly verbal (social isolation, ridicule, insults); physical violence rare (~2.4%)
- **Phases**: starts with subtle aggression, escalates to open bullying, then stigmatisation of the victim, finally trauma affecting physical and mental health
- **Causes**: the bully (envy, status competition, insecurity), the victim (overachievers, rigid, seen as annoying), the workplace (poor leadership, work design flaws, low moral standards)

**Q: Evaluate Einarsen (1999) with reference to reductionism vs holism. [4]**
- Einarsen's findings are **holistic** because they consider BOTH individual factors (personality of bully and victim) AND situational factors (workplace leadership, design, morality).
- This is a **strength** because it gives a more complete explanation and acknowledges that reducing bullying requires addressing workplace issues, not just blaming individuals.
- However, using a **review article** methodology means the original studies' robustness is unknown, lowering reliability.

**Q: Discuss the individual vs situational debate in relation to conflict at work. [4]**
- **Individual factors**: personality differences (one person aggressive, another shy), the victim being an overachiever
- **Situational factors**: poor communication, organisational changes, competition for scarce resources, deficiencies in leadership
- This shows that just changing the situation (e.g. improving communication) may not be enough — you also need to consider workers' personalities
- A complete explanation of conflict needs BOTH perspectives
