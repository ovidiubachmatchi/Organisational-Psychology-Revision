Question 13 [4 marks]
Ulrich manages an office where there is some conflict between the workers. Ulrich has read that a more stereotypically feminine leadership style may help him to manage his workers better. Suggest how Ulrich could change his leadership style to one that is more stereotypically feminine.

Ulrich could focus more on building relationships with his workers rather than just giving orders. Question 14 [2+2 marks]
(a) Outline what is meant by intrinsic motivators at work.
(b) Explain why intrinsic motivators at work can differ between cultures.

(a) Intrinsic motivators are things that come from within the person rather than external rewards like money. They include things like enjoying the work itself and feeling a sense of achievement or personal growth from doing the job.

(b) Intrinsic motivators can differ between cultures because what people value at work depends on the culture they come from. In individualist cultures people might be more motivated by personal achievement and recognition but in collectivist cultures people might find more motivation from contributing to the group and feeling like they belong to the team. So what actually motivates people depends on their cultural values.


Question 15 [4+2 marks]
Tayyibah is the manager of a small factory making fabric. Tayyibah has noticed that her workers do not seem to be motivated although they receive good wages. She thinks that using Maslow's hierarchy of needs could help.
(a) Suggest two ways in which Tayyibah could use Maslow's hierarchy of needs to motivate her workers.
(b) Explain one weakness of Maslow's hierarchy of needs.

(a) Since the workers already receive good wages their basic physiological and safety needs are probably already met. Tayyibah could focus on the higher levels of the hierarchy. Tayyibah could try to meet social needs by creating a better sense of community in the factory like organising team activities or making sure workers feel included. She could also focus on esteem needs by recognising and praising workers for good work so they feel valued and respected rather than just being paid.

(b) One weakness of Maslow is that the hierarchy assumes everyone goes through the needs in the same fixed order but this is not always the case. Some people might prioritise social belonging over safety or esteem over everything else depending on their personality and circumstances. the model is too rigid to account for individual differences in what motivates people.


Question 16 [6+10 marks]
(a) Describe what psychologists have discovered about group development and decision-making: stages of group development, and Belbin's nine team roles.
(b) Evaluate what psychologists have discovered about group development and decision-making: stages of group development, and Belbin's nine team roles, including a discussion about application to everyday life.

(a) Tuckman studied group development and identified stages that groups go through. Forming is when the group first comes together and members are uncertain about their role and try to figure out what is acceptable. after that comes storming where conflict starts because different personalities come out and people disagree about things. once the conflict gets resolved the group moves into norming and they agree on how to work together and roles become settled. Performing is where the group actually works well together as a team. Tuckman and Jensen later added adjourning for when the group finishes and breaks apart.

Belbin identified nine team roles that people tend to take on in groups. These include the coordinator who organises the team and the plant who comes up with creative ideas. the shaper is the one who pushes things forward and the implementer gets things done practically. There are also team workers who keep the group together and the monitor evaluator who judges ideas carefully. Resource investigators bring in outside contacts and specialists provide expert knowledge and completers make sure things are finished properly.

(b) Tuckman model is useful because it gives a clear structure for understanding how groups develop over time. It has been updated too since Jensen helped add a fifth stage based on reviewing more articles which shows the model is still being developed. However one weakness is that it assumes groups go through the stages in a fixed order which might not always happen in real life because stages can overlap or be revisited. Tuckman also admitted that the model lacked empirical testing and was mostly based on theoretical articles.

Belbin is useful for application to everyday life because organisations can use the team roles to build better teams. If a manager knows what roles are missing in a team they can recruit someone to fill that gap. For example if a team has no plant they might struggle with creativity so they could bring in someone who fits that role. This has practical value in workplaces and is already used by many companies.

However Belbin has weaknesses too. The self-perception inventory that Belbin uses to identify roles relies on people being honest about themselves which might not always be accurate. People might also take on different roles in different situations so the roles are not necessarily fixed. In terms of application to everyday life both theories are widely used in workplaces for team building and management training which shows they do have practical value. the fact that they simplify group behaviour into categories or stages means they might miss the complexity of how real groups actually work.

Question 7 [4+2+4 marks]
The key study by Landry et al. (2019) on applying self-determination theory to motivational rewards included a 'manipulation check' as part of the procedure in study 1.
(a) Describe the conditions of the independent variable used in study 1.
(b) Suggest one effect on the results if a manipulation check had not been included in study 1.
(c) Explain one strength and one weakness of the experimental design as used in study 1.

(a) The independent variable had two conditions that participants were randomly assigned to. In the autonomy-supportive condition participants read an instruction paragraph that used encouraging and supportive language and the reward was described as a token of appreciation for their contribution. this gave the reward an informational meaning. In the controlling condition the language was more pressurising and the reward was described as being used to reinforce performance standards which gave it a controlling meaning. Both groups received the same actual reward which was a gift card but the way it was presented to them was different.

(b) Without a manipulation check the researchers could not be sure that participants actually saw the two conditions as different. if some participants did not notice the difference in how the reward was framed then the IV might not have worked as intended and the results would be less valid because any differences in motivation might not actually be caused by the framing.

(c) One strength of the independent measures design is that participants only experience one condition so there are no order effects or demand characteristics from doing both. if participants did both conditions they might figure out the study is about how rewards are framed and change their behaviour which would affect the results.

One weakness is that there are different people in each condition which means individual differences between the groups could still affect the results. even though random assignment was used it is still possible that one group happened to have more naturally motivated participants than the other which would make it harder to be sure the differences were caused by the framing of the reward.


Question 8 [2+2+4 marks]
Muczyk and Reimann proposed four styles of leader behaviour.
(a) Outline the 'directive autocrat' style of leader behaviour proposed by Muczyk and Reimann.
(b) Suggest one closed question that a leader could be asked to investigate whether they have a directive autocrat style of leader behaviour.
(c) Explain two strengths of using a structured observation to investigate the behaviour of a directive autocrat leader.

(a) The directive autocrat is a leader who does not allow workers to participate in decision making and also monitors how they do their work closely. this style tends to be used in situations like a crisis or when workers are inexperienced and need more direction.

(b) A closed question could be "Do you make important decisions without asking for input from your workers?" this is relevant because the directive autocrat style involves no worker participation in decisions so a yes answer would suggest the leader uses this style.

(c) One strength is that a structured observation allows the researcher to see how the leader actually behaves in real situations instead of just going off what the leader says about themselves. Leaders might not be fully honest about their style on a questionnaire but an observation captures their real behaviour which makes it more valid.

Another strength is that a structured observation uses pre-set behavioural categories which means different observers can use the same checklist. this makes the data more reliable because you can check inter-rater reliability by comparing what two observers recorded independently and if they both get similar results then the findings are more trustworthy.

Question 12 [10+4+4+2+2+2 marks]
(a) Plan a study using an online questionnaire to investigate which type of achievement motivation is more likely to motivate workers.
Your plan must include details about:
- question format
- the sample of participants.

(a) I would use an online questionnaire with closed questions to investigate which type of achievement motivation is more common among workers. The questionnaire would be based on McClelland theory of achievement motivation which identifies three types of work needs which are achievement and affiliation and power.

For the question format I would use closed questions with a five point Likert scale where 1 is strongly disagree and 5 is strongly agree. Closed questions are good because they give quantitative data which can be compared across participants. Examples of statements could be things like "I prefer tasks where I can see clear results of my efforts" for achievement need and "I enjoy working as part of a team more than working alone" for affiliation and "I like being in charge of making important decisions" for power. Each need would have several statements so the researcher gets a reliable measure of each type rather than basing it on just one question.

The sample would be workers from different organisations and industries selected through opportunity sampling. I would share the questionnaire link through email and social media because this is practical and it lets me reach a wide range of workers from different job levels and types of work which makes the results more generalisable. I would aim for around 100 participants to have enough data.

The questionnaire would be anonymous and participants would give informed consent before starting by reading an information page explaining the purpose of the study and they would be told they can withdraw at any time by closing the browser. this is important ethically because workers might worry about their employer seeing their answers so anonymity should help them be more honest.

(b) For one piece of psychological knowledge on which your plan is based:

(i) McClelland proposed the achievement motivation theory which says that workers have three types of needs. the need for achievement is about wanting to master tasks and succeed at challenging work and people high in this need prefer moderate challenges and want regular feedback. the need for affiliation is about wanting to be liked and accepted and these people prefer teamwork over competition and do not like being singled out. the need for power is about wanting control and influence over decisions. McClelland said everyone has all three needs but one tends to be dominant and this is shaped by life experience and culture.

(ii) One feature I used was that McClelland identifies three types of motivation. I designed the questionnaire with separate sections for each need so that participants rate statements about achievement and affiliation and power individually. this way the data shows which type scores highest for each participant and the researcher can compare which type is most common across the whole sample.

Another feature I used was that McClelland said one need tends to be dominant for each person. I used a Likert scale rather than just asking people to pick which need matters most because the scale shows the strength of each need. a person might score highly on both achievement and power but one could still be slightly higher and the rating scale captures that difference.

(c)

(i) I chose opportunity sampling of workers from different organisations because this gives the study a wider range of participants than just one workplace. Workers in different industries might have different dominant needs so sampling from various places makes the results more generalisable to the working population.

(ii) One weakness of opportunity sampling is that the people who volunteer might not be representative of all workers. People who are more motivated or interested in psychology might be more likely to take part which would bias the results. for example workers with high achievement motivation might be more willing to complete a questionnaire which would make achievement seem more common than it actually is.

(iii) I chose closed questions with a Likert scale because they produce quantitative data that can be compared across the three types of need. Since the study wants to find out which type of motivation is most common the researcher needs numbers to calculate averages and make comparisons. open questions would give qualitative data which is harder to compare statistically across a large sample.
