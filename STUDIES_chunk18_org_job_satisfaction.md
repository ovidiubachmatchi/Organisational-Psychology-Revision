## STUDY 1

### Herzberg, Mausner & Snyderman (1959) — What actually makes people happy OR unhappy at work?

---

**1. Why this study exists**

It was the late 1950s. Factories and offices across America were plagued with **strikes, go-slows, and poor morale**.

> "Workers are unhappy — but WHY? Is it money? The boss? The job itself?"

Nobody had a clear answer. Herzberg had done a **literature review of 2,000 existing studies** (1900–1955) and found contradictory results — some said pay matters most, others said relationships at work do. He couldn't make sense of it.

So he formed a bold hypothesis: **satisfaction and dissatisfaction are NOT opposites on the same line.** They are two completely separate dimensions. He decided to test this properly.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine it's 1957. Herzberg's team drives to **nine different workplaces near Pittsburgh, USA**.

They sit down with **203 accountants** (no engineers in this version — some sources mention both, but the textbook specifies accountants here).

The interviewer says: *"Tell me about a time you felt really GOOD about your job."*

The worker tells a story. Then the interviewer says: *"Now tell me about a time you felt really BAD about your job."*

💡 This is the **Critical Incident Technique** — you don't ask "how do you feel in general?" You ask for specific real moments. Like asking "tell me about a car crash you remember" rather than "do you like driving?"

After each story, the worker rates the incident on a **1–21 scale**:
- 1 = barely affected my feelings
- 21 = had a huge impact on my feelings

The researchers then **coded** all the stories, looking for patterns. What kinds of things kept appearing in the good stories? What appeared in the bad stories?

**Sample:**
> Good story: "I got promoted after finishing a huge project — I felt incredible."
> Bad story: "My manager micromanaged everything I did — it was humiliating."

---

**3. What they found**

The pattern was striking: **different types of things caused satisfaction vs. dissatisfaction.**

| Type | Name | Examples | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Context of the job** | **Hygiene Factors** | Pay, company policy, physical conditions, interpersonal relations | If ABSENT → dissatisfaction. If PRESENT → neutral (not happy, just not unhappy) |
| **Content of the job** | **Motivational Factors** | Recognition, achievement, growth, responsibility | If PRESENT → genuine satisfaction & motivation. If ABSENT → neutral |

> Think of it like a bathroom. Hygiene doesn't make you happy — it just stops you being disgusted. But the good stuff (recognition, achievement) is what actually makes you feel great.

**Key insight:**
- Giving someone a pay rise removes dissatisfaction, but it does NOT create motivation.
- Giving someone meaningful responsibility creates genuine motivation.
- You need BOTH to have a fully functioning, motivated workforce.

---

**4. Why it matters**

Before Herzberg, managers thought: **"Pay people more → they'll work harder."**

After Herzberg, managers understood: **"Pay just stops people from complaining. To actually motivate them, give them challenge, responsibility, and recognition."**

This completely changed how organisations thought about **job design**, **management styles**, and **HR policy**.

Herzberg linked his findings to **Maslow's hierarchy**: hygiene factors meet lower-level needs (safety, social), while motivational factors meet higher-level needs (esteem, self-actualisation).

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength 1 — Theoretical backing**
The theory is supported by Maslow's hierarchy of needs, giving it additional credibility and a broader theoretical framework. This means it's not just one study saying something random — it fits a wider understanding of human motivation.

✅ **Strength 2 — Practical application**
The theory gives managers a clear, actionable two-step checklist: (1) fix hygiene factors, (2) build motivational factors. Organisations have successfully used this to redesign jobs and improve performance.

❌ **Weakness 1 — Ignores individual differences**
The theory assumes all workers are motivated by the same things (recognition, achievement, responsibility). **But people differ.** An introverted worker may find recognition embarrassing; a worker in financial crisis may care only about pay. Because the theory applies the same formula to everyone, its **validity is reduced** when applied to real diverse workforces.

❌ **Weakness 2 — Cultural bias**
All 203 participants were **American accountants near Pittsburgh** — a very specific cultural and occupational sample. Edward & Teoh (2009) studied university workers in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia and found that **extrinsic factors (hygiene) ALSO positively contributed to satisfaction** — not just neutralising dissatisfaction as Herzberg claimed. The absence of intrinsic factors actively demotivated workers (rather than just making them neutral). This **challenges the universality** of the theory and means it may not apply across cultures.

❌ **Weakness 3 — Method bias (self-serving attribution)**
The Critical Incident Technique relies on participants' own memories and stories. People tend to attribute **good experiences to themselves** (internal attribution — "I succeeded because I'm talented") and **bad experiences to external factors** ("It was the company's fault"). This means the data may be systematically skewed — satisfiers looking internal and dissatisfiers looking external — not because that's the truth, but because of how memory works. This threatens the **internal validity** of the findings.

❌ **Weakness 4 — Sample is too narrow**
Only accountants were studied. Accountants are professional, educated, likely already meeting basic needs. The theory may not apply to manual workers, service workers, or people in very different economic situations.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Study: 203 accountants, Pittsburgh, semi-structured interviews, critical incident technique, 1–21 rating scale
- Two factors: **Hygiene** (context, extrinsic, prevent dissatisfaction) and **Motivational** (content, intrinsic, create satisfaction)
- Absence of hygiene = dissatisfied. Presence of hygiene = neutral. Presence of motivators = satisfied/motivated.
- Key weakness: cultural bias (Edward & Teoh 2009 contradicted it in Malaysia)
- Link to Maslow: hygiene = lower needs; motivators = higher needs

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
> Herzberg et al. (1959) conducted a five-year research programme. Using the critical incident technique, they interviewed 203 accountants near Pittsburgh, USA. Participants described incidents where they felt good or bad about their job and rated the impact on a 1–21 scale. From this data, Herzberg identified two categories: hygiene factors (extrinsic, e.g. pay and working conditions) which prevent dissatisfaction but do not create satisfaction; and motivational factors (intrinsic, e.g. recognition and achievement) which create genuine satisfaction and motivation. He concluded that organisations must address both categories.

📋 **Skeleton answer (evaluation with cultural differences — 10-mark):**
> One strength is that the theory has theoretical support from Maslow's hierarchy of needs, increasing its credibility... However, one weakness is that it lacks cultural validity. Herzberg only studied American workers and assumed the model was universal. Edward and Teoh (2009) studied workers at two universities in Kuala Lumpur and found that extrinsic factors positively contributed to satisfaction, not merely neutralising dissatisfaction as Herzberg claimed. This suggests cultural differences affect which factors motivate workers, reducing the theory's cross-cultural applicability...

## STUDY 2

### Hackman & Oldham (1970s) — The Job Characteristics Model: Five levers of job satisfaction

---

**1. Why this study exists**

After Herzberg, researchers knew that intrinsic factors mattered. But **which specific features of a job** create that sense of meaning and motivation?

Hackman and Oldham wanted to go deeper. They asked: **"What is it about a job, specifically, that makes a person find it meaningful, feel responsible, and want to do it well?"**

They also noticed that **personality matters** — the same job can be fulfilling for one person and boring for another. So they built a theory that takes a **person-fit approach**: it considers both the job AND the person.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Hackman and Oldham developed the **Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS)** — a psychometric questionnaire — to test their theory.

💡 Think of the JDS like a health check-up for a job. You measure the job's "vital signs" on five dimensions, and you measure the employee's personality and growth need strength. Then you see if they match.

They collected data from **6,930 employees across 876 jobs in 56 organisations**. They used the JDS to measure:
- How does this job rate on the five core characteristics?
- How does the employee respond psychologically?
- What are the job outcomes (motivation, satisfaction, performance)?

This gave them a massive, quantitative dataset to test whether their theoretical model held up in the real world.

---

**3. What they found**

The model has three layers that connect to each other:

**Layer 1 — Five Core Job Characteristics:**

| Characteristic | What it means | Psychological state it creates |
|---|---|---|
| **Skill variety** | Uses many different skills and abilities | Experienced **meaningfulness** of work |
| **Task identity** | You do a whole job from start to finish | Experienced **meaningfulness** of work |
| **Task significance** | The job has real impact on others | Experienced **meaningfulness** of work |
| **Autonomy** | Freedom to decide HOW you work | Experienced **responsibility** for outcomes |
| **Feedback** | Clear info on how well you're performing | **Knowledge** of actual results |

**Layer 2 — Three Psychological States** (created by the characteristics above):
1. Experienced **meaningfulness** of work
2. Experienced **responsibility** for outcomes
3. **Knowledge** of results

**Layer 3 — Job Outcomes** (when all three states are present):
- High **internal work motivation**
- High **growth** satisfaction
- High **general** job satisfaction
- High **work effectiveness**

> 💡 Analogy: Think of a video game. Skill variety = different challenges. Task identity = completing a full quest, not just a random fragment. Task significance = your actions affect the game world. Autonomy = you choose your strategy. Feedback = you see your XP go up. All five together = you're HOOKED.

**The Motivating Potential Score (MPS)** — they combined the five characteristics into a single formula to predict how motivating a job would be.

---

**4. Why it matters**

This model gave HR departments and managers a **practical diagnostic tool**. Instead of vague advice ("make work more interesting"), the JDS tells you **exactly which dimension is low** and what to fix.

It also introduced the **person-fit approach** — acknowledging that a high-autonomy job might thrill someone with a strong growth need, but stress someone who prefers clear instructions. This made job design much more nuanced.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength 1 — Practical application**
The five characteristics act as a concrete checklist for job design and appraisal systems. Managers can use the JDS to identify exactly which element is missing in a job. This makes the theory directly useful to organisations — not just theoretical.

✅ **Strength 2 — Holistic approach**
Unlike Herzberg, Hackman and Oldham considered **personality, values, culture and ethnicity** alongside workplace factors. This makes it a more complete and realistic model of job satisfaction.

✅ **Strength 3 — Cross-cultural support**
Ayandele & Nnamseh (2014) tested the model with civil servants in **Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria (Africa)** and found the model was valid — the five characteristics predicted performance and psychological wellbeing even in a non-Western, non-manufacturing setting. This suggests the model has **broader generalisability** than might be expected.

❌ **Weakness 1 — Limited generalisability (Western bias)**
Despite the Nigerian study, the model was built primarily from organisations in **Western countries (USA)**. In collectivist cultures, **autonomy** and **task identity** may be less important than group harmony or seniority. This limits how universal the theory can truly be claimed to be.

❌ **Weakness 2 — Ignores practical constraints**
The theory assumes organisations can freely redesign jobs to include all five characteristics. In reality, many jobs are **structurally fixed** — a factory line worker cannot easily be given more autonomy or skill variety. The theory is sometimes too idealistic about what organisations can actually change.

❌ **Weakness 3 — Individual differences not fully captured**
While the model acknowledges "growth need strength" as a moderator (some people want enriched jobs, others don't), it doesn't deeply account for all the ways people differ — e.g. mental health, life circumstances, cultural background. For some workers, more responsibility may **increase stress** rather than motivation.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Five core characteristics: **Skill variety, Task identity, Task significance, Autonomy, Feedback**
- Three psychological states: **Meaningfulness, Responsibility, Knowledge of results**
- Person-fit approach = considers personality, values, culture alongside job features
- Supporting study: Ayandele & Nnamseh (2014) — valid in African civil service
- Weakness: Western bias; idealistic assumptions about job redesign

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe, 6 marks):**
> Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics theory, developed in the 1970s, identifies five core job characteristics that influence three psychological states, which in turn determine job outcomes. The five characteristics are skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback. The first three create a sense of meaningfulness; autonomy creates a sense of responsibility; and feedback creates knowledge of results. When all three psychological states are satisfied, the employee experiences high internal motivation, growth satisfaction, and work effectiveness. The model takes a person-fit approach, acknowledging that personality and individual values influence how workers respond to these characteristics.

## STUDY 3

### Belias & Sklikas (2013) — Job Design: Three techniques for making work better

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Knowing what makes a job satisfying (Herzberg, Hackman & Oldham) is one thing. But **how do you actually change a job** in the real world?

Belias and Sklikas (2013) reviewed and analysed **three concrete techniques** that organisations use to redesign jobs and improve satisfaction. Think of this as the "HOW-TO" guide that follows the theories.

They defined job design as:
> *"The specification of the contents, methods, and relationships of jobs in order to satisfy technological and organisational requirements as well as the social and personal requirements of the job holder"* (Armstrong, 2003)

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Belias and Sklikas conducted a **theoretical review and analysis** — examining existing research and evidence on job design interventions across multiple industries.

They weren't running a new lab experiment; instead, they were **synthesising the best available evidence** on three well-known job design techniques and their effects on employees.

💡 Think of them as food critics who visited lots of restaurants and wrote a comprehensive guide: "Here are the three best cooking techniques, what works, what doesn't, and when to use each one."

---

**3. What they found**

**Three core job design techniques:**

| Technique | What it is | Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Job Rotation** | Moving employees between different roles/tasks | Reduces boredom, builds skills, controls musculo-skeletal disorders, improves productivity | Requires time to train; gender stereotypes may block access to certain roles; skill gaps when rotating |
| **Job Enlargement** | Adding more tasks at the same level (horizontal) or giving more decision-making power (vertical) | Reduces boredom, increases variety, improves engagement | May just be "more of the same boring work" if not done carefully; can increase workload without genuine enrichment |
| **Job Enrichment** | Making the job deeper — more challenge, autonomy, responsibility, growth opportunities, and feedback | Increases motivation, performance, and job satisfaction; positively correlates with feedback | Assumes workers want challenge; may increase stress for some workers |

**Two main features of job enrichment (especially important):**
1. **Variety** — different types of tasks
2. **Feedback** — clear information about performance

Employee motivation **positively correlates with feedback** — when workers know how they're doing, they are more motivated to improve.

---

**4. Why it matters**

This paper gives organisations a **practical toolkit**. Rather than just saying "make work more meaningful", it shows three specific mechanisms:

- **Job rotation** targets boredom and physical strain
- **Job enlargement** targets monotony and skill development
- **Job enrichment** targets motivation and psychological growth

Chen & Lu (2012) provided empirical support, finding that **job design had a positive impact on employee motivation and performance** — and importantly, this was achieved through **non-monetary means** (no extra pay required), linking directly to intrinsic motivation research.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Strength 1 — Practical and directly applicable**
The three techniques are concrete and actionable. Organisations can immediately implement job rotation schemes, redesign job roles, or introduce feedback systems. This gives the theory high **real-world utility**.

✅ **Strength 2 — Addresses physical AND psychological wellbeing**
Job rotation in particular targets **musculo-skeletal disorders** (physical harm from repetitive work) as well as boredom — showing a broad understanding of worker wellbeing beyond just "motivation."

✅ **Strength 3 — Supported by empirical evidence**
Chen & Lu (2012) found positive effects of job design on motivation and performance, giving the framework empirical backing beyond just theory.

❌ **Weakness 1 — Idealistic assumptions (nomothetic problem)**
The theory assumes all organisations CAN implement these techniques. But for some industries (e.g. factory lines, call centres), job rotation or enrichment may be structurally impossible. The theory gives **general prescriptions that don't fit every context**, reducing its universal applicability.

❌ **Weakness 2 — Ignores individual differences (idiographic problem)**
What looks like "enrichment" to one worker may be overwhelming for another. A worker already under family or financial stress may experience more responsibility as a burden, not an opportunity. The theory doesn't account for how **individual circumstances and personality** affect responses to job design changes.

❌ **Weakness 3 — Risk of increased stress**
Adding tasks (enlargement) without increasing meaning or reducing other burdens can simply mean **more work for the same pay** — which can decrease satisfaction rather than increase it. Not all forms of enlargement are genuine enrichment.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Three techniques: **Rotation, Enlargement, Enrichment**
- Job rotation: reduces boredom AND musculo-skeletal disorders; problem = gender stereotypes
- Job enlargement: horizontal (more tasks) vs vertical (more decision-making)
- Job enrichment: variety + feedback; motivation correlates with feedback
- Weakness: idiographic approach would be better (individual differences matter)

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe, 6 marks):**
> Belias and Sklikas (2013) identified three key techniques of job design. First, job rotation allows employees to move between different roles, which reduces boredom, builds skills and reduces work-related musculo-skeletal injuries. Second, job enlargement expands a job to include more or different tasks — either horizontally (more tasks at the same level) or vertically (more decision-making). Third, job enrichment challenges employees through greater variety, autonomy, responsibility and feedback. Research shows that motivation positively correlates with feedback. Together, these techniques can increase motivation and job satisfaction without monetary incentives.

## SUPPORTING STUDY A

### Edward & Teoh (2009) — Herzberg doesn't fully work in Malaysia

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Herzberg's theory was created in Pittsburgh, USA, with American accountants. A critical question for any theory is: **does it work everywhere, or just in the culture where it was developed?**

Edward and Teoh tested Herzberg's two-factor theory in a **non-Western context**.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Edward and Teoh studied **faculty members (university lecturers/academics) at two major universities in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia**.

They used a **questionnaire based on Herzberg's ten major factors** of job satisfaction and dissatisfaction, applied to a random sample of faculty members.

They asked: "Which of these factors contribute to your job satisfaction, and which cause dissatisfaction?"

---

**3. What they found**

| Finding | Herzberg's prediction | Edward & Teoh's actual result |
|---|---|---|
| Extrinsic factors (hygiene) | → neutral (neither satisfied nor dissatisfied) | → **positively contributed to satisfaction** |
| Absence of intrinsic factors | → neutral | → **actively demotivated workers** |

The **major sources of satisfaction** were: policy, administration, and **salary** (all extrinsic/hygiene factors).

The **major sources of dissatisfaction** included: lack of recognition, responsibility, personal growth — (intrinsic factors).

> This contradicts Herzberg. In Malaysia, extrinsic factors like salary didn't just "prevent dissatisfaction" — they **actively created satisfaction**. And missing intrinsic factors didn't just create neutrality — they **actively demotivated**.

---

**4. Why it matters**

This is used as a **cultural weakness** of Herzberg's theory. It shows that the relationship between hygiene factors and motivational factors is **culturally mediated** — what acts as a motivator in one culture may not do the same in another.

It also supports the idea that **individual and cultural differences** must be considered when applying any theory of job satisfaction.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ Uses real workers in a real organisation (ecological validity).
✅ Directly tests Herzberg's framework, making comparison clean.

❌ Sample limited to **Malaysian university academics** — may not represent all Malaysian workers, let alone all non-Western workers.
❌ Self-report questionnaire — social desirability bias may affect answers.

## SUPPORTING STUDY B

### Ayandele & Nnamseh (2014) — Hackman & Oldham's model works in Africa too

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Hackman & Oldham's Job Characteristics Model was developed in Western organisations. A legitimate criticism is that it may not apply in **collectivist cultures** where autonomy and individual task identity are valued less.

Ayandele & Nnamseh tested whether the model held up in **Africa**.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

They studied **civil servants in Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria** — a real, government public sector workforce in West Africa.

They measured the five core characteristics against employee performance and psychological wellbeing using a structured approach.

---

**3. What they found**

The Job Characteristics Model **was valid** in the African civil service context. The five characteristics still predicted:
- Psychological wellbeing
- Growth and development
- Internal motivation

The model was found to apply not just across cultures, but also across **different types of organisations** (manufacturing vs service-oriented).

---

**4. Why it matters**

This study is used as a **strength** of Hackman & Oldham's theory — it provides cross-cultural validity evidence from a non-Western, African setting. It challenges the assumption that Western organisational psychology theories are necessarily culturally limited.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ Extends validity evidence beyond Western settings — strengthens generalisability.
✅ Government/public sector context adds breadth (theory tested outside private sector).

❌ One country, one state (Akwa Ibom) — may not represent all of Africa.
❌ Civil service has specific characteristics (job security, bureaucracy) that may affect how the model applies elsewhere.

## SUPPORTING STUDY C

### Chen & Lu (2012) — Job design improves motivation and performance

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Belias & Sklikas described job design techniques theoretically. Chen & Lu provided **empirical evidence** that these techniques actually work in practice.

---

**2. What they found**

Job design had a **positive impact on employee motivation and performance**.

Crucially: this was achieved through **non-monetary means** — through autonomy, responsibility and feedback — directly supporting the concept of **intrinsic motivation** being more effective long-term than pay increases.

---

**3. Why it matters**

This supports both Herzberg (intrinsic factors matter more for performance) and Hackman & Oldham (job characteristics drive motivation). It shows that investing in job redesign is a **cost-effective way** to increase organisational productivity without simply increasing wages.

## MEASURING JOB SATISFACTION — The Psychometric Tools (9.5.2)

> These are not "studies" with a procedure/findings structure, but they ARE assessable content. Here's a clear breakdown.

---

### Tool 1: The Job Descriptive Index (JDI) — Smith, Kendall & Hulin (1969)

**What it is:**
The **JDI** is perhaps the most widely used psychometric tool for measuring job satisfaction. Developed by Smith, Kendall and Hulin in 1969 at Cornell University.

**What it measures — five facets:**

| Facet | What it asks about |
|---|---|
| Work itself | The nature and content of the actual tasks |
| Pay | Satisfaction with remuneration |
| Promotion | Opportunities for career advancement |
| Supervision | Relationship with and quality of management |
| Coworkers | Relationships with colleagues |

**How it works:**
- 72 items total (adjectives or short phrases)
- For each item, participant responds: **Y (yes), N (no), or ? (unsure)**
- Scoring: Y on positive items = 3 points; N on positive items = 0 points; ? = 1 point

> 💡 Analogy: Like a checklist where you tick whether each description fits your job. "Fascinating" for the work itself — yes/no/?

**Strengths:**
- Highly **reliable** — has been validated and revised multiple times (1969, 1985, 1997, 2009)
- Measures **specific facets** separately — gives detailed, actionable information (you know WHICH aspect is low)
- Most widely used measure globally — comparative data available

**Weaknesses:**
- Measures only five facets — may miss important aspects of satisfaction
- Adjective-based responses (Y/N/?) are blunt — no nuance in how strongly someone agrees
- Focuses on **individual** factors (personality, satisfaction with role) — may miss **situational** factors (workload, organisational culture, economic pressures)

---

### Tool 2: The Quality of Work Life (QWL) Scale

**What it is:**
A broader, multidimensional psychometric scale that measures not just job satisfaction but the **overall quality of a person's working life**.

**What it measures — multiple dimensions including:**

| Dimension | Example |
|---|---|
| Work environment | Safety, physical conditions |
| Organisational culture | Trust, communication |
| Compensation & rewards | Pay, benefits |
| Job satisfaction & security | How secure and fulfilled they feel |
| Autonomy | Degree of control over their work |
| Training & development | Opportunities to grow |
| Relationships | With colleagues and supervisors |

**How it works:**
Multi-item rating scale (typically Likert-type: 1–5 or 1–7). Often uses factor analysis to confirm that items cluster into the expected dimensions.

**Strengths:**
- Much broader than JDI — captures the **full picture** of working life
- Considers situational factors (culture, environment) not just individual feelings
- Psychometrically validated — high reliability and validity reported in multiple studies

**Weaknesses:**
- **Very broad** — may be harder to act on (if overall QWL is low, which specific dimension do you fix?)
- Still relies on **self-report** — susceptible to social desirability bias and response set
- Like JDI, may miss truly **individual** factors — a rating scale gives averages, not the unique experience of each worker

---

## OVERALL EVALUATION THEMES

**Issue 1 — Reliability of measurement tools**

Both JDI and QWL are **psychometric tests** that have been tested for reliability and validity. The JDI in particular has high test-retest reliability and has been repeatedly revised. However, all self-report measures share the limitation of **social desirability bias** — workers may not honestly report dissatisfaction if they fear consequences.

**Issue 2 — Cultural differences (runs through everything)**

> 🌍 Every theory in this chapter is criticised for cultural bias.
- Herzberg: built on US accountants; Edward & Teoh (2009) showed it doesn't fully apply in Malaysia
- Hackman & Oldham: built on Western data; Ayandele & Nnamseh (2014) showed it DOES hold in Nigeria
- JDI/QWL: developed in Western contexts; different cultures may prioritise different facets of satisfaction

**Issue 3 — Idiographic vs Nomothetic**

These theories and tools all take a **nomothetic approach** — finding general patterns across large groups. But individual workers may need **idiographic** approaches (case studies, personalised interventions) to truly understand what motivates them. A rating scale can't capture the full complexity of one person's experience.

**Issue 4 — Application to everyday life**

All theories and tools ultimately aim for one goal: helping **organisations improve job satisfaction in practical ways**. The strongest evaluation points always link theory → real-world action → evidence that it works.
