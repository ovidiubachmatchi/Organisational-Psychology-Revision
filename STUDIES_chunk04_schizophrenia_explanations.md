## STUDY 1

### Gottesman & Shields (1966) — Twin study showing schizophrenia has a genetic component

**1. Why this study exists**

- Before the 1960s, people argued endlessly about whether schizophrenia was caused by bad parenting, poverty, or brain biology.
- Gottesman and Shields wanted to test the genetic argument using a clean comparison: identical twins vs. non-identical twins.
- If genes cause schizophrenia, **identical twins** (who share 100% of DNA) should both develop it far more often than **non-identical twins** (who share only ~50% of DNA).
- This is a fundamental question: **is schizophrenia inherited?**

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you are working in a large psychiatric hospital in London — the **Maudsley Hospital** — in the 1960s.

- The researchers trawled through **hospital records** looking for patients who had a twin.
- They found **57 pairs of twins** where at least one twin had been diagnosed with schizophrenia.
- Ages ranged from 19 to 64.
- They confirmed whether twins were **monozygotic (MZ)** — identical — or **dizygotic (DZ)** — non-identical — using blood tests, fingerprint analysis, and physical appearance.

💡 Think of MZ twins as photocopies of each other genetically. DZ twins are like ordinary siblings who happened to be born at the same time.

- The key question: if Twin A has schizophrenia, does Twin B also have it?
- This is called the **concordance rate** — the percentage of pairs where BOTH twins have the condition.

---

**3. What they found**

| Twin type | DNA shared | Concordance rate |
|---|---|---|
| Monozygotic (MZ) — identical | ~100% | **42%** |
| Dizygotic (DZ) — non-identical | ~50% | **9%** |

- For **severe schizophrenia** only, MZ concordance climbed to **75%** vs 22% for DZ.
- Both MZ and DZ twins were raised in the same household, so the environment was roughly equal.
- The big gap between 42% and 9% pointed firmly at **genes** playing a major role.

> 🧠 Quick check: if schizophrenia were PURELY genetic, what would the MZ concordance rate be? Answer: 100%. It's only 42% — so something else must matter too.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This was landmark evidence that **schizophrenia runs in families via genetics**, not just bad parenting.
- But MZ concordance is NOT 100% — which means **environmental factors also matter**.
- This led Gottesman and Shields to propose the **diathesis-stress model**: you inherit a genetic vulnerability (diathesis) but need a life trigger (stress) for the disorder to develop.
- Also introduced the idea that schizophrenia is **polygenic** — caused by many genes, not just one.

**Real-world implication:** Genetic counselling for families with schizophrenia history. If your sibling has it, your risk is elevated but NOT certain.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Large, systematic sample** — 57 pairs is substantial for the 1960s, drawn from hospital records (not self-selected volunteers). This improves representativeness.

✅ **Natural comparison group built in** — MZ vs DZ twins is a clever design because you control for family environment while varying genetic similarity. This is as close to a controlled experiment as twin studies get.

✅ **Supports the diathesis-stress model** — the fact that MZ concordance is NOT 100% is actually a strength: it means the study honestly reflects complexity rather than forcing a simple answer.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Equal environments assumption is flawed** — The logic only works if MZ and DZ pairs share equally similar environments. But MZ twins are always the same sex, look nearly identical, and often treated more similarly. This means MZ twins may share MORE similar environments than DZ twins — so we cannot be sure the higher concordance is purely genetic.

- Consequence: the genetic contribution may be **overstated**.

❌ **Concordance, not causation** — Even a 42% concordance rate only shows correlation. It cannot tell us which specific genes are involved or HOW they cause schizophrenia.

- Consequence: the study is evidence FOR genetics but gives no mechanistic explanation.

❌ **Diagnostic reliability issues** — In the 1960s, schizophrenia was diagnosed much more loosely than today (especially in the USA vs. UK). Different raters may have classified the same symptoms differently.

- Consequence: concordance rates may be **inflated or deflated** depending on how strictly schizophrenia was defined.

❌ **Sample is a hospital population** — People who are in hospital are likely to have more severe schizophrenia. The concordance rates may not apply to people with mild or subclinical presentations.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- MZ = 42%, DZ = 9% (these numbers WILL appear in exam questions)
- The study supports genetics but NOT genetic determinism (not 100%)
- Leads to the diathesis-stress model

📋 **Skeleton answer — 6 marks (describe):**

> Gottesman and Shields (1966) conducted a twin study using 57 pairs of twins drawn from hospital records at the Maudsley Hospital, where at least one twin had been diagnosed with schizophrenia. They compared concordance rates between monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) twins. Since both types of twins were raised in similar environments, any difference in concordance was attributed to genetics. They found a concordance rate of 42% in MZ twins and 9% in DZ twins, suggesting a significant genetic contribution to schizophrenia, though the fact that concordance was not 100% indicated that environmental factors also play a role.

📋 **Skeleton answer — evaluation point (AO3):**

> A weakness of Gottesman and Shields' twin study is that it relies on the equal environments assumption — the idea that MZ and DZ twins experience equally similar environments. However, this is questionable because MZ twins are always the same sex, look more similar, and may be treated more similarly by parents and peers. This means the greater similarity in environment for MZ twins, not just their genetic similarity, could explain the higher concordance rate, which undermines the validity of the genetic conclusion.

## STUDY 2

### Kim et al. (2020) — DiGeorge Syndrome: when a chunk of DNA goes missing and schizophrenia risk skyrockets

**1. Why this study exists**

- Researchers know schizophrenia is partly genetic, but which genes?
- **DiGeorge syndrome** (also called 22q11.2 deletion syndrome) is a natural experiment: a specific chunk of DNA — about 30–40 genes — gets **deleted from chromosome 22** during cell division.
- About **1 in 4** people with DiGeorge syndrome develop schizophrenia.
- Compare that to the general population: less than **1 in 100**.
- Kim et al. (2020) examined psychiatric presentations in DiGeorge syndrome patients to understand how this deletion relates to psychosis, focusing on the **COMT gene**.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine DNA replication as a massive photocopying job for every cell in your body.

💡 The copy machine occasionally has a paper jam or glitch — and instead of copying a section, it skips it entirely. That missing section is called a **deletion**.

- In DiGeorge syndrome, the deletion on chromosome 22 is so large it removes 30–40 genes at once.
- One of those genes is **COMT** (Catechol-O-Methyltransferase).
- COMT codes for an **enzyme** — a biological janitor — that breaks down neurotransmitters like **dopamine** once they've done their job in the synapse.
- If the COMT gene is deleted, there is **no enzyme to clear the dopamine**.
- Result: dopamine accumulates in the prefrontal cortex, and potentially dysregulates the entire dopaminergic system.
- Kim et al. studied psychiatric case reports of patients with the 22q11.2 deletion, documenting rates of psychotic symptoms.

---

**3. What they found**

| Group | Schizophrenia risk |
|---|---|
| General population | < 1% |
| People with DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion) | ~25% (1 in 4) |

- The deletion of COMT was identified as a key driver of this elevated risk.
- The loss of COMT → reduced dopamine breakdown → **dopamine accumulates** → disrupts prefrontal cortex function → psychotic symptoms.
- This supports the **dopamine deficiency hypothesis**: it's not just about having "too much" dopamine overall, but about having dopamine in the wrong places at the wrong amounts.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- DiGeorge syndrome is the strongest known **single genetic risk factor** for schizophrenia.
- It demonstrates that a precise, identifiable genetic event can dramatically raise schizophrenia risk.
- Links the genetic explanation directly to the **dopamine hypothesis** — the missing gene → the missing enzyme → the dopamine problem → the symptoms.
- Opens the door to targeted treatments: if we understand which enzyme is missing, we might be able to supplement or replace it.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Biological precision** — Unlike most schizophrenia genetics research (which deals with thousands of small gene variants), DiGeorge syndrome involves one identifiable deletion. This makes the mechanism clearer and the study more falsifiable.

✅ **Bridges genetic and biochemical explanations** — The study directly connects a specific gene (COMT) to a neurotransmitter (dopamine), providing an explanatory mechanism rather than just a correlation.

❌ **DiGeorge syndrome is rare** — This is a very specific and uncommon condition. The findings may not generalise to the vast majority of people with schizophrenia who do NOT have this deletion.

- Consequence: DiGeorge syndrome is a special case; schizophrenia in the general population is caused by a far more complex combination of factors.

❌ **3 in 4 people with DiGeorge syndrome do NOT develop schizophrenia** — Even with the COMT gene missing, most people are okay. This shows that even this powerful genetic factor is not sufficient on its own.

- Consequence: reinforces that schizophrenia requires multiple converging factors — the diathesis-stress model again.

❌ **Case report methodology** — Kim et al. (2020) used case report data, which lacks the statistical power of large controlled studies. Individual cases may not be representative.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- COMT gene → codes for enzyme that **breaks down dopamine**
- Deletion on **chromosome 22** (22q11.2)
- 1 in 4 with DiGeorge syndrome develop schizophrenia vs. less than 1 in 100 in the general population
- Links genetic explanation TO dopamine hypothesis

🧠 Quick check: Why does losing the COMT gene increase schizophrenia risk? Because COMT breaks down dopamine — without it, dopamine cannot be cleared properly.

📋 **Skeleton answer — evaluation point:**

> A strength of the genetic explanation is that research by Kim et al. (2020) demonstrates a specific biological mechanism. People with DiGeorge syndrome, in which the COMT gene is deleted from chromosome 22, have a 1 in 4 risk of developing schizophrenia compared to less than 1 in 100 in the general population. As COMT codes for an enzyme that breaks down dopamine, its absence may result in excessive dopamine activity, directly linking the genetic and dopamine explanations of schizophrenia. However, as most DiGeorge syndrome patients do not develop schizophrenia, other factors must also contribute.

## STUDY 3

### Egan et al. (2001) — The COMT Val allele: a tiny genetic difference with measurable effects on the brain and thinking

**1. Why this study exists**

- Not everyone with a COMT gene problem has DiGeorge syndrome. The COMT gene itself comes in two versions (alleles): **Val** and **Met**.
- These two versions create different amounts of the COMT enzyme.
- Egan and colleagues wanted to find out: does which version of COMT you carry affect your **brain function**, **cognitive performance**, and **risk of schizophrenia**?
- This is more directly relevant to everyday schizophrenia (not just rare deletion syndromes).

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

You are a researcher at the National Institute of Mental Health in the USA in the early 2000s.

- You recruit **175 patients with schizophrenia**, their **219 unaffected siblings**, and **55 healthy controls**.
- Everyone gives a DNA sample, which is genotyped for the COMT Val/Met polymorphism.
- Participants complete the **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST)** — a standard measure of frontal lobe cognitive ability.

💡 The WCST is like a card game where the rules keep secretly changing and you have to figure out the new rule from the feedback. It tests your ability to think flexibly and update your understanding — a skill that requires the prefrontal cortex.

- A subgroup also undergoes **fMRI brain scans** while performing a working memory task, so researchers can see which brain regions are active and HOW efficiently they work.
- The key variable: does having the **Val allele** (associated with HIGHER COMT enzyme activity = MORE dopamine breakdown = LESS dopamine in prefrontal cortex) predict worse performance?

---

**3. What they found**

| COMT genotype | Prefrontal dopamine | WCST performance | fMRI efficiency | Schizophrenia risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Met/Met | Lower COMT activity → more dopamine | Better | More efficient | Lower risk |
| Val/Val | Higher COMT activity → less dopamine | Worse | Less efficient | Higher risk |

- The **Val allele** was transmitted significantly more often to children with schizophrenia than expected by chance (family-based association analysis).
- COMT genotype explained **4% of the variance** in WCST errors — small but statistically significant.
- The Val allele impaired prefrontal physiology even in **healthy controls and unaffected siblings**, suggesting it is a genuine genetic vulnerability, not just an artefact of having schizophrenia.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides molecular-level support for **dopamine deficiency** in the prefrontal cortex as a cause of cognitive symptoms.
- Shows that a common genetic variant (not a rare deletion) can influence schizophrenia risk through its effect on dopamine metabolism.
- Explains WHY negative and cognitive symptoms exist: less dopamine in the prefrontal cortex → impaired working memory, attention, and executive function.
- Opens the door to **pharmacogenomics**: tailoring antipsychotic medication based on a patient's COMT genotype.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Multi-method design** — Combines genetics (genotyping), neuropsychology (WCST), and neuroimaging (fMRI). Convergent evidence from three different methods strengthens confidence in the finding.

✅ **Includes siblings and controls** — By showing the effect of Val allele on cognition even in people WITHOUT schizophrenia, the study demonstrates that this is a genuine biological mechanism, not just a consequence of being ill.

✅ **Directly links two explanations** — Connects the genetic explanation (COMT allele) to the biochemical explanation (dopamine levels in the prefrontal cortex), providing a chain of causation.

❌ **Effect size is small** — COMT genotype explained only 4% of variance in cognitive performance. Schizophrenia is highly polygenic; this one gene variant is just a tiny piece of a vast puzzle.

- Consequence: COMT Val allele is a risk factor, not a cause. This finding alone cannot explain most cases of schizophrenia.

❌ **Gender moderation** — Later research (Wang et al., 2020) found the Val allele's effect on cognition may be stronger in men with schizophrenia than women, suggesting the picture is even more complex.

- Consequence: findings may not generalise equally across all groups.

❌ **Deterministic interpretation risk** — The study could be misread as saying "if you have Val/Val, you will get schizophrenia." The reality: Val/Val is a minor elevated risk, and most Val/Val people are perfectly healthy.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- COMT gene has two alleles: **Val** (more enzyme = less dopamine) and **Met** (less enzyme = more dopamine)
- Val allele → impaired prefrontal function → cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia
- Study used Wisconsin Card Sorting Test + fMRI
- Explains dopamine **deficiency** in prefrontal cortex (not excess)

> Memory hook: Val = Valleys (low dopamine = dips in prefrontal function). Met = More dopamine = better thinking.

📋 **Skeleton answer — evaluation AO3:**

> Support for the genetic explanation comes from Egan et al. (2001), who found that the Val allele of the COMT gene was associated with decreased dopamine activity in the prefrontal cortex, poorer performance on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, and a slightly elevated risk of schizophrenia. As the Val allele increases COMT enzyme activity, dopamine is broken down more rapidly in the prefrontal cortex, resulting in dopamine deficiency in this region. This is significant because it directly links the genetic explanation of schizophrenia to the dopamine deficiency hypothesis, providing a molecular mechanism for the cognitive and negative symptoms of the disorder.

## STUDY 4

### St Clair et al. (1990) — The Scottish Family: DISC1 and a chromosome glitch running through generations

**1. Why this study exists**

- Researchers noticed that in certain families, schizophrenia and other serious mental illnesses appeared generation after generation at far higher rates than chance.
- One extraordinary Scottish family became the focus of genetic investigation.
- St Clair and colleagues wanted to know: is there a single identifiable genetic abnormality passing through this family that is causing psychiatric illness?

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you are a genetic psychiatrist in Scotland in the late 1980s.

- A large Scottish family is referred to your attention because an unusual number of its members have been hospitalised for severe mental illness: schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depression.
- You start building a **family tree (pedigree)** and documenting diagnoses across multiple generations.
- You then take blood samples and conduct chromosomal analysis.
- Discovery: **34 members of this family** carry a rare chromosomal abnormality — a **balanced translocation** between chromosomes 1 and 11.

💡 A balanced translocation is like accidentally swapping two pages from two different books. The total information is the same, but the gene ends up in the wrong place — and when it is in the wrong place, it stops working properly.

- The disrupted gene sits at the junction of this translocation — later named **DISC1**: **D**isrupted **I**n **S**chizophrenia **1**.
- DISC1 is involved in regulating GABA, a neurotransmitter that inhibits over-activity in dopamine circuits.

---

**3. What they found**

- **34 family members** carried the DISC1 translocation.
- Those who carried it had dramatically elevated rates of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and recurrent depression compared to family members without the translocation.
- The translocation **co-segregated** with mental illness — meaning it appeared in the same people who were mentally ill, not in the healthy ones.

| Genetic status | Mental illness present |
|---|---|
| Carried DISC1 translocation | Yes (most cases) |
| Did not carry the translocation | No (mostly healthy) |

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This was one of the **first specific genetic loci** ever identified for schizophrenia.
- It showed that a single chromosomal event could predispose a person to a range of serious mental illnesses, not just schizophrenia.
- It led to decades of DISC1 research, including animal studies (see Dahoun et al., 2017 below).
- It also raised questions about the **boundary between diagnoses** — if one gene variant causes schizophrenia in some people and bipolar disorder in others, maybe these conditions share more biology than we thought.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Rare natural experiment** — The translocation provided an unusually clear genetic signal. Unlike polygenic studies with thousands of small variants, this family had a single identifiable genetic event, making it easier to study causation.

✅ **Co-segregation is compelling evidence** — The fact that the translocation tracked with mental illness (and NOT with healthy family members) is a powerful genetic signal.

❌ **Single family — very limited generalisability** — This is essentially a case study of one family. The DISC1 translocation is extremely rare outside this family, so findings cannot be applied to the broader schizophrenia population.

- Consequence: DISC1 explains very few cases of schizophrenia worldwide.

❌ **DISC1's role is highly contested** — Sullivan (2013) and others argue that the contribution of DISC1 to schizophrenia has been overstated, and that it is also associated with many other conditions (bipolar disorder, ADHD, autism), undermining its specificity.

- Consequence: DISC1 may not be a schizophrenia gene per se — it may be a general "psychiatric vulnerability" gene.

❌ **Ethical concerns with family pedigree studies** — Identifying individuals in a family who carry a genetic risk for psychiatric illness raises serious ethical questions about privacy, stigma, and the psychological impact of being told you carry a risk gene.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- DISC1 = Disrupted In Schizophrenia 1
- Found in a large Scottish family — **34 members** carried the translocation
- Involves chromosome **1 and 11** translocation
- DISC1 linked to GABA regulation, which affects dopamine circuits
- Role is contested — also linked to other disorders

🧠 Quick check: Why is the DISC1 gene called "disrupted"? Because the chromosomal translocation physically breaks the gene apart, disrupting its function.

## STUDY 5

### Dahoun et al. (2017) — Meta-analysis: DISC1 and dopamine dysregulation across 14 animal studies

**1. Why this study exists**

- After St Clair et al. (1990), researchers conducted many animal experiments to understand HOW the DISC1 gene might cause schizophrenia at a neurochemical level.
- No single animal study is enough — animals are not humans, and individual studies can give contradictory results.
- Dahoun and colleagues conducted a **meta-analysis** of 14 animal experiments to find the overall pattern: does DISC1 disruption cause dopamine dysregulation?

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

A meta-analysis is like being a judge who has to decide a case by reading 14 different witnesses' testimonies and working out what the combined evidence suggests.

- Dahoun et al. searched the scientific literature for animal experiments where DISC1 had been genetically manipulated (typically mice with the DISC1 gene knocked out or mutated).
- They extracted data on **presynaptic dopamine function** — how the brain manufactures and releases dopamine BEFORE it crosses the synapse.
- They then ran statistical analyses to pool the results from all 14 studies.

💡 Think of presynaptic dopamine dysregulation as a factory that produces too many (or too few) goods before shipping. Problems at the production stage affect everything downstream.

---

**3. What they found**

- Across 14 controlled animal experiments, DISC1 disruption consistently produced **presynaptic dopamine dysregulation**.
- This means the problem is in how dopamine is manufactured and released, not just how it binds to receptors.
- The finding supports DISC1 as a biological mechanism linking a specific gene to the dopamine abnormalities observed in schizophrenia.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Meta-analyses carry more evidential weight than individual studies — they pool data to reduce the effect of random results.
- Animal experiments are tightly controlled, so they have high **internal validity** (we can be more sure that the DISC1 manipulation caused the dopamine effect, not some other variable).
- Provides a mechanistic link: DISC1 gene → disrupts dopamine synthesis/release → schizophrenic-like brain chemistry.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **High internal validity** — Animal experiments allow strict control of variables. By genetically manipulating just the DISC1 gene, researchers can isolate its effect on dopamine, which is much harder to do in human studies.

✅ **Meta-analysis reduces random error** — Pooling 14 studies is more reliable than any single experiment. If the same pattern appears across multiple labs and species, it is likely a genuine biological effect.

❌ **Animal studies cannot fully model human schizophrenia** — Mice do not experience delusions or hear voices. We can only measure behavioural proxies (strange movements, social withdrawal, sensorimotor gating deficits) that might correspond to schizophrenic symptoms.

- Consequence: the dopamine effects seen in mice may not translate directly to the human experience of schizophrenia.

❌ **DISC1 affects only a tiny fraction of schizophrenia cases** — Even if DISC1 causes dopamine problems in animal models, the DISC1 translocation is vanishingly rare in the general human population.

- Consequence: this mechanism explains almost no real-world schizophrenia, limiting practical applicability.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Meta-analysis of **14 animal experiments**
- Finding: DISC1 → **presynaptic dopamine dysregulation**
- Strength: high internal validity from animal experiments
- Weakness: animal models ≠ human schizophrenia

📋 **How to use this in an exam evaluation:**

> Further support comes from a meta-analysis by Dahoun et al. (2017), which analysed 14 animal experiments and found consistent evidence that DISC1 disruption leads to presynaptic dopamine dysregulation. As animal experiments are tightly controlled, this finding has high internal validity. However, as animal models cannot fully replicate the complex human experience of schizophrenia, the relevance of these findings to clinical populations should be interpreted with caution.

## STUDY 6

### Tenn et al. (2003) — Rats on amphetamines: creating a chemical model of schizophrenia

**1. Why this study exists**

- The **dopamine hypothesis** predicts that excess dopamine causes schizophrenic symptoms.
- To test this directly in a living brain — ethically — you cannot inject humans with drugs to give them psychosis.
- Tenn, Fletcher, and Kapur (2003) used rats instead, asking: if you artificially raise dopamine activity using **amphetamines**, do rats start showing schizophrenia-like behaviour?
- This also allowed them to test whether blocking dopamine receptors could reverse those symptoms.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Picture a laboratory in Canada. Rows of rats in cages. A team of researchers with syringes.

- Rats were randomly assigned to receive either **amphetamine injections** or **saline (salt water) injections** (control group).
- Amphetamine is a stimulant that causes dopamine to flood the synapses by preventing its reuptake and pushing dopamine release — it's like jaming the dopamine "on" switch.
- The dose was increased progressively across three weeks:
  - Week 1: 1 mg/kg amphetamine, three times per week
  - Week 2: 2 mg/kg
  - Week 3: 3 mg/kg
  - Total: **nine injections** over three weeks.

💡 This progressive increase mimics **sensitisation** — the brain becomes increasingly reactive to the same chemical stimulus, like turning up the volume on dopamine again and again.

- After the injection period, researchers observed the rats' behaviour.
- They measured **prepulse inhibition (PPI)** — a standard test for schizophrenic-like sensorimotor gating. A startling noise is preceded by a quieter warning tone; normal rats (and people) filter out the big startle. People with schizophrenia (and the sensitised rats) fail to filter it — they overreact.
- They also measured locomotor activity (how much the rats moved around) and neurochemistry (dopamine receptor binding in the striatum).
- Finally, they tested whether giving rats **D1 receptor blockers** (dopamine antagonists) could reverse the behaviours.

---

**3. What they found**

| Condition | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Saline-injected (control) rats | Normal movement, normal PPI, normal social behaviour |
| Amphetamine-sensitised rats | Strange movements, social withdrawal, disrupted PPI |
| Amphetamine rats given D1 blockers | Symptoms alleviated |

- The sensitised rats showed **lower binding of dopamine receptors** in the striatum (consistent with upregulation/downregulation in human schizophrenia).
- Social withdrawal and abnormal movements **mirrored negative and positive symptoms** of schizophrenia.
- Blocking D1 receptors **reversed** the symptoms — supporting the dopamine hypothesis.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides causal evidence (not just correlation) that excess dopamine activity produces schizophrenic-like symptoms.
- Shows that dopamine-blocking drugs can reverse those symptoms — directly supporting antipsychotic treatment.
- Also shows that **environmental factors** (drug use) can trigger biological dopamine dysregulation — bridging nature and nurture.
- Useful animal model for testing new antipsychotic drugs before human trials.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Experimental control** — Random assignment to amphetamine vs. saline means we can attribute the behavioural differences to the drug, not to pre-existing differences between rats. High **internal validity**.

✅ **Causal evidence** — Unlike human twin studies or brain scans, this study directly manipulates dopamine and measures the outcome. This is the strongest type of evidence for a causal claim.

✅ **Supports treatment** — The reversal of symptoms with D1 blockers directly supports antipsychotic medication as a treatment strategy.

❌ **Animal-to-human generalisability** — Rats do not experience paranoia, thought disorder, or delusions. "Strange movements" and "social withdrawal" are not the same as hearing voices or believing the government is monitoring you.

- Consequence: animal models can only partially mirror human schizophrenia. They model some symptoms (particularly positive ones) but miss the subjective, cognitive, and negative symptom dimensions.

❌ **Amphetamine use is not how most people develop schizophrenia** — Deliberately sensitising rats with escalating drug doses is an artificial route to dopamine excess. Most people with schizophrenia have never used amphetamines.

- Consequence: the model may be more relevant to drug-induced psychosis than to "naturally occurring" schizophrenia.

❌ **Ethical concerns** — Deliberately causing drug-induced distress in animals raises serious ethical questions, even if the research has scientific value.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Rats given **amphetamine** (increases dopamine) → showed schizophrenic-like behaviour
- Symptoms **alleviated by D1 receptor blockers** (dopamine antagonists)
- Supports the dopamine hypothesis with **causal evidence**
- Weakness: animal models do not fully map onto human experience

🧠 Quick check: Why does amphetamine cause schizophrenic-like behaviour? It floods synapses with dopamine by blocking reuptake and pushing release.

📋 **Skeleton answer — evaluation:**

> Support for the dopamine hypothesis comes from Tenn et al. (2003), who injected rats with progressively increasing doses of amphetamine over three weeks. The sensitised rats displayed schizophrenic-like behaviours including social withdrawal and disrupted prepulse inhibition. Crucially, these symptoms were alleviated when rats were given drugs to block their D1 dopamine receptors, supporting the causal role of excess dopamine in schizophrenic symptoms. However, rats cannot experience the subjective symptoms of schizophrenia such as delusions or hallucinations, which limits the generalisability of these findings to humans.

## STUDY 7

### Dépatie & Lal (2001) — The Apomorphine Problem: when a dopamine agonist DOESN'T cause psychosis

**1. Why this study exists**

- If excess dopamine causes schizophrenia, a logical prediction is: **give someone a drug that activates dopamine receptors** (an agonist), and it should worsen or trigger psychotic symptoms.
- **Apomorphine** is a powerful dopamine agonist — it directly stimulates dopamine receptors.
- Dépatie and Lal reviewed the evidence: does apomorphine cause psychosis in healthy people, or worsen symptoms in people who already have schizophrenia?
- The answer turned out to be deeply inconvenient for the dopamine hypothesis.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

This is a **review paper**, not a single experiment — think of it as a courtroom summary of all the evidence for and against apomorphine causing psychosis.

- Dépatie and Lal surveyed the existing scientific literature on apomorphine and psychosis.
- They examined studies where:
  - Healthy controls were given apomorphine — did they develop psychosis?
  - People with existing schizophrenia were given apomorphine — did symptoms worsen?

💡 If dopamine excess = psychosis, then a dopamine agonist like apomorphine should be like pouring petrol on a fire. But the evidence said something very different.

---

**3. What they found**

| Question | Expected (if dopamine hypothesis is correct) | Actual finding |
|---|---|---|
| Does apomorphine cause psychosis in healthy people? | Yes | No (little to no evidence) |
| Does apomorphine worsen psychosis in people with schizophrenia? | Yes | No (little to no evidence) |

- Apomorphine largely **failed to induce or worsen psychotic symptoms**.
- One possible explanation offered: apomorphine has relatively **weak affinity for the D3 receptor** compared to dopamine itself. Indirect agonists (like amphetamine) that increase synaptic dopamine may be more pathologically relevant.
- This suggests the dopamine hypothesis is incomplete — the TYPE of receptor activation matters, not just dopamine levels in general.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This is a **critical challenge** to the simple version of the dopamine hypothesis.
- It forces researchers to refine the hypothesis — it is not just "dopamine excess causes schizophrenia" but a much more complex story involving specific receptors (D1, D2, D3), specific brain regions (prefrontal cortex vs. limbic system), and specific mechanisms.
- Illustrates that the brain's response to dopamine is not a simple on/off switch.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Challenges a dominant theory constructively** — Science progresses when evidence contradicts existing models. This paper helped push researchers toward more sophisticated versions of the dopamine hypothesis.

✅ **Review of multiple studies** — By examining the broader literature rather than one experiment, the findings are more robust than a single study.

❌ **Review paper limitations** — The conclusions depend on the quality and selection of studies reviewed. If only certain studies are included (publication bias — positive results are more likely to be published), the overall picture may be distorted.

❌ **Does not disprove the dopamine hypothesis** — The failure of apomorphine to cause psychosis may be explained by receptor specificity (D3 vs. D2/D1). The hypothesis may still be correct in a more nuanced form.

- Consequence: this study complicates rather than refutes the dopamine hypothesis.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Apomorphine is a **dopamine agonist** (activates dopamine receptors)
- It does NOT worsen symptoms in people with schizophrenia
- This CHALLENGES the simple "excess dopamine = schizophrenia" version of the dopamine hypothesis
- Shows the dopamine hypothesis is **oversimplified**

> Memory hook: Apomorphine = awkward evidence. It should cause psychosis (if dopamine excess = problem), but it doesn't. That's a BIG problem for the simple hypothesis.

📋 **Skeleton answer — evaluation:**

> A limitation of the dopamine hypothesis is highlighted by Dépatie and Lal (2001), who found that apomorphine, a dopamine agonist, does not worsen symptoms in people with schizophrenia and does not trigger psychosis in those without the disorder. This is difficult to explain if excess dopamine directly causes schizophrenic symptoms, as a drug that activates dopamine receptors should logically exacerbate the condition. This suggests that the dopamine hypothesis is over-simplified and that other factors, including the specific receptor types and brain regions involved, must be considered.

## STUDY 8

### Okubo et al. (1997) — PET scans reveal fewer D1 receptors in the prefrontal cortex of people with schizophrenia

**1. Why this study exists**

- If the **dopamine deficiency hypothesis** is correct — that negative and cognitive symptoms are caused by TOO LITTLE dopamine in the prefrontal cortex — then we need direct evidence from living human brains.
- **PET scanning** (Positron Emission Tomography) allows researchers to visualise receptor binding in the brain in real time using radioactive tracers.
- Okubo and colleagues used PET to directly measure **D1 dopamine receptor binding** in the prefrontal cortex of people with schizophrenia vs. healthy controls.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you can take a photograph of dopamine activity in someone's brain — while they are awake and thinking.

- Participants: drug-naive or drug-free patients with schizophrenia, and **matched controls** (same age, sex, background).
- "Drug-naive" means participants had never taken antipsychotic medication — this is crucial, because antipsychotics themselves change receptor levels, which would confound the results.
- A **radioactive tracer** that binds specifically to D1 dopamine receptors was injected intravenously.
- The PET scanner detected where this tracer accumulated in the brain — the more tracer detected in a region, the more D1 receptors are present there.
- The participants also completed the **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST)** — a measure of prefrontal cognitive function (same task used by Egan et al.).
- Researchers looked for correlations between D1 receptor binding, symptom severity, and cognitive performance.

---

**3. What they found**

| Brain region | Finding in schizophrenia patients |
|---|---|
| Prefrontal cortex | **Decreased D1 receptor binding** (fewer D1 receptors) |
| Striatum | No significant difference from controls |

- The reduction in D1 binding in the prefrontal cortex was **correlated with:**
  - Greater severity of **negative symptoms** (e.g. emotional withdrawal)
  - Worse performance on the **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test**
- This supported the idea that dopamine **deficiency** in the prefrontal cortex is responsible for cognitive and negative symptoms.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides **direct neuroimaging evidence** linking dopamine deficiency in the prefrontal cortex to the symptoms of schizophrenia.
- Supports **biological determinism** — the biology of the brain (receptor numbers) predicts symptom severity.
- Confirms that schizophrenia is not one disorder: **positive symptoms** (hallucinations, delusions) may be caused by dopamine excess in the mesolimbic pathway, while **negative and cognitive symptoms** are caused by dopamine deficiency in the prefrontal cortex.
- Shapes modern treatment: targeting prefrontal dopamine deficiency might require different drugs (or doses) than targeting mesolimbic excess.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Direct measurement in living human brains** — Unlike post-mortem studies or animal models, PET scanning measures receptor binding in living, conscious participants. This is highly relevant to understanding the actual experience of schizophrenia.

✅ **Drug-naive participants** — By using participants who had never taken antipsychotics, the study avoided the confound of medication-induced receptor changes. This makes the findings more valid as evidence about schizophrenia itself (not its treatment).

✅ **Correlational evidence supports causality** — The finding that reduced D1 binding correlates with BOTH symptom severity and cognitive performance creates converging evidence for the same mechanism.

❌ **Correlation ≠ causation** — The study shows that low D1 binding and negative symptoms go together. But does low D1 binding cause the symptoms, or do the symptoms (or another factor) cause the low D1 binding?

- Consequence: we cannot conclude from this study alone that dopamine deficiency causes schizophrenia.

❌ **Small sample** — PET studies are expensive and time-consuming. Sample sizes tend to be small, limiting statistical power and generalisability.

❌ **Deterministic framing risks stigma** — Presenting schizophrenia as determined by receptor levels may reduce hope for recovery and reinforce the idea that patients cannot influence their own outcomes. This ignores evidence that therapy, lifestyle, and environment can modify brain chemistry.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- PET scan study measuring **D1 receptor binding** in the prefrontal cortex
- Found **DECREASED** D1 binding in schizophrenia patients
- Correlated with negative symptoms and poor WCST performance
- Supports **dopamine deficiency hypothesis** (not excess hypothesis)
- Drug-naive participants = important methodological strength

> Memory hook: PET = "photograph of the problem." Fewer D1 receptors in the prefrontal cortex = less dopamine signalling = more negative symptoms and worse thinking.

📋 **Skeleton answer — AO3:**

> Support for the dopamine deficiency hypothesis comes from Okubo et al. (1997), who used PET brain imaging to measure D1 dopamine receptor binding in drug-naive patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls. They found decreased D1 binding in the prefrontal cortex, which correlated significantly with the severity of negative symptoms and poor performance on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test. This supports the idea that dopamine deficiency in the prefrontal cortex contributes to the cognitive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia, and demonstrates that the biological basis of schizophrenia can be directly measured in the living human brain.

---

## STUDY 9

### Allen et al. (2007) — fMRI shows voice-hearers cannot tell their own voice from someone else's

**1. Why this study exists**

- Frith's cognitive theory argues that **auditory hallucinations** occur because people with schizophrenia misattribute their own inner speech to an external source.
- That is a bold theoretical claim. Allen et al. wanted to test it directly using **brain imaging** while people were actively listening to their own voice vs. someone else's voice.
- If the self-monitoring error theory is correct, voice-hearers should show **different brain activity** when processing their own voice versus an external voice — or specifically, they should fail to distinguish between the two.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

You are lying inside an fMRI scanner — a loud, tunnel-like machine that measures blood flow in your brain in real time.

- Three groups of participants:
  1. People with schizophrenia who **hear voices** (auditory verbal hallucinations — AVH)
  2. People with schizophrenia who do **NOT** hear voices
  3. Healthy controls
- Each participant had their own voice **pre-recorded** reading a series of words.
- During the scan, they listened to words that were either:
  - Spoken by themselves or by someone else
  - **Undistorted** or **distorted** (digitally altered to sound less recognisable)
- This created four conditions: own-undistorted, own-distorted, other-undistorted, other-distorted.
- After each word, participants indicated: "Was that my voice or someone else's?"
- The fMRI measured brain activity — particularly in the **left superior temporal gyrus** (a key region for processing auditory information and distinguishing self from other).

---

**3. What they found**

| Group | Behaviour | Brain activity pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy controls | Correctly distinguished own vs. other voice | Different brain activity across all four conditions |
| Non-hallucinating schizophrenia patients | Reasonably correct | Showed some differentiation |
| Voice-hearing schizophrenia patients | **Misidentified own voice as someone else's** more often | Brain activity was **the same regardless of whose voice it was** |

- Voice-hearers were **more likely to say their own voice belonged to someone else**.
- Crucially, their brain **did not differentiate** between hearing their own voice and an external voice — the neural signature was identical.
- Control participants showed distinct neural responses for each condition — their brains knew the difference, even when voices were distorted.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This is **direct empirical support** for Frith's self-monitoring error theory.
- It shows that voice-hearing is not "imaginary" — there is a **real and measurable neurological difference** in how voice-hearers process auditory information.
- It validates the cognitive explanation with biological evidence — a strength of the approach.
- Implications for treatment: targeting the brain regions involved in self-monitoring (e.g., through neurostimulation like TMS — transcranial magnetic stimulation) might reduce voice-hearing.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Directly tests the cognitive theory** — Rather than just correlating symptoms with brain data, this study creates specific experimental conditions that map directly onto the self-monitoring error hypothesis. This gives it high construct validity.

✅ **Neuroimaging provides biological grounding** — fMRI data shows that the cognitive deficit has a measurable neural basis, bridging the cognitive and biological explanations.

✅ **Three-group design** — Including both hallucinating AND non-hallucinating schizophrenia patients allows researchers to isolate the effect of voice-hearing specifically, rather than just "having schizophrenia."

❌ **Small, self-selected sample** — People who volunteer for fMRI studies may not be representative of all voice-hearers. Those with severe symptoms may be unable or unwilling to participate.

- Consequence: findings may not generalise to the full range of people with schizophrenia and auditory hallucinations.

❌ **Correlation in the brain scan data** — Even though the fMRI shows different patterns of brain activity, this does not prove that the brain difference CAUSES voice-hearing. The abnormal brain activity could be a consequence of years of experiencing hallucinations, not their cause.

❌ **Artificial laboratory conditions** — Listening to pre-recorded words in a scanner is not the same as the spontaneous, intrusive voice-hearing that people experience in daily life. Ecological validity is limited.

- Consequence: laboratory measurements of self-monitoring may not fully capture what happens during a real hallucinatory episode.

❌ **Reductionist** — The study shows one cognitive-neural mechanism but does not explain why some people develop this self-monitoring error in the first place, or why only some people with this deficit experience schizophrenia.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- fMRI study with three groups: voice-hearers, non-hallucinating schizophrenia, healthy controls
- Voice-hearers **misidentified their own voice** as belonging to someone else more often
- Their brains showed **identical activity** regardless of whether voice was their own or external
- Controls showed distinct neural responses across conditions
- Supports Frith's **self-monitoring error** theory

🧠 Quick check: What does "self-monitoring error" mean? Failing to correctly label your own inner speech as self-generated — so it feels like it comes from outside your mind.

📋 **Skeleton answer — AO3:**

> Experimental support for Frith's cognitive explanation comes from Allen et al. (2007), who used fMRI to show that people with schizophrenia who hear voices are more likely to misattribute their own pre-recorded voice as belonging to someone else. Furthermore, brain activity in voice-hearers was identical regardless of whether they were listening to their own or another person's voice, whereas control participants showed distinct neural responses across conditions. This demonstrates that auditory hallucinations have a neurological basis in disrupted self-monitoring and provides empirical support for Frith's theory. However, as the sample was self-selected and laboratory conditions were artificial, the ecological validity of the findings should be considered.

## STUDY 10

### Garety et al. (1991) — The Beads Task: people with schizophrenia jump to conclusions with less evidence

**1. Why this study exists**

- Frith's cognitive theory also proposes that delusions — bizarre false beliefs — develop because people with schizophrenia make **reasoning errors**.
- Specifically, they may be biased toward drawing conclusions from very little evidence and resistant to updating those conclusions when contradictory evidence appears.
- Garety, Hemsley, and Wessely (1991) designed an elegant experiment — the **Beads Task** — to test this "jumping to conclusions" (JTC) bias directly.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you walk into a psychology lab. The researcher shows you two jars of coloured beads.

- **Jar A:** 85 pink beads and 15 green beads
- **Jar B:** 85 green beads and 15 pink beads

The researcher hides both jars behind a screen. They are going to draw beads one at a time from ONE of the jars and show you each bead's colour.

- Your job: work out which jar the beads are being drawn from.
- You can ask to see as many beads as you like before making your decision — or you can make a decision at any point.

💡 Think of it like a detective deciding whether the suspect is guilty based on clues. A careful detective waits for more clues. A hasty detective jumps to a verdict after just one piece of evidence.

- Three groups of participants:
  - People with schizophrenia (some with delusions, some without)
  - People with other psychiatric diagnoses (comparison group)
  - Healthy controls
- The key measure: **how many beads** did each person need to see before making a decision?

---

**3. What they found**

| Group | Average beads needed before decision |
|---|---|
| Healthy controls | ~5 or more beads |
| People with schizophrenia (especially with delusions) | **1–2 beads** |

- People with schizophrenia requested significantly **fewer beads** before committing to a decision.
- They were also **more likely to switch their decision** based on a single contradictory bead.
  - Example: after four consecutive green beads (suggesting Jar B), one pink bead would cause them to switch their guess to Jar A.
- This pattern was most pronounced in participants with **delusions** (fixed false beliefs), linking the JTC bias specifically to the symptom it is meant to explain.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides experimental evidence that people with schizophrenia have a **specific reasoning bias** — not just "they think differently," but a measurable, quantifiable difference in how they process probabilistic information.
- This "jumping to conclusions" bias directly explains how **delusions form and persist**:
  - A person encounters an ambiguous event (a stranger looks at them)
  - They immediately conclude: "That person is monitoring me for the government"
  - Even when contradictory evidence appears, they do not update their belief
- Supports the cognitive explanation with objective, experimental data.
- This is now one of the **most replicated findings** in schizophrenia research. A 1999 meta-analysis by Garety and Freeman found that 7 out of 8 beads task studies replicated the JTC bias in people with delusions.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

✅ **Objective, quantifiable measure** — The number of beads requested before making a decision is a precise, numerical outcome. This avoids subjective bias in measurement and allows reliable comparison across groups.

✅ **High internal validity** — Participants are randomly exposed to the same jar sequence under controlled conditions. Any difference in decision-making is attributable to the participant's reasoning, not external variation.

✅ **Directly maps to a real symptom** — The JTC bias mirrors exactly how delusions form: snap judgements with minimal evidence, resistant to disconfirmation. This gives the study strong face validity.

✅ **Well-replicated** — The Garety et al. beads task has been used in hundreds of subsequent studies, and the JTC bias has been consistently found in people with delusions across different countries and cultures.

❌ **Low ecological validity** — Choosing between two jars of beads in a quiet lab is nothing like the complex, emotionally charged situations in which delusions actually develop. People do not form the belief that the CIA is monitoring them by looking at beads.

- Consequence: the JTC bias measured in the lab may not perfectly capture the reasoning errors that occur in real life.

❌ **Does not explain WHY the bias exists** — The study shows the bias exists, but not why some people have it. Is it due to dopamine dysregulation? Traumatic experience? Anxiety? The cognitive explanation is incomplete without this upstream cause.

- Consequence: the cognitive explanation is only a partial account of schizophrenia.

❌ **Participant selection issues** — As the textbook notes, people who agree to take part in laboratory studies may be different from people who refuse (more educated, less severely ill, more motivated). This limits generalisability to the full schizophrenia population.

❌ **Same cognitive bias appears in other conditions** — The JTC bias is also found in ADHD, anxiety disorders, and autism spectrum disorder. It is not specific to schizophrenia, which weakens its explanatory power for THIS disorder specifically.

- Consequence: JTC may be a general cognitive vulnerability rather than the cause of schizophrenia's delusions specifically.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- The beads task: two jars, 85:15 ratio of two colours
- People with schizophrenia (especially with delusions) needed **fewer beads** to make a decision
- This is called the **"jumping to conclusions" (JTC) bias**
- Directly explains how **delusions form** — snap judgements with minimal evidence
- One of the most replicated findings in schizophrenia cognitive research

🧠 Quick check: How does the JTC bias explain paranoid delusions? A person sees ambiguous social behaviour and immediately concludes it is threatening, without gathering enough evidence to be sure.

📋 **Skeleton answer — 6 marks (describe the study):**

> Garety et al. (1991) investigated reasoning biases in people with schizophrenia using a probabilistic inference task known as the beads task. Participants were shown two hidden jars of coloured beads — one with 85 pink and 15 green beads, the other with 85 green and 15 pink — and were shown one bead at a time drawn from one of the jars. They had to decide which jar the beads were being drawn from, and could request as many beads as they wished before deciding. People with schizophrenia, particularly those with delusions, requested significantly fewer beads before making their decision than healthy controls, demonstrating a "jumping to conclusions" bias. They were also more likely to change their mind based on a single contradictory bead. This suggests that people with schizophrenia make decisions based on insufficient evidence, which may explain how delusional beliefs are formed and maintained.

📋 **Skeleton answer — evaluation:**

> A strength of Garety et al. (1991) is that it provides objective, quantifiable evidence of a specific cognitive bias in schizophrenia. The number of beads requested is a precise measure, giving the study high internal validity. Furthermore, the JTC bias directly mirrors the way delusional beliefs form — from snap judgements made on minimal evidence — giving the study strong face validity and supporting Frith's cognitive explanation. However, a weakness is that the artificial laboratory task has low ecological validity, as it does not replicate the emotionally charged, complex circumstances in which real delusions typically develop. This means the beads task may underestimate or simplify the role of reasoning biases in real-world psychosis.

## ISSUES & DEBATES CHEAT SHEET

| Debate | How it applies to this chapter |
|---|---|
| **Nature vs. nurture** | Genetics and dopamine = nature; life stressors, culture, drug use = nurture. MZ concordance < 100% shows BOTH matter. |
| **Reductionism vs. holism** | Dopamine hypothesis is reductionist (one chemical). CBT + lifestyle factors = more holistic. |
| **Determinism vs. free will** | Biological explanations (PET scan receptor data) are deterministic. BUT therapy shows free will can override biology. |
| **Individual vs. situational** | Cognitive deficits (JTC, self-monitoring errors) = individual. Stress and culture triggering episodes = situational. |
| **Idiographic vs. nomothetic** | Twin studies, beads tasks = nomothetic (group data). St Clair family, case reports = idiographic. |
