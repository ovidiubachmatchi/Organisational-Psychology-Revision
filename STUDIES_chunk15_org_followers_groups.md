## Study 1

### Kouzes & Posner (1987–ongoing) — Five Practices of Exemplary Leadership + the LPI

---

**1. Why this study exists**

For most of history, people assumed leaders were **born, not made** — you either had "it" or you didn't.
Kouzes and Posner wanted to smash that idea.

They asked: *What do ordinary people do when they lead at their very best?*
Their goal was to find **learnable, observable behaviours** that anyone could develop — not personality traits you're stuck with at birth.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine a researcher sits down with hundreds of managers and asks one simple question:
> "Tell me about a time you led something brilliantly. What did YOU actually DO?"

That is literally how it started. Over **30 years**, Kouzes and Posner collected:
- Thousands of **case studies** (rich real-world stories)
- Hundreds of **in-depth interviews**
- **Millions** of survey questionnaires

💡 Think of it like a massive crowdsourced recipe book. They asked leaders "what are your best ingredients?" — and after reading thousands of recipes, five ingredients kept appearing in every great one.

**Phase 1 — Qualitative Discovery:**
They spotted patterns in all the interviews and case studies.
Five consistent clusters of behaviour emerged. These became the **Five Practices of Exemplary Leadership**.

**Phase 2 — Building the LPI:**
They then built the **Leadership Practices Inventory (LPI)** — a 30-question psychometric questionnaire to *measure* those five practices scientifically.

The LPI has **two parts**:
- **LPI Self**: The leader rates themselves (how often do I do this?)
- **LPI Observer**: Up to 10 other people (bosses, peers, subordinates) rate the leader

Both use the **same 30 questions**, rated on a **10-point scale** (1 = almost never → 10 = almost always).
Six questions per practice.

**Sample questions:**
| Statement | Practice |
|-----------|---------|
| "Sets a personal example of what is expected" | Model the Way |
| "Seeks challenging opportunities to test skills" | Challenge the Process |
| "Describes a compelling image of the future" | Inspire a Shared Vision |
| "Treats people with dignity and respect" | Enable Others to Act |
| "Expresses confidence in people's abilities" | Encourage the Heart |

---

**3. What they found — The Five Practices**

| Practice | Core Idea | Key Behaviour |
|---------|-----------|--------------|
| **Model the Way** | Be the example you want to see | Stay honest, humble, walk the talk |
| **Inspire a Shared Vision** | Paint a vivid picture of a better future | Get others excited about *your* dream |
| **Challenge the Process** | Don't wait — seek change, take risks | Trial and error is how great leaders grow |
| **Enable Others to Act** | Empower your team | Foster collaboration, build trust |
| **Encourage the Heart** | Leadership is emotional | Praise, rewards, recognition — make heroes of others |

> 🧠 "Credibility is the single most important asset a leader can have. If you don't believe in the messenger, you won't believe the message." — Kouzes & Posner

**Validity study (Kouzes & Posner, 1993):**
- Sample: **2,876 managers and observers** across many organisations
- Finding: Self-ratings and observer ratings matched in **rank order**
- No significant differences by **gender** or **type of organisation**
- Conclusion: The LPI is **reliable and valid** across different contexts

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Proves leadership is **not about who you are** — it's about **what you do**
- Gives organisations a practical tool to **measure and develop** leadership behaviour
- Can identify gaps: e.g., a leader who never "encourages the heart" can be coached on recognition
- Over 30 years of data = one of the most extensively validated leadership instruments in existence

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Uses both qualitative AND quantitative methods**
→ WHY it's a strength: Case studies give rich context; the questionnaire gives measurable, comparable data
→ CONSEQUENCE: Results are both deep and generalisable — best of both worlds

✅ **Highly validated (1993 study)**
→ WHY: 2,876 participants, no gender bias, consistent across organisations
→ CONSEQUENCE: Researchers can trust the LPI measures what it claims to measure (high validity)

✅ **Practical application**
→ WHY: The LPI is actually used in real workplaces right now
→ CONSEQUENCE: Theory directly improves performance and leadership development

❌ **Social desirability bias**
→ WHY: People completing self-reports may lie or exaggerate — leaders want to look good; observers may have personal grudges
→ CONSEQUENCE: Scores may not reflect real behaviour, lowering the validity of results

❌ **Western cultural bias**
→ WHY: The five practices are built on Western ideas of leadership (assertive, forward-looking, visible)
→ CONSEQUENCE: The LPI may not work for collectivist cultures where humility or deference is valued — limits generalisability

❌ **Forced-choice questionnaire**
→ WHY: A 10-point rating doesn't capture nuance or *why* a leader behaves a certain way
→ CONSEQUENCE: Results can be misleading if the participant doesn't fit the scale — reduces validity

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- 30+ years, case studies + interviews + questionnaires → five practices
- LPI = 30 questions, 10-point scale, LPI Self + LPI Observer
- 1993 validation: 2,876 managers, no gender bias
- Key weakness: social desirability + cultural differences

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe):**
> Kouzes and Posner researched leadership over 30 years using qualitative methods (case studies, interviews) and quantitative methods (surveys) with thousands of managers. From this they identified five practices of exemplary leadership: Model the Way, Inspire a Shared Vision, Challenge the Process, Enable Others to Act, and Encourage the Heart. They then developed the LPI — a 30-item psychometric questionnaire with a 10-point rating scale, completed by the leader themselves (LPI Self) and up to 10 observers (LPI Observer). Kouzes and Posner (1993) validated the LPI using 2,876 managers and found it was reliable with no significant gender or organisational differences.

## Study 2

### Kelley (1988) — Five Followership Styles

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Psychology had obsessed over leaders for decades.
But **who leads the followers?** Who ARE the followers?

Robert Kelley — writing in *Harvard Business Review* in 1988 — argued that for every leader in an organisation, there are **many more followers**, yet followers get no training, no theory, no respect.

He wanted to map out *what kinds of followers exist* and what makes some followers far more effective than others.

> 🧠 "Most people in their careers are more likely to be followers than leaders, yet they have neither the glamour nor the training that leaders have." — Kelley

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Kelley observed workers across organisations and examined existing literature on follower behaviour.
He wasn't running a lab experiment — this was **theoretical model building** supported by observation and research review.

He identified **two key dimensions** that separate followers:

**Dimension 1: Critical Thinking**
- Does the follower *question, reason, and analyse* what they're told?
- Or do they just *accept everything without question*?

**Dimension 2: Active/Passive Participation**
- Does the follower *take initiative, get involved, own their work*?
- Or do they *sit back and wait* for others to act?

💡 Imagine a **grid** — one axis is "thinking for yourself" (low→high), the other is "engagement" (passive→active).
Plotting where a person falls on that grid gives you their **followership style**.

---

**3. What they found — The Five Styles**

| Style | Thinking | Participation | Key Trait | Problem |
|-------|---------|--------------|----------|---------|
| **Passive (Sheep)** | Low | Low | Does what they're told, then stops | No initiative, no responsibility |
| **Alienated** | High | Low | Cynical, critical — but does nothing | Whispers on sidelines, resentful |
| **Pragmatic Survivor** | Medium | Medium | Shifts style to what's safe | Cautious, "better safe than sorry" |
| **Conformist (Yes Person)** | Low | High | Always obedient, never questions | Kills innovation; enables bad decisions |
| **Exemplary** | High | High | Energetic, assertive, challenges the leader | None — this is the ideal |

> 🧠 **Exemplary followers** are risk-takers, problem-solvers, honest and courageous. They don't see the leader as a hero — they see them as a person who manages change.

**Supporting evidence — Favara (2009):**
- Sample: 175 workers in one organisation
- Method: Three questionnaires measuring followership style, job satisfaction, and job performance
- Finding: **Significant positive correlation** between exemplary followership and both job satisfaction and performance
- Supports Kelley's claim that exemplary followers perform better

**Cross-cultural challenge — Oyetunji (2013):**
- Studied lecturers in **Botswana universities**
- Found: **Passive followers** rated themselves as the best performers — not exemplary ones
- Reason suggested: In Botswana culture, *challenging leaders* is socially unacceptable
- Implication: Kelley's model may not generalise beyond Western cultures

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Shifts organisational focus from just finding **great leaders** to also developing **great followers**
- Helps managers **identify** which type of follower they have — and what they need
- Suggests that even without strong leadership, **exemplary followers** can drive results
- Practical: organisations can design training to move workers toward more exemplary styles

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Research support (Favara, 2009)**
→ WHY: Empirical study with 175 workers directly tested the theory
→ CONSEQUENCE: Shows exemplary followership → better performance, giving the theory reliability

✅ **Novel focus — followership, not leadership**
→ WHY: First major theory to classify follower types systematically
→ CONSEQUENCE: Gave organisations a completely new lens to improve workplace effectiveness

❌ **Western cultural bias (Oyetunji, 2013)**
→ WHY: The exemplary follower (challenges leaders, thinks independently) only works in cultures that value that
→ CONSEQUENCE: Theory cannot be generalised to collectivist or high-power-distance cultures — limited external validity

❌ **Reductionist — only 5 types**
→ WHY: Real people are messy; someone might be exemplary in one situation but alienated in another
→ CONSEQUENCE: Pigeonholing people into 5 categories ignores situational factors and individual complexity

❌ **Ignores situational factors (link to Milgram)**
→ WHY: Milgram showed that *situational pressure* overrides personality — obedience is situational, not just personal
→ CONSEQUENCE: Kelley's claim that followers stay in a "type" may be wrong — the situation may change their style entirely

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Two dimensions: critical thinking (high/low) + participation (active/passive)
- Five styles: Passive, Alienated, Pragmatic, Conformist, Exemplary
- Favara (2009): 175 workers, exemplary = better performance (supports)
- Oyetunji (2013): Botswana, passive = best performers (cross-cultural weakness)
- Reductionism: only 5 types ignores situation

📋 **Skeleton answer (evaluate):**
> A strength of Kelley's theory is research support from Favara (2009) who found a significant positive correlation between exemplary followership and job performance in 175 workers, supporting the theory's reliability. However, a weakness is that the five styles may not apply across all cultures. Oyetunji (2013) found that passive followers in Botswana rated themselves as best performers, suggesting that exemplary followership — which involves challenging leaders — may be culturally inappropriate in non-Western contexts, limiting generalisation. Furthermore, the theory is reductionist, as reducing a follower's complex behaviour to just five types ignores situational variables, which Milgram's research shows can be more powerful than individual characteristics.

## Study 3

### Tuckman (1965) & Tuckman and Jensen (2010) — Stages of Group Development

---

**1. Why this study exists**

When a group of strangers is thrown together at work, they don't immediately become a high-performing team.
There's an awkward phase. Then arguments. Then things settle. Then they finally work well.

Tuckman (1965) asked: **Is this process predictable?**
Can we map the stages a group reliably goes through on its way to effectiveness?

If yes, managers could prepare for those stages — especially the painful ones.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Tuckman didn't do a lab experiment. Instead, he acted like a **literature detective**.

**Phase 1 — Tuckman (1965): Review of 55 articles**

He reviewed **55 published studies** on small group development across a wide range of settings:
- Therapy groups
- Training groups
- Task-based groups
- Natural groups

He read each study carefully, looking for **common patterns** in how groups evolved over time.

💡 Imagine reading 55 different diaries written by 55 different groups — and noticing that almost every group goes through the same emotional journey, just at different speeds.

From this, Tuckman identified **four stages**: Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing

**Phase 2 — Tuckman and Jensen (2010): Review of 22 more articles**

Decades later, Tuckman revisited his model with Jensen.
They reviewed **22 new articles** that had attempted to study or test the original four stages.

- Most supported the four stages
- Two studies identified a fifth stage: the **ending** of the group
- The fifth stage was added: **Adjourning**

Note: Only **one study (Runkel, 1971)** ever set out specifically to *empirically test* Tuckman's model — and it mainly supported it, though its methodology was prone to observer bias.

---

**3. What they found — The Five Stages**

| Stage | What Happens | Key Challenge | Risk |
|-------|-------------|--------------|------|
| **Forming** | Members get to know each other, test the boundaries | Uncertainty, finding a leader | Dependence on authority |
| **Storming** | Conflict emerges, personalities clash, subgroups form | Getting through conflict | Resentment if avoided |
| **Norming** | Conflict resolves, roles confirmed, unity builds | Avoiding groupthink | Dominant members silencing others |
| **Performing** | Mature, organised, creative, effective teamwork | Maintaining momentum | Complacency |
| **Adjourning** | Goals completed, group disbands, members reassigned | Managing the emotional end | Grief/loss for members |

> 🧠 Storming is the **most critical** stage. If a team skips it, unresolved tensions can destroy them later. If they get through it, they unlock creativity.

> 🧠 Adjourning was added because research showed that the **"death" of a group** is psychologically significant — especially the connections made between members.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Managers can **predict and prepare** for group tensions (storming is normal — don't panic!)
- Helps leaders know *when* to intervene and *when* to step back
- Explains why newly formed teams underperform — they're just **forming and storming**
- The model is used globally in management training, team-building workshops, and HR

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Dynamic model — updated with new evidence**
→ WHY: Tuckman added the fifth stage based on 22 new review articles — he was willing to revise the model
→ CONSEQUENCE: The ability to update based on evidence increases the validity of the theory over time

✅ **Broad base — based on 55 original articles**
→ WHY: Covering many types of groups (therapy, task, natural) adds breadth
→ CONSEQUENCE: Makes the model more likely to generalise across different group contexts

❌ **Very little direct empirical evidence**
→ WHY: Tuckman himself admitted more empirical testing was needed; the 2010 update was based mostly on *review articles*, not original experiments
→ CONSEQUENCE: The model lacks scientific rigour — it's hard to say it's been "proven" rather than "observed"

❌ **Linear model may be unrealistic**
→ WHY: Real groups don't always move cleanly from one stage to the next — they can jump back and forth
→ CONSEQUENCE: Presenting stages as fixed and sequential reduces the validity and real-world applicability of the model

❌ **Observer bias in Runkel (1971)**
→ WHY: The one study designed to test the model directly used observation — which is prone to subjectivity
→ CONSEQUENCE: The main empirical support is methodologically weak

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- 1965: reviewed 55 articles → 4 stages (Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing)
- 2010: reviewed 22 more articles with Jensen → added Adjourning (5th stage)
- Storming = most critical stage
- Weakness: stages overlap in reality, almost no direct empirical testing
- Only one study (Runkel, 1971) directly tested the model

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe):**
> Tuckman (1965) reviewed 55 articles on small group development and identified four stages: Forming (uncertainty, finding leadership), Storming (conflict as personalities emerge), Norming (roles confirmed, unity established), and Performing (mature, creative, effective teamwork). Tuckman and Jensen (2010) then reviewed 22 further articles and added a fifth stage — Adjourning — where the group disbands after achieving its goals, recognising the emotional significance of the group ending. The model is dynamic as it was revised based on new theoretical and empirical evidence.

## Study 4

### Belbin (1981) — Nine Team Roles and the Self-Perception Inventory

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Companies assumed: if you hire the **smartest people**, you'll have the best team.

Meredith Belbin ran experiments at Henley Management College in the 1970s and found this was **completely wrong**.
Teams of highly intelligent people often *failed*.
Teams with a **balance of different behavioural roles** succeeded.

His question: **What different roles do people naturally play in teams — and which combination creates success?**

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Belbin ran business simulation games at Henley in the 1970s — like a real-world strategy game where teams competed to make business decisions.

As teams played, Belbin and colleagues **observed** their behaviour.
Alongside observation, participants completed **psychometric tests** (personality and ability assessments).

💡 Think of it like watching football teams play, while also knowing each player's stats. Over time you notice: teams that win always have a mix of certain player types. Teams stacked with strikers and no defenders always lose.

After extensive observation and analysis, Belbin identified **8 clusters of behaviour** that determined team success.
Later, when he applied the theory in real industry settings, a **9th role** emerged: the **Specialist** (who brings deep technical knowledge).

He then created the **Belbin Self-Perception Inventory (BSPI)**:
- Participants answer **forced-choice statements** about their own behaviours
- Up to **6 observers** also rate the individual
- The self and observer ratings are **correlated** to check validity

---

**3. What they found — The Nine Roles**

| Role | Type | Key Strength | Key Weakness |
|------|------|-------------|-------------|
| **Plant** | Thinking | Creative, solves complex problems | Ignores group, focused on own ideas |
| **Resource Investigator** | Social | Enthusiastic, explores opportunities, great networker | Enthusiasm fades fast |
| **Co-ordinator** | Social | Calm, delegates well, spots talent | May manipulate to get own way |
| **Shaper** | Action | Goal-driven, overcomes obstacles, decisive | Aggressive, argumentative |
| **Monitor Evaluator** | Thinking | Logical, critical thinker, accurate | Slow, overly critical, boring |
| **Implementer** | Action | Reliable, disciplined, gets things done | Rigid, resists change |
| **Teamworker** | Social | Diplomatic, caring, popular, reduces conflict | Indecisive under pressure |
| **Completer Finisher** | Action | Detail-focused, perfectionist, meets deadlines | Creates anxiety, doesn't delegate |
| **Specialist** | Thinking | Deep expert knowledge, respected | Anti-social, stubborn about their field |

> 🧠 No single role is "best". A great team needs a **balance** of roles. The Plant brings ideas; the Implementer builds them; the Completer Finisher makes sure they're perfect.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Used worldwide in HR, team-building, and recruitment
- Helps organisations build **balanced teams** rather than just hiring for intelligence or CV
- Identifies **gaps**: if a team has no Monitor Evaluator, decisions may be rushed; if it has no Teamworker, morale may collapse
- The BSPI gives individuals self-awareness about how they contribute

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Triangulation — observation + psychometrics + observer ratings**
→ WHY: Using multiple methods catches what one method alone would miss; observer reports add external validity
→ CONSEQUENCE: The findings are more robust because they're converged across methods

✅ **Nomothetic, scientific approach**
→ WHY: Quantitative psychometric data allows comparison, replication, and objective scoring
→ CONSEQUENCE: Results are reliable and can be compared across organisations worldwide

❌ **Social desirability bias in self-report**
→ WHY: Forced-choice statements on the BSPI may not reflect real behaviour; people present their best self
→ CONSEQUENCE: Self-ratings may be inaccurate, lowering validity

❌ **Observer bias**
→ WHY: Observers may be influenced by personal conflicts or external situational variables
→ CONSEQUENCE: The observer correction mechanism may itself be unreliable

❌ **Western cultural bias**
→ WHY: The nine roles are built on Western workplace norms (e.g., the Shaper's assertiveness is valued in individualist cultures)
→ CONSEQUENCE: Roles may not translate to collectivist cultures where individual self-promotion is inappropriate — limits generalisability

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Research at Henley in 1970s: observation + psychometrics → 9 roles
- Key finding: *balance* beats intelligence
- Specialist was added later when theory was applied in real industry
- BSPI = forced-choice + up to 6 observer reports → correlated for validity
- Weaknesses: social desirability, observer bias, Western cultural bias

📋 **Skeleton answer (evaluate):**
> A strength of Belbin's theory is its use of triangulation — combining observation, psychometric tests, and observer statements to measure team roles. This multi-method approach increases the reliability and validity of findings. However, a weakness is that the BSPI relies on self-reports which are subject to social desirability bias, as participants may not report their true behaviour. Furthermore, Belbin's nine roles reflect Western workplace norms and may not apply to collectivist cultures, limiting the theory's generalisability.

## Study 5

### Janis (1971) — Groupthink: Theory, Symptoms, and Real-World Examples

---

**1. Why this study exists**

In 1961, President Kennedy — surrounded by his *best and brightest* advisors — approved the **Bay of Pigs invasion**, a plan to overthrow Cuba's government. It was a catastrophic failure.

Irving Janis was baffled. How could the most intelligent, highly trained group of decision-makers in the world make such an obviously flawed decision?

His answer: **Groupthink** — a psychological phenomenon where the desire for group harmony overrides rational, critical thinking.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Janis didn't run an experiment. He used **retrospective case study analysis** — studying the historical records of major political decision-making disasters.

💡 Think of Janis as a psychological detective reviewing CCTV footage of crimes that already happened. He went back through minutes, memos, testimonies, and historical records of:
- The **Bay of Pigs invasion** (1961)
- The decision to ignore warnings before **Pearl Harbor** (1941)
- Later researchers applied his framework to the **Challenger disaster** (1986)

From these case studies, Janis built a theory: he identified the *conditions* that make groupthink likely, and the *symptoms* that reveal it's happening.

**Conditions that trigger groupthink:**
1. **High group cohesiveness** (tight-knit group with strong loyalty)
2. **Group isolation** (no outside voices allowed in)
3. **Stressful crisis** (pressure forces quick decisions)
4. **Strong, directive leader** (signals what answer they want)

---

**3. What they found — The 8 Symptoms of Groupthink**

| Symptom | What it looks like | Real example |
|---------|------------------|-------------|
| **Illusion of invulnerability** | "We can't fail" — over-optimism, risk-taking | NASA believed Challenger was safe despite warnings |
| **Collective rationalisation** | Dismiss warnings without analysis | Japan "would never dare" attack Pearl Harbor |
| **Belief in moral superiority** | "We're the good guys — our cause is right" | Justifying ethically questionable decisions |
| **Stereotyping outgroups** | Enemy seen as weak, stupid, or evil | Underestimating Cuban forces |
| **Pressure on dissenters** | Anyone who doubts is pressured to conform | Thiokol engineers pressured to approve Challenger launch |
| **Self-censorship** | Members stop voicing doubts | Engineers stopped raising concerns after repeated pressure |
| **Illusion of unanimity** | Silence = agreement (even when it doesn't) | Moore was told "everyone agrees" on the Challenger launch |
| **Mindguards** | Members actively shield group from contradictory info | Experts excluded from the Challenger final decision meeting |

> 🧠 **Golkar (2013)** extended Janis: high stress + low self-esteem + past failures → even more vulnerable to groupthink

**Strategies to prevent groupthink:**

| Strategy | How it works | Limitation |
|---------|------------|-----------|
| Invite outside experts | Challenges group assumptions | Members may become dishonest in front of outsiders |
| Devil's advocate role | One member must challenge every decision | Only works if group believes the person means it |
| Leader stays neutral | Leader doesn't signal preferred answer | Members may still guess what they want |
| Critical evaluator role | Everyone must air their doubts explicitly | Can make process very slow and dysfunctional |

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Explains disasters that *shouldn't* have happened with smart people in charge
- Applied to: Bay of Pigs, Pearl Harbor, Challenger disaster, Iraq War decisions
- Gives organisations **practical tools** to prevent faulty group decisions
- Changes how we run meetings, boards, crisis committees

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Real-world application — explains actual disasters**
→ WHY: Theory was built on real political/organisational case studies, not artificial lab settings
→ CONSEQUENCE: If it explains real disasters, the prevention strategies can be applied to real organisations — high practical value

✅ **Holistic — considers both individual AND situational factors**
→ WHY: Janis acknowledged both situational factors (group cohesion, stress) and personality factors (strong leader, low self-esteem via Golkar)
→ CONSEQUENCE: More complete explanation than theories that only look at one type of factor

❌ **Lack of empirical research (Aldag & Fuller, 1993)**
→ WHY: Support comes mainly from retrospective case studies — which rely on memory and are prone to hindsight bias
→ CONSEQUENCE: We can't be sure the *pattern* Janis saw is real, or if he was finding what he expected to find — lowers reliability

❌ **Case studies from individualist, Western cultures**
→ WHY: Bay of Pigs, Pearl Harbor — all US-based political decisions; collectivist cultures may have very different group dynamics
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot generalise groupthink to all cultures — limited external validity

❌ **Overused / oversimplified in popular culture (Fuller & Aldag, 1998)**
→ WHY: "Groupthink" is now applied to every bad decision, ignoring other explanations
→ CONSEQUENCE: The term has lost precision; other causal factors (bad information, time pressure) get ignored

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Janis (1971) built theory from case studies (Bay of Pigs, Pearl Harbor)
- 8 symptoms (know at least 4 clearly)
- Conditions: cohesion, isolation, stress, directive leader
- Strategies (know 2 with limitations)
- Weakness: retrospective case studies = hindsight bias + limited to individualist cultures

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe):**
> Janis (1971) explained groupthink as occurring when the desire for group agreement becomes so strong that it overrides objective decision-making. It is most likely when a group is highly cohesive, isolated from outside information, under stress, and has a directive leader. Janis identified eight symptoms including the illusion of invulnerability, self-censorship, mindguards, and the illusion of unanimity. These were demonstrated in real cases such as the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961) and the Challenger disaster (1986). Janis suggested strategies to prevent groupthink such as assigning a devil's advocate role or inviting outside experts to challenge group decisions.

## Study 6

### Forsyth (2006) / Kerr et al. (1992) — Cognitive Limitations and Errors in Group Decision-Making

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Groups still make bad decisions — even without the extreme social pressure of groupthink.

Forsyth (2006), drawing on Kerr et al.'s (1992) framework, asked: **what cognitive (thinking) errors do people make in group settings?**

While groupthink is about social pressure killing critical thinking, cognitive errors are about the **mental shortcuts and biases** that distort reasoning — even in low-pressure situations.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Kerr et al. (1992) reviewed psychological research on how groups make decisions.
Forsyth (2006) synthesised this in his textbook *Group Dynamics*, creating a taxonomy of cognitive errors in groups.

💡 Think of it like a hospital audit. Kerr and Forsyth reviewed all the different ways doctors make medical errors — not because of sabotage, but because human brains take shortcuts when overwhelmed.

They also conducted research on **confirmation bias** — creating groups that were:
- **Unanimous** in their initial preference
- Had **one minority member**
- Had **two minority members**

Finding: Unanimous groups showed the **strongest confirmation bias**.
Groups with two minority members were *better than individuals* at overcoming it.

**Osmani (2016)** tested cognitive errors in real groups:
- Presented groups with two projects — one had a sunk cost, one didn't
- **86% recommended** the project with a sunk cost (vs 26% without)
- Confirmed that **sunk cost bias** significantly distorts group decisions

---

**3. What they found — Three "Decisional Sins"**

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Sins of Commission** | Using information you shouldn't | Belief perseverance, Sunk cost bias, Extra-evidentiary bias, Hindsight bias |
| **Sins of Omission** | Ignoring information you should use | Base rate bias, Fundamental attribution error |
| **Sins of Imprecision** | Over-simplifying with mental shortcuts | Availability heuristic, Conjunction bias, Representativeness heuristic |

**Key errors explained:**

| Error | What it means | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Belief perseverance** | Keep using info already proven wrong | Continuing a failed strategy because "we invested so much" |
| **Sunk cost bias** | Can't abandon a project because money's already spent | Osmani (2016): 86% pushed forward despite evidence against it |
| **Extra-evidentiary bias** | Using info you were explicitly told to ignore | A rumour about a worker that the group was told to disregard |
| **Fundamental attribution error** | Blaming personality, not situation | "They're lazy" rather than "the equipment is broken" |
| **Availability heuristic** | Deciding based on what comes to mind most easily | Fearing plane crashes more than car crashes (planes are more dramatic) |
| **Confirmation bias** | Only seeking info that confirms what you already believe | Unanimous groups = worse than diverse groups at avoiding this |

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Explains everyday meeting disasters without needing extreme groupthink conditions
- Knowing these errors helps groups **spot and correct** them in real time
- Diverse groups (with minority voices) make better decisions — practical implication for team composition
- Training in cognitive error awareness reduces their impact

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Supported by research (Osmani, 2016)**
→ WHY: Lab study showed sunk cost bias powerfully distorts group recommendations (86% vs 26%)
→ CONSEQUENCE: Provides empirical backing that cognitive errors are real and significant — increases reliability

✅ **Applied value — strategies can be taught**
→ WHY: Unlike groupthink (which is harder to prevent), cognitive errors can be corrected through education and group diversity
→ CONSEQUENCE: Direct practical application to workplace training and meeting structure

❌ **Reductionist — ignores emotion and stress**
→ WHY: The theory focuses purely on cognitive (mental) errors, ignoring emotional factors like stress (which Janis included) or sleep deprivation
→ CONSEQUENCE: Incomplete explanation — other variables also affect group decisions, making the theory too narrow

❌ **Cultural differences — fundamental attribution error is not universal**
→ WHY: Forsyth himself noted the fundamental attribution error mainly occurs in *individualist* cultures, not collectivist ones (Japan, China)
→ CONSEQUENCE: The "sins" framework does not apply equally across cultures — limits generalisability

❌ **Group vs individual comparisons are artificial**
→ WHY: Lab studies (e.g., Osmani, 2016) use artificial tasks and pre-formed groups — not like real workplace teams with history and relationships
→ CONSEQUENCE: Low ecological validity; may not reflect real group dynamics in organisations

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Three categories: Sins of Commission, Omission, Imprecision
- Key errors: sunk cost bias, extra-evidentiary bias, fundamental attribution error, confirmation bias
- Confirmation bias stronger in unanimous groups (Kerr et al.)
- Osmani (2016): 86% chose sunk cost option
- Weakness: reductionist (ignores stress/emotion); not universal across cultures

📋 **Skeleton answer (evaluate):**
> A strength of Forsyth's theory is empirical support from Osmani (2016) who found that 86% of group members recommended a project with a sunk cost compared to only 26% without one, supporting the idea that cognitive errors significantly distort group decision-making. However, Forsyth's theory is reductionist as it only considers cognitive factors and ignores the role of stress, emotion, and personality which groupthink theory (Janis, 1971) incorporates. Furthermore, Forsyth himself acknowledged that some errors, such as the fundamental attribution error, are more common in individualist cultures, suggesting the theory is not universally applicable.

## Study 7

### Triplett (1898) — The First Social Facilitation Experiment

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Norman Triplett was watching bicycle races and noticed something strange:
cyclists went **faster when racing against others** than when riding alone against the clock.

He wondered: does the mere **presence of other people** change how well we perform?
This became arguably the **first formal experiment in social psychology**.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

**Observation (naturalistic):**
Triplett examined official records of the **American Cyclist Union**, comparing times of cyclists racing alone (against the clock) vs. racing against other competitors.

He noticed: **group racing = faster times** than solo racing.

But correlation isn't causation — maybe the cyclists just happened to try harder in races?

**Laboratory experiment:**
Triplett brought **40 children** into a lab.
They played a game involving **turning a small fishing reel as fast as possible**.

Two conditions:
1. **Alone** — just turning the reel by themselves
2. **In pairs** — two children turning reels side by side simultaneously

💡 Think of it like a spinning game at a funfair. Triplett wanted to know: do kids spin faster when a friend is doing it next to them?

---

**3. What they found**

- Children turned the reel **slightly faster in pairs** than when alone
- Consistent with his naturalistic observation of cyclists

> ⚠️ Important caveat: not all children showed the effect.
> Some performed better alone. Some showed no difference.
> Half of participants showed **no social facilitation**.

This suggests the effect exists, but **individual differences matter** — not everyone is equally affected by others' presence.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Founded the field of **social facilitation research**
- Led to decades of follow-up: Zajonc (drive theory), Cottrell (evaluation apprehension)
- Still referenced as the founding study of social psychology experiments
- Demonstrates that **others' presence affects performance** — relevant to open-plan offices, presentations, competitive sales floors

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Both naturalistic observation AND lab experiment**
→ WHY: Triangulation — naturalistic data sparked the question; lab controlled confounds
→ CONSEQUENCE: Findings are more credible than if only one method was used

✅ **First quantitative social psychology experiment**
→ WHY: Used measured, objective data (reel speed)
→ CONSEQUENCE: Set the template for experimental social psychology

❌ **Very small, non-representative sample (40 children)**
→ WHY: Only children; no variation in age, occupation, or cultural background
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot generalise to adults or workers in real workplaces — low population validity

❌ **Low ecological validity**
→ WHY: Turning a fishing reel is nothing like real work tasks
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot be certain that the effect translates to complex workplace performance

❌ **Half participants showed no effect**
→ WHY: Individual differences (personality, anxiety) moderated the result
→ CONSEQUENCE: Social facilitation is not a universal effect — the theory is oversimplified

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- First social psychology experiment (1898)
- Two phases: naturalistic (cyclists) + lab (40 children, fishing reel)
- Finding: slightly faster in pairs — but only for some participants
- Weakness: children only, artificial task, half showed no effect

📋 **One-liner for an exam:**
> Triplett (1898) found that 40 children turned a fishing reel slightly faster when working alongside a partner than alone, providing early evidence for social facilitation, though the effect was not universal.

## Study 8

### Ringelmann (1913) — Social Loafing and the Rope-Pulling Study

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Max Ringelmann was a French agricultural engineer in the late 1800s/early 1900s.
He was interested in a practical question: **does adding more people to a task always increase output proportionally?**

If 1 person can pull 63 kg — should 2 people pull 126 kg?
He suspected the answer was no. And he was right.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Ringelmann had participants **pull a rope attached to a pressure gauge** (a dynamometer — a device that measures force).

They pulled in groups of:
- 1 person
- 2 people
- 3 people
- Up to 8 people

💡 Imagine a tug-of-war. You'd expect that adding more people to your side would double, triple, or quadruple the pulling force. Ringelmann measured exactly how much force was actually being produced.

---

**3. What they found**

| Group Size | Expected Force | Actual Force | Loss |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|------|
| 1 person | 63 kg | 63 kg | 0% |
| 2 people | 126 kg | 118 kg | ~6% |
| 3 people | 189 kg | ~160 kg | ~15% |
| 8 people | 504 kg | ~248 kg | ~51% |

> The more people added, the **further below potential** each individual performed.

Ringelmann suggested two causes:
1. **Coordination loss** — people's efforts don't perfectly synchronise (he thought this was the main cause)
2. **Motivation loss** — individuals pull less hard because they think others will compensate

The motivation loss idea is what we now call **social loafing**.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- First evidence of **social loafing**
- Explains why large project teams often underperform despite having many members
- Directly linked to **diffusion of responsibility**: the more people present, the less each feels accountable
- Challenged the intuition that "more people = more productivity"

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Objective, quantitative measurement**
→ WHY: Dynamometer provides precise, unambiguous force data — no interpretation needed
→ CONSEQUENCE: Results are reliable and replicable

✅ **Simple, elegant design**
→ WHY: One clear variable manipulated (group size); one measured (force)
→ CONSEQUENCE: Easy to replicate and verify — good scientific design

❌ **Physical task only — low ecological validity**
→ WHY: Rope-pulling has little in common with cognitive, creative, or collaborative work tasks
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot confidently apply findings to modern workplaces where work is primarily mental

❌ **Coordination loss vs. motivation loss unresolved**
→ WHY: Ringelmann acknowledged both but couldn't separate them in his design
→ CONSEQUENCE: It's unclear how much of the performance loss is truly "loafing" (motivational) vs. physical coordination failure

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Physical rope-pulling with pressure gauge; group sizes 1–8
- More people → lower individual force output (not proportional)
- Two explanations: coordination loss (main) + motivation loss (social loafing)
- Weakness: physical task, low ecological validity, can't separate the two causes

---

## Study 9

### Schmitt et al. (1986) — Evaluation Apprehension and Social Facilitation

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Zajonc (1966) proposed that just the **mere presence** of others raises arousal and causes social facilitation.
But Cottrell argued it wasn't presence that mattered — it was **being evaluated** that caused the arousal.

Schmitt and colleagues designed a study to test whether **mere presence alone** is enough to produce social facilitation, or whether evaluation is the key factor.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

**Participants:** 45 college undergraduates

**Three conditions:**
1. **Alone** — participant works by themselves, no one watching
2. **Non-evaluative presence** — another person is in the room wearing a **blindfold and headphones** (they genuinely cannot see or hear the participant)
3. **Evaluative presence** — an experimenter watches and evaluates the participant's performance

**Tasks — these were cleverly designed:**
- **Simple task**: type your own name repeatedly (easy, over-learned behaviour)
- **Complex task**: type your name *backwards* with ascending numbers inserted between each letter (e.g., N1A2M3E4 → extremely difficult)

💡 The trick: before participants realised the study had begun, they were already being watched and asked to do these tasks. This prevented demand characteristics.

---

**3. What they found**

| Condition | Simple Task | Complex Task |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Alone | Baseline | Baseline |
| Blindfolded observer | Faster | Slower |
| Evaluating experimenter | Even faster | Even slower |

Key conclusions:
- **Mere presence** (blindfolded observer) *was* enough to produce social facilitation on simple tasks and social inhibition on complex ones
- **Evaluation** made both effects even stronger
- Both effects are real — they work together, not instead of each other

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Confirms both **drive theory** (mere presence raises arousal) AND **evaluation apprehension** (being judged raises it further)
- Explains why people do well on presentations they've rehearsed, but choke on unprepared ones
- Relevant to open-plan offices: being seen helps with routine work, hurts complex thinking
- Lab evidence with controlled conditions — adds scientific credibility

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Controlled lab experiment — isolates variables**
→ WHY: Three conditions cleverly separated "mere presence" from "evaluation" — elegant design
→ CONSEQUENCE: Can be confident about which factor caused what — high internal validity

✅ **Tests two competing theories simultaneously**
→ WHY: Rather than supporting one theory, shows both drive theory AND evaluation apprehension operate
→ CONSEQUENCE: More nuanced and complete understanding of social facilitation

❌ **Artificial setting — low ecological validity**
→ WHY: Typing your name is not representative of real work tasks
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot be certain results apply to real workplace performance situations

❌ **Undergraduate student sample**
→ WHY: College students are not representative of the general working population
→ CONSEQUENCE: Limits generalisation to workers of different ages, backgrounds, and cultures

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- 45 undergraduates; 3 conditions: alone / blindfolded observer / evaluating experimenter
- Simple task: faster with audience; Complex task: slower with audience
- Mere presence = some effect; Evaluation = stronger effect
- Supports BOTH drive theory (Zajonc) AND evaluation apprehension (Cottrell)

📋 **One-liner:**
> Schmitt et al. (1986) found that 45 undergraduates performed a simple task faster in the presence of both a blindfolded observer and an evaluating experimenter, compared to alone — with effects strongest in the evaluating condition — supporting both mere presence and evaluation apprehension explanations of social facilitation.

---

## Study 10

### Latané (1981) — Social Impact Theory and Social Loafing

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Ringelmann had identified social loafing in 1913.
But **why** does it happen?

Bibb Latané (1981) provided the theoretical mechanism: **Social Impact Theory**.
He also ran a direct study measuring the effect of group size on noise output (clapping/shouting) to test the theory.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

**Social Impact Theory (1981):**
Latané proposed that the social "impact" others have on you depends on three factors:
- **Strength** — how important or powerful those people are
- **Immediacy** — how close (physically or psychologically) they are
- **Number** — how many people there are

💡 Think of it like an overhead projector spreading light across a room. The same amount of light spread over a large screen makes each part of the screen dimmer. The more people sharing the responsibility, the less "responsibility light" lands on each individual.

As group size increases, **responsibility is diffused** across more people → each person's individual accountability drops → they put in less effort.

**The clapping/shouting study:**
- Participants: groups of varying sizes
- Task: **clap or shout as loud as possible**
- Conditions: participants sometimes heard via headphones (to isolate individual contributions); sometimes believed others were also clapping/shouting
- Measured: **noise level** produced by each individual

---

**3. What they found**

| Group Size | Individual Performance (% of alone capacity) |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------|
| Alone | 100% |
| 2 people | ~82% |
| 6 people | ~74% |

> The larger the group, the less effort each individual contributed — even when coordination wasn't a factor (headphones isolated their sounds).

This ruled out coordination loss as the main cause — it was **motivation loss** (loafing).

> 🧠 Latané also used **cockroaches** in social facilitation studies — which does raise questions about whether results generalise to humans, but it demonstrates the effect is deep and biological.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Provides the **mechanism** behind social loafing: diffusion of responsibility
- Group size directly and predictably affects individual motivation
- Explains why accountability measures in workplaces matter
- Connects to **Piliavin et al.'s** subway Samaritan study: same diffusion of responsibility explains both reduced helping AND reduced work effort

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Quantitative, scientific method**
→ WHY: Noise levels are objectively measurable; controlled conditions allow replication
→ CONSEQUENCE: Results are reliable and can be compared across studies

✅ **Theory provides the mechanism**
→ WHY: Social Impact Theory explains *why* social loafing happens, not just that it does
→ CONSEQUENCE: Makes the theory more useful — allows predictions about when loafing will be worst (large, anonymous groups)

❌ **Low ecological validity**
→ WHY: Clapping and shouting are not real work tasks; the cockroach studies are not human
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot be certain the effect applies to complex workplace tasks or across species

❌ **Cultural differences not considered**
→ WHY: Studies mostly used Western, individualist participants
→ CONSEQUENCE: Earley (1989, 1993) showed social loafing doesn't appear the same way in collectivist cultures — limits generalisability

❌ **Individual differences ignored**
→ WHY: Social Impact Theory treats all group members as equivalent
→ CONSEQUENCE: Doesn't account for highly motivated individuals who won't loaf regardless of group size

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Social Impact Theory: strength + immediacy + number of others
- Diffusion of responsibility = mechanism of social loafing
- Clapping study: 100% alone → ~74% in group of 6
- Weakness: low ecological validity (clapping ≠ real work); doesn't apply equally across cultures

📋 **One-liner:**
> Latané (1981) found that participants clapping and shouting in groups produced progressively less individual noise as group size increased, supporting social impact theory's claim that diffusion of responsibility causes social loafing.

## Study 11

### Earley (1989 & 1993) — Social Loafing, Culture, and Ingroup/Outgroup Performance

---

**1. Why this study exists**

All prior social loafing research was done in **Western, individualist cultures**.
But what happens in China? In Israel? Do people from cultures that value *the group* over *the self* also loaf?

P. Christopher Earley ran two linked studies to find out — and the results changed how psychologists think about social loafing fundamentally.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

**Earley (1989) — First study: Does culture affect social loafing?**

Compared **managers from the USA** (individualist culture) vs **managers from China** (collectivist culture).
Both groups performed a shared-responsibility task.
Earley measured individual effort levels.

Finding: **Americans loafed; Chinese didn't.**

But why? Was it culture itself — or something specific about *who* you're working with?

---

**Earley (1993) — Focus study: Is it culture, or is it ingroup vs outgroup?**

**Participants:** 165 entry/middle managers
- 45 Israeli (collectivist)
- 60 Chinese (collectivist)
- 60 American (individualist)
- Age: 25–40; all had bachelor's degrees or higher

**Three conditions (randomly assigned):**
| Condition | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Alone** | Works on task alone |
| **Ingroup** | Told they are part of a 10-person group from their own region; similarities emphasised |
| **Outgroup** | Told they are part of a 10-person group from a different region; differences emphasised |

> ⚠️ Crucial detail: In the ingroup and outgroup conditions, participants were **NOT physically with group members**. They were in a room with others, but were told those people were NOT their group. The group existed **only in their minds**.

💡 This is brilliant because it shows that even **imagining you're part of a group** changes how hard you work.

**The task:**
- A simulated "in-basket" of **40 workplace tasks**: writing memos, prioritising client interviews, rating job applications
- 60 minutes to complete as many correctly as possible
- 5-minute practice trial first (to calibrate baseline expectations)

**What was measured:**
1. **Performance**: number of correctly completed tasks in 60 minutes
2. **Individualism-collectivism**: 5-point scale questionnaire (higher = more collectivist)
3. **Individual self-efficacy**: "How confident are you that YOU can complete X tasks?"
4. **Group self-efficacy**: "How confident are you that your GROUP can complete X tasks?"

---

**3. What they found**

**Table 9.3 (from the textbook):**

| Country | Alone | Outgroup | Ingroup |
|---------|-------|---------|--------|
| **China** | 18.49 | 18.37 | **23.83** |
| **USA** | **24.55** | 16.21 | 16.57 |
| **Israel** | 20.79 | 18.63 | **23.18** |

Key findings:
- **Collectivists (China, Israel)**: performed best in **ingroups**, worse alone or in outgroups
- **Individualists (USA)**: performed best **alone**, worse in any group context
- **Self-efficacy mediated** the results: collectivists expected to perform better in ingroups → they did; individualists expected to perform better alone → they did

> 🧠 This means it's not just culture that determines loafing — it's whether you **identify with** the group you're in.
> Collectivists in an **outgroup** loafed just like individualists.
> The key variable is **group identification**, not culture alone.

**Conclusions:**
1. Social loafing does **not occur universally** — it depends on culture and group type
2. **Ingroup membership** increases performance for collectivists
3. **Cognitive bias** operates: collectivists assume ingroup members share their values → expect better group performance → achieve it
4. Individualists assume others will perform as they would (alone) → expect to do best alone → achieve it

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Revolutionises understanding of social loafing — **not a universal phenomenon**
- Shows that **who you think you're working with** matters as much as the task itself
- Practical: multinational companies cannot apply Western management strategies to collectivist cultures
- Suggests that **team composition and group identity** are more powerful than previously assumed

---

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Multi-national sample — tests culture directly**
→ WHY: Three countries with different cultural orientations were compared within one study
→ CONSEQUENCE: Directly tests cultural effects on social loafing — much stronger evidence than single-culture studies

✅ **Quantitative methodology — reliable and replicable**
→ WHY: Performance scores, rating scales for efficacy and collectivism/individualism — objective data
→ CONSEQUENCE: Results are not subject to interpretation bias; study can be replicated — high reliability

✅ **Extends Earley (1989) to show greater nuance**
→ WHY: 1993 study showed it's not just about culture — *ingroup vs outgroup* is the more important variable
→ CONSEQUENCE: More holistic and complete theory of social loafing — corrects oversimplification of the 1989 finding

❌ **Low ecological validity**
→ WHY: Participants were in an artificial lab setting; the "ingroup" only existed in their imagination — they were actually strangers
→ CONSEQUENCE: May not reflect real ingroup dynamics formed over years of working together — limits applicability to real organisations

❌ **Nomothetic approach — ignores individual variation**
→ WHY: Group averages were analysed; individual differences within each cultural group were overlooked
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot explain why some Chinese managers loafed or why some Americans performed well in groups — an idiographic approach would add important nuance

❌ **Cultural determinism risk**
→ WHY: Earley's conclusions can be read as "all collectivists behave X, all individualists behave Y"
→ CONSEQUENCE: Within-culture variation is significant — individualists within collectivist cultures exist; this oversimplification is deterministic

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- 1989: USA vs China managers; Americans loafed, Chinese didn't
- 1993: 165 managers from USA/China/Israel; 3 conditions: alone, ingroup, outgroup
- Chinese/Israelis (collectivists): best performance in **ingroup**
- Americans (individualists): best performance **alone**
- Self-efficacy *mediates* the relationship (it explains part of why)
- Key conclusion: **ingroup identity**, not just culture, is the critical factor
- Weakness: low ecological validity (imagined groups ≠ real workplace groups)

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe):**
> Earley (1993) studied 165 managers from China (collectivist), Israel (collectivist), and the United States (individualist) who were randomly assigned to work alone, in an ingroup (emphasising similarities with group members), or an outgroup (emphasising differences). The task was a 60-minute in-basket of simulated workplace tasks. Results showed that collectivists performed best in ingroup conditions and worst alone or in outgroups, while individualists performed best alone. Earley concluded that social loafing does not occur universally and that ingroup identification — rather than culture alone — is the key variable, mediated by individual and group self-efficacy.

📋 **Skeleton answer (evaluate):**
> A strength of Earley's study is its use of a multi-national sample from three cultures, allowing direct comparison of collectivists and individualists within a single controlled study, increasing the study's validity. The use of quantitative measures (performance score, rating scales) also increases reliability. However, a weakness is that the study lacks ecological validity, as the ingroup condition was artificial — participants imagined group members who were not physically present and were strangers. Earley himself acknowledged this means findings may not reflect the true dynamics of real workplace ingroups. Additionally, the nomothetic approach ignores individual variation within cultures, which limits how much can be concluded about any single person's behaviour.
