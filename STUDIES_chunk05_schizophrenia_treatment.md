## Study 1

### Zhao et al. (2016) — Do antipsychotic drugs actually stop relapse?

**1. Why this study exists**

Before drugs like **chlorpromazine** (the first antipsychotic, 1950s), the only option for someone with schizophrenia was hospitalisation.
- Drugs changed everything — but do they actually work long-term?
- Do different drugs work better than others?
- Are some just fancy placebos?

This study tried to answer those questions at the largest scale possible.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Picture a librarian in a university archive. Their job: collect every decent clinical drug trial ever run on antipsychotics and schizophrenia.

- They searched electronic databases and pulled **56 randomised control trials (RCTs)**.
- Combined, these trials included data from **over 10,000 people** with schizophrenia.
- They examined **18 different antipsychotic drugs** — both **typical** (older, e.g. chlorpromazine, haloperidol) and **atypical** (newer, e.g. olanzapine, risperidone, clozapine).
- Treatment duration averaged **48 weeks** (ranging from 1 month to 2 years).
- Each study compared a drug to a **placebo** (a dummy pill with no active ingredient).
- Trial quality was checked using the **Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool** — a standard checklist used to judge how trustworthy a study is.
- Relapse was measured in most studies using the **Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)** — a validated rating scale that combines self-report, clinician observation, and caregiver input.
- This overall approach is called a **meta-analysis**: pooling data from many studies to get a clearer, bigger picture.

💡 Think of it like averaging many restaurant reviews instead of trusting just one.

---

**3. What they found**

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Drugs beating placebo | 17 out of 18 drugs had lower relapse rates than placebo |
| Drugs NOT beating placebo for hospital admissions | 4 drugs performed no better than placebo |
| Best performer | Olanzapine significantly outperformed chlorpromazine and haloperidol |
| Second-best | Fluphenazine decanoate also outperformed chlorpromazine |
| Overall conclusion | Most drugs showed only modest differences from each other |

- **60–80% of patients relapse** if they stop taking their medication (Patel et al., 2014).
- Drug **efficacy drops** the longer treatment is delayed — the first 5 years are the most critical window.
- **30–70% of patients** do not respond meaningfully to any drug treatment (Howes et al., 2017).

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This is the largest and most comprehensive review of antipsychotic drug effectiveness ever conducted at the time.
- It shows that drugs are genuinely better than doing nothing — a massive step forward from the era of pure hospitalisation.
- But it also reveals huge limitations: many people still relapse, and many don't respond at all.
- It provides the evidence base for protocols like the **Texas Medication Algorithm Project (TMAP)**, which guides doctors on the order in which to try different drugs.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Very large sample (n = 10,177)**
- Why: More data = more statistical power = more confidence in results
- Consequence: Findings can be generalised to the wider population of people with schizophrenia with greater confidence

✅ **Longitudinal design (average 48 weeks)**
- Why: Short studies miss long-term effects like delayed relapse or tolerance building
- Consequence: More ecologically valid picture of how drugs work in the real world

✅ **Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool used**
- Why: Filters out poorly designed studies that might skew the findings
- Consequence: Increases the overall validity and trustworthiness of the meta-analysis

✅ **PANSS used for relapse measurement**
- Why: PANSS has high validity — it combines self-report, clinician observation AND caregiver data
- Consequence: Reduces the chance of measurement bias affecting results

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Nomothetic approach — misses individual differences**
- Why: Averages across thousands of people hide the fact that some people do brilliantly and others not at all
- Consequence: Doctors can't use this data to predict how any one patient will respond; a more idiographic approach (e.g. interviews) would capture factors like therapeutic alliance and social support

❌ **Relapse measured differently across studies**
- Why: Different studies used different definitions and tools for measuring "relapse"
- Consequence: Comparing them introduces noise — it's like averaging scores from different sports

❌ **Only measures one outcome: relapse rates**
- Why: Relapse is important but incomplete — it says nothing about quality of life, side effects, or negative symptoms
- Consequence: A drug could technically "prevent relapse" while leaving the patient dazed, overweight, and unable to work

---

**6. Exam tips**

> ⚡ Must-knows:
> - 56 RCTs, 10,177+ participants, 18 drugs, average 48 weeks
> - 17/18 drugs beat placebo for relapse
> - 30–70% of patients don't respond at all
> - 60–80% relapse if they stop medication
> - PANSS is the measurement tool; Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was used to assess study quality

🧠 Quick check:
- What does "nomothetic" mean and why is it both a strength AND weakness here?
- What is the PANSS and why does it have high validity?
- What is the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool?

📋 Skeleton answer — 6 marks: "Describe one study of biological treatment for schizophrenia"

> Zhao et al. (2016) conducted a meta-analysis of 56 randomised control trials (RCTs), examining data from over 10,000 people with schizophrenia who took one of 18 different antipsychotic drugs. Treatment lasted an average of 48 weeks. The researchers compared relapse rates between those on active drugs and those on placebos, using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) to measure symptoms. They found that 17 out of 18 drugs produced significantly lower relapse rates than placebo. However, 4 drugs showed no advantage over placebo for reducing hospital admissions. The researchers concluded that antipsychotic medication is broadly effective for relapse prevention, though individual variation in response is significant.

📋 Skeleton answer — 10 marks (evaluation): "Evaluate biological treatment for schizophrenia, including a discussion of longitudinal studies."

> One strength of Zhao et al. (2016) is the use of a **longitudinal design** (average 48 weeks). Longitudinal studies are important because they allow researchers to monitor whether improvements are sustained over time or whether relapse eventually occurs. This is particularly relevant for schizophrenia, where relapses are common and may emerge months after initial improvement. The long duration increases the ecological validity of the findings, as short studies could overestimate drug effectiveness by not capturing delayed relapse.
>
> However, a weakness is the **nomothetic approach**. By pooling data from thousands of participants, the meta-analysis produces generalised findings that cannot predict individual responses. For example, 30–70% of patients don't respond to any drug (Howes et al., 2017). A more idiographic approach, using interviews or case studies, could reveal why certain individuals respond and others don't — for instance, by capturing social factors like family support or the quality of the doctor-patient relationship. This would allow for more personalised treatment planning.
>
> A further weakness is that **relapse rates were measured differently** across the 56 studies. Although most used the PANSS, definitions of "relapse" varied. This inconsistency introduces noise into the pooled data, reducing confidence in the comparisons made between drugs.

## Study 2

### Petrides et al. (2015) — Can electric shocks help when drugs completely fail?

**1. Why this study exists**

Some people with schizophrenia try every drug available and still don't get better. These people are called **treatment-resistant**. Even **clozapine** — the most powerful antipsychotic, used only as a last resort — fails up to 70% of treatment-resistant patients.

So what's left?

**Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** — where controlled electric pulses are delivered to the brain via scalp electrodes, inducing a brief seizure — had shown promise, but no rigorous trial had tested it alongside clozapine for this specific, hardest-to-treat group.

Petrides et al. designed exactly that trial.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you're a patient. You've been on antipsychotics for years. Nothing has worked. Your doctor tells you about a new trial.

- You are one of **39 participants** (n=39), all diagnosed with schizophrenia resistant to clozapine.
- You are **randomly assigned** to one of two groups:
  - **ECT + clozapine group** (n=20): You continue your clozapine dose AND receive ECT sessions.
  - **Clozapine only group** (n=19): You continue your clozapine dose as usual — no ECT.

ECT was delivered **3 times per week** for the first 4 weeks, then **twice per week** for the final 4 weeks — a total of **8 weeks**.

The "response criterion" was a **40% or greater reduction in symptoms**, measured by blind-rated assessments.

Crucially, this was a **single-blind study**: the clinicians assessing symptoms did not know which group each patient was in — but the patients themselves knew whether they had received ECT.

💡 Think of it like a taste test where the judges are blindfolded, but the cooks know which recipe they used.

---

**3. What they found**

| Group | Met response criterion (40%+ symptom reduction) |
|---|---|
| ECT + clozapine | 50% of patients |
| Clozapine only | 0% of patients |

- In a crossover phase (where clozapine-only patients then received ECT), **47% responded**.
- There were **no significant differences in global cognition** between the groups — ECT did not cause measurable cognitive damage in this study.
- The researchers concluded: ECT augmentation of clozapine is a **safe and effective** treatment for clozapine-resistant schizophrenia.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This is a lifeline finding for some of the most severely unwell people in society, including those at risk of suicide.
- It demonstrates that ECT has genuine clinical value beyond its historical reputation as a brutal last resort.
- It supports the inclusion of ECT in treatment protocols like the TMAP after all other options fail.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Randomised design**
- Why: Random allocation to groups minimises selection bias — the two groups should be equivalent at baseline
- Consequence: Any difference in outcomes is more likely due to the ECT intervention and not pre-existing differences between patients

✅ **Blind raters assessed symptoms**
- Why: Clinicians who scored symptoms didn't know which group patients were in, reducing observer bias
- Consequence: The 50% response rate is less likely to reflect wishful thinking on the part of the assessors

✅ **Crossover phase included**
- Why: Allows the clozapine-only group to eventually receive ECT, providing additional data and acting as partial replication
- Consequence: The 47% crossover response rate corroborates the main finding

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Very small sample (n=39)**
- Why: With only 39 participants, the findings could easily be due to chance
- Consequence: Results cannot be confidently generalised to all people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia

❌ **Single-blind design — placebo effects possible**
- Why: Patients knew they had received ECT; this awareness may have raised their expectations of improvement
- Consequence: Some of the 50% improvement could be a **placebo (expectancy) effect**, not the biological effect of ECT
- This is directly supported by Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) (see Study 3 below), who found no significant difference between real and sham ECT

❌ **Short duration (8 weeks)**
- Why: Schizophrenia is a chronic, lifelong condition; 8 weeks cannot tell us whether benefits are sustained
- Consequence: We don't know if patients relapsed after the trial ended — long-term effectiveness is unproven from this study alone

---

**6. Exam tips**

> ⚡ Must-knows:
> - n=39; 8 weeks; ECT 3x/week then 2x/week
> - 50% of ECT+clozapine group met response criterion; 0% of clozapine-only group
> - Single-blind: clinicians were blind, but patients weren't
> - The key weakness: placebo/expectancy effects are possible
> - Small sample size limits generalisability

🧠 Quick check:
- What does "single-blind" mean and why is it a problem for this study specifically?
- What is the response criterion (40% reduction) and how does it relate to the PANSS?
- Why can't we conclude ECT is effective from this study alone?

📋 Skeleton answer — 6 marks: "Describe the methodology of one study of ECT for schizophrenia"

> Petrides et al. (2015) conducted a prospective, randomised single-blind study with 39 participants who had been diagnosed with clozapine-resistant schizophrenia. Participants were randomly assigned to either clozapine plus ECT (n=20) or clozapine alone (n=19). ECT was administered three times per week for four weeks, then twice weekly for four weeks. The response criterion was a 40% or greater reduction in PANSS-rated symptoms, assessed by clinicians who were blind to which group participants were in. Fifty percent of the ECT+clozapine group met this criterion, compared with none in the clozapine-only group, leading to the conclusion that ECT augmentation is an effective treatment for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.

## Study 3

### Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) — Is ECT just an expensive placebo?

**1. Why this study exists**

Petrides et al. (2015) showed impressive results — but there was a flaw: patients knew they were receiving ECT. If you know you're getting a powerful treatment, you might feel better simply because you expect to. This is the **placebo effect** (also called the **expectancy effect**).

To test whether ECT's benefits are real or just expectancy, you need a **sham ECT** condition — where patients go through all the motions of ECT (anaesthetic, electrodes, the whole procedure) but no electrical pulse is actually delivered and no seizure is induced.

Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) ran exactly this test.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine two groups of patients, both of whom have clozapine-resistant schizophrenia.

- **Group A (real ECT, n=13):** They're given anaesthetic, electrodes are placed on their scalp, and a real electrical pulse is delivered. A seizure is induced.
- **Group B (sham ECT, n=10):** They go through the exact same ritual — anaesthetic, electrodes placed, machine switched on — but **no electrical pulse is delivered**. They wake up with no memory of any difference, because the anaesthetic affects memory anyway.

Both groups believed they might be receiving real ECT.

Symptoms were measured before and after using the **PANSS** (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale).

💡 It's like testing whether a sugar pill works by comparing it to an identical-looking real pill — neither group knows which they got.

---

**3. What they found**

| Group | PANSS total score reduction | PANSS positive subscale reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Real ECT (n=13) | −8.78% (81.23 → 74.75) | −19% (19.31 → 16.17) |
| Sham ECT (n=10) | −15.27% (96.80 → 87.43) | −27.81% (22.90 → 19.14) |

- The sham group actually showed **greater improvement** than the real ECT group on both measures.
- There was **no statistically significant difference** between the two groups.
- Conclusion: **ECT showed no benefit over sham ECT** — the apparent efficacy may be a placebo effect.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This directly challenges the findings of Petrides et al. (2015) and Wang et al. (2018a).
- It raises a major question: if ECT works, why does a fake version work just as well or better?
- It doesn't prove ECT is useless — the sample is tiny and the study is a pilot — but it is a serious challenge to the claim that ECT has a genuine neurological effect.
- It highlights the importance of using **sham controls** and **double-blind designs** when testing ECT.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Sham control condition**
- Why: Directly tests whether ECT's effects are biological or expectancy-driven
- Consequence: More rigorous than single-blind studies like Petrides et al. — placebo effects are controlled for

✅ **Both groups received identical procedure except for the pulse**
- Why: Eliminates confounds like "amount of attention received" or "hospital visits"
- Consequence: Any difference in outcomes is specifically due to the electrical stimulation

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Extremely small sample (n=23 total; 13 real, 10 sham)**
- Why: With such few participants, results can easily be due to chance variation
- Consequence: The study is underpowered — it cannot reliably detect a genuine ECT effect even if one exists; described as a "pilot" study

❌ **Labelled a pilot — not designed to give a definitive answer**
- Why: Pilot studies are exploratory and typically use very small samples
- Consequence: Cannot be used to conclude ECT doesn't work — only that more research is needed

❌ **Participants had worse baseline PANSS scores in the sham group**
- Why: With n=10 in sham group, random allocation may not have fully balanced the groups
- Consequence: The sham group's larger percentage improvement might partly reflect having more room to improve (regression to the mean)

---

**6. Exam tips**

> ⚡ Must-knows:
> - n=23; sham vs real ECT
> - Sham group improved MORE than real ECT group
> - No statistically significant difference between groups
> - This suggests ECT's effect may be a placebo/expectancy effect
> - Directly contradicts Petrides (2015) and Wang (2018)

🧠 Quick check:
- What is "sham ECT" and why is it necessary?
- What does it mean if a sham group improves as much as the real treatment group?
- Why can't we conclude ECT is definitively useless from this study?

📋 Skeleton answer — evaluation point:

> A weakness of ECT as a treatment is that its effectiveness may be explained by placebo (expectancy) effects rather than genuine neurological mechanisms. Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) found no significant difference in symptom improvement between participants who received real ECT (n=13) and those who received sham ECT (n=10) — in which all the procedures occurred but no electrical pulse was delivered. In fact, the sham group showed greater percentage improvement on the PANSS. This challenges the validity of findings from single-blind studies like Petrides et al. (2015), where patients knew they had received ECT and may have improved partly because they expected to.

## Study 4

### Wang et al. (2018a) — Large-scale meta-analysis of ECT in China

**1. Why this study exists**

Petrides et al. (2015) was small (n=39) and Western. Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) was tiny and raised doubts. Neither gave a big, confident answer.

Wang et al. (2018a) attempted to resolve this by pooling data from **18 randomised control trials** — mostly from China — to see whether the picture became clearer at scale.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Another meta-analysis. The researchers:
- Searched databases for RCTs of **ECT combined with clozapine** in patients with schizophrenia.
- Found **18 eligible trials**, covering a total of **1,769 participants** across 20 active treatment arms.
- The vast majority of studies — 17 out of 18 — were conducted in **China**.
- Outcomes included symptom improvement, speed of response, and side effects.
- The review was published in the **Journal of Psychiatric Research** in October 2018.

---

**3. What they found**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Efficacy | ECT + clozapine was superior to clozapine alone at all timepoints |
| Speed of effect | Improvement visible as early as **week 1–2** |
| Side effects | ECT group had more self-reported memory impairment and headaches |
| Overall conclusion | ECT + clozapine is "highly effective and relatively safe" for treatment-resistant schizophrenia |

---

**4. Why it matters**

- Schizophrenia prevalence in China **doubled between 1990 and 2010** (Chan et al., 2015), creating enormous clinical need.
- This meta-analysis is the largest evidence base for ECT in treatment-resistant schizophrenia and supports its use as an add-on to clozapine.
- However, the near-exclusive use of Chinese samples raises serious questions about **cultural generalisability**.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Very large combined sample (n=1,769)**
- Why: Much larger than any single trial; statistical conclusions are more robust
- Consequence: Reduces the risk that the findings are due to chance variation in any one study

✅ **Consistent direction of findings across 18 studies**
- Why: Convergence across multiple independent studies in different hospitals strengthens the evidence
- Consequence: Makes it harder to dismiss as a fluke — the effect appears to replicate

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **17 out of 18 studies conducted in China — a high power-distance culture**
- Why: In **high power-distance cultures** (Hofstede's framework), people are more likely to defer to authority figures like doctors without question. Patients may have had especially strong expectations that the doctor's treatment would work.
- Consequence: High **expectancy (placebo) effects** may have inflated improvements in the ECT group; findings may not generalise to Western patients, who have greater media exposure to negative portrayals of ECT and may be more skeptical

❌ **Cannot separate biological effect from placebo effect**
- Why: None of the 18 Chinese trials used a sham ECT control condition
- Consequence: It is impossible to know whether improvements were due to ECT's neurological action or patients' beliefs and expectations — Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) showed sham ECT can produce equivalent improvement

❌ **Memory impairment and headaches reported as side effects**
- Why: ECT disrupts normal brain activity, and bilateral ECT (electrodes on both sides) is particularly associated with memory problems
- Consequence: The claim that ECT is "relatively safe" must be tempered — cognitive side effects can affect daily functioning and quality of life

---

**6. Exam tips**

> ⚡ Must-knows:
> - 18 RCTs, 1,769 participants, mostly from China
> - ECT + clozapine outperformed clozapine alone, effects visible by week 1–2
> - Key weakness: Chinese samples; high power-distance culture; possible placebo inflation
> - No sham controls = cannot rule out expectancy effects
> - Cultural difference point: findings may not generalise to Western patients

🧠 Quick check:
- What is a high power-distance culture and why does it matter for interpreting ECT research?
- Why does having 17/18 studies from China specifically limit generalisability?
- How does this study relate to Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017)?

📋 Skeleton answer — cultural issues evaluation point:

> A weakness of Wang et al.'s (2018a) meta-analysis is that 17 out of 18 included studies were conducted in China — a high power-distance culture in which people are generally less likely to question the wisdom of authority figures, including doctors. This means participants may have had particularly strong beliefs that the ECT treatment would be effective, leading to heightened expectancy (placebo) effects. As a result, the findings may not be generalisable to patients in Western cultures, where greater exposure to negative media portrayals of ECT may produce different expectancies and therefore different outcomes. This is particularly relevant given that Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017) found no significant difference between real and sham ECT, suggesting expectancy effects may account for a substantial portion of ECT's apparent efficacy.

## Study 5

### Sensky et al. (2000) — Can talking therapy work when drugs can't?

**1. Why this study exists**

Drugs are the frontline treatment for schizophrenia. But what about the people who are still experiencing symptoms even while on medication?

**Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT)** had already proven effective for depression and anxiety. Could it be adapted to help with the positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia — hallucinations, delusions, flat affect?

Sensky et al. (2000) set out to test this. Their team was brought together by **Aaron Beck** himself — the founder of CBT — making this a historically significant trial.

There was also a smart methodological challenge to solve: how do you rule out placebo effects in psychotherapy? You can't give someone a "sugar-pill therapy." Instead, Sensky et al. created a **befriending** control group — same amount of human contact, same empathic tone, but no CBT techniques.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

It's London, Newcastle, Cleveland, and Durham — 5 clinical services across England.

You are one of **90 participants**, aged 16–60. You have been diagnosed with schizophrenia (confirmed by both ICD-10 and DSM-IV criteria). You have been on at least 300mg of chlorpromazine daily for 6+ months — and you're still having positive symptoms. The drugs haven't been enough.

You are **randomly allocated** to one of two groups:

**Group A — CBT:**
- A trained nurse therapist works with you collaboratively.
- Sessions use specific techniques depending on your symptoms (see table below).
- You attend an average of **19 sessions over 9 months**.

**Group B — Befriending:**
- You also meet with a therapist for the same number of sessions, at the same intervals.
- But these sessions are conversational only — you talk about hobbies, sport, current events.
- The therapist is empathic and warm, but deliberately uses no CBT techniques.

Your symptoms are rated by **blind raters** (clinicians who don't know which group you're in) at three points:
1. **Baseline** — before therapy starts
2. **Post-treatment** — up to 9 months later
3. **Follow-up** — 9 months after therapy ended

Session tapes are **randomly audited by a blind rater** to check therapists didn't accidentally cross over techniques between conditions.

---

**CBT techniques used (Table 6.4 from textbook):**

| Symptom | Technique |
|---|---|
| **Hearing voices** | Kept symptom diaries; critical analysis of beliefs about voices; devised coping strategies |
| **Delusions** | **Socratic questioning** — probing questions to challenge irrational beliefs; **downward arrow technique** — tracing beliefs to their core; exploring alternative interpretations |
| **Disorganised thinking** | **Thought linkage** — repeatedly asking patients to explain connections between unrelated thoughts |
| **Negative symptoms** | **Paced activity scheduling** — structuring activity to avoid overwhelm and fatigue; pleasure/accomplishment diaries |

---

**3. What they found**

| Timepoint | CBT group | Befriending group |
|---|---|---|
| Post-treatment | Significant symptom reduction | Significant symptom reduction |
| 9-month follow-up | **Continued to improve** | Improvements **no longer evident** |

- Both groups improved during therapy — but only CBT showed **lasting benefits**.
- The befriending group's gains **disappeared** after sessions stopped.
- No difference in medication use between the groups — so improvement was not due to drug changes.
- A 5-year follow-up study confirmed: CBT produced significantly greater and more durable effects on overall symptom severity and negative symptoms.

---

**4. Why it matters**

- This is landmark evidence that **psychological therapy can work** even when drugs have not fully resolved symptoms.
- It shows CBT does not just temporarily soothe — it appears to create **lasting cognitive change** that continues after therapy ends.
- It legitimises CBT as a core component of schizophrenia treatment, not just a biological "last resort" framework.
- It also shows that social contact alone (befriending) has short-term benefits but cannot sustain improvement — real skill-building is needed.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Befriending control group — elegant design**
- Why: Controls for the non-specific benefits of human contact and therapist empathy (which could themselves improve symptoms)
- Consequence: Any advantage of CBT over befriending is specifically due to CBT techniques, not just social engagement — increases internal validity

✅ **Blind raters assessed outcomes**
- Why: Clinicians scoring symptoms didn't know which group participants were in, reducing assessment bias
- Consequence: Symptom scores are more objective — less likely to reflect the rater's expectations

✅ **Audiotaped sessions reviewed by blind rater**
- Why: Checks that befriending sessions stayed free of CBT elements, and that CBT sessions covered all required components
- Consequence: **Treatment fidelity** confirmed — we can trust that the two conditions were genuinely different

✅ **Sample drawn from 5 clinical services across 4 locations**
- Why: Urban (London) and more rural (Durham, Cleveland, Newcastle) settings included
- Consequence: **Generalisability** is enhanced — not just a London population

✅ **Longitudinal design with follow-up**
- Why: Three measurement points (baseline, post-treatment, 9-month follow-up) reveal whether gains are lasting
- Consequence: The critical finding — CBT gains hold; befriending gains vanish — would have been missed in a short study

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **CBT combined with drug treatment**
- Why: All participants remained on chlorpromazine throughout the study — it was not a drug-free CBT trial
- Consequence: We cannot conclude CBT alone is effective; it may be the combination of drugs + CBT that drives improvement

❌ **Participant dropout in a longitudinal study**
- Why: 6 participants failed to engage at all; 9 dropped out before receiving sufficient therapy
- Consequence: The remaining sample may be **systematically different** — e.g., only those with sufficient communication skills and motivation completed the study; results may not apply to all people with schizophrenia

❌ **CBT requires communication skills and motivation**
- Why: CBT is a collaborative, homework-based therapy; participants need to keep diaries, attend sessions, engage in Socratic dialogue
- Consequence: CBT may be selectively effective — people with severe negative symptoms (avolition, alogia), low literacy, or high disorganisation may be unable to engage adequately

❌ **Single-blind, not double-blind**
- Why: Participants knew which group they were in; those in the CBT group knew they were receiving the "real" therapy
- Consequence: **Expectancy effects** could partly explain CBT's advantage — knowing you're getting the "proper" treatment may itself boost outcomes

---

**6. Exam tips**

> ⚡ Must-knows:
> - 90 participants; 5 clinical services; 19 sessions over 9 months
> - Randomly allocated to CBT or befriending (matched contact time)
> - Both groups improved during therapy; only CBT group continued to improve at 9-month follow-up
> - Blind raters; audiotaped sessions reviewed — high internal validity
> - Weakness: all participants still on chlorpromazine; can't say CBT alone is effective

- What is the key difference between the CBT and befriending conditions?
- Why was it important to use blind raters AND to audiotape sessions?
- What does the 9-month follow-up finding tell us that the post-treatment data alone couldn't?

📋 Skeleton answer — 6 marks: "Describe the procedure of Sensky et al. (2000)"

> Sensky et al. (2000) recruited 90 participants aged 16–60, all diagnosed with schizophrenia (ICD-10 and DSM-IV) who continued to experience positive symptoms despite taking at least 300mg chlorpromazine daily for at least six months. Participants were randomly allocated to either a CBT group or a befriending control group. Therapy was delivered by trained nurses who received regular supervision. The CBT group received techniques including Socratic questioning for delusions, thought linkage for disorganised thinking, and paced activity scheduling for negative symptoms. The befriending group received the same number of sessions but discussed only neutral topics. Symptoms were assessed by blind raters at baseline, post-treatment, and at a nine-month follow-up.

📋 Skeleton answer — 10 marks (with idiographic vs nomothetic):

> Sensky et al. (2000) took a largely **nomothetic** approach — randomly allocating 90 participants and comparing group averages across standardised symptom scales. A strength of this is that the use of blind raters and randomisation allows the researchers to draw conclusions about CBT's effectiveness that can potentially be generalised to other people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia across a range of settings (5 clinical services, 4 UK locations).
>
> However, a limitation of this nomothetic approach is that it obscures individual differences in response to CBT. For example, Sensky et al. noted that 15 participants either failed to engage or dropped out. This suggests that CBT may only be effective for a particular subset of people — perhaps those with sufficient verbal fluency, motivation, and insight to engage in Socratic questioning and diary-keeping. A more **idiographic** approach — such as using semi-structured interviews or case studies — could reveal why some individuals respond while others don't, enabling therapists to tailor the intervention more effectively.
>
> A further strength is the **longitudinal design**, which tracked participants at three points: baseline, post-treatment, and 9-month follow-up. This is critical because the key finding — that CBT's benefits persist after therapy ends while befriending's do not — would have been invisible in a cross-sectional study. Longitudinal designs allow researchers to distinguish between temporary symptom relief and genuine, lasting cognitive change. The 9-month follow-up is particularly important given that schizophrenia is a chronic condition prone to relapse.
