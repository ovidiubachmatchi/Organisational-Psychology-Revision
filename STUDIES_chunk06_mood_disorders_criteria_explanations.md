## SECTION 1 — Diagnostic Criteria (6.2.1)

### Understanding the ICD-11 Criteria — Before the Studies

**Before diving in:** The ICD-11 is the World Health Organization's classification system for disorders. Think of it like a rulebook for doctors. These are the diagnostic benchmarks that the studies below were testing, measuring, and challenging.

**Unipolar Depressive Disorder** = mood drops into a very low place (depressive episodes only).
- Must last **at least 2 weeks**
- Symptoms include: depressed mood, loss of interest, fatigue, guilt, hopelessness, suicidal thoughts, sleep/appetite changes, poor concentration.
- Diagnosed as **recurrent** after 2+ episodes.
- Rated **mild / moderate / severe** based on impact on daily life.

**Bipolar Disorder Type 1** = both very low AND very high mood (manic episodes).
- **Manic episodes** last **at least 1 week**
- Symptoms: euphoria, grandiosity, fast speech, racing thoughts, reckless behaviour, reduced need for sleep.

**Bipolar Disorder Type 2** = depressive episodes + **hypomanic** (lower-intensity) episodes.
- Person is still functioning, not fully impaired.

**Other disorders:**
- **Dysthymia** = low-level depression, most of the time, for 2+ years (below the threshold for a full episode).
- **Cyclothymia** = mild ups and downs for 2+ years, never reaching full episode thresholds.

💡 *Analogy: Think of mood on a dial. Unipolar keeps dipping into the red. Bipolar swings between red and blue. Dysthymia sits permanently just inside the red zone without fully entering it.*

### Reed et al. (2018) — ICD-11 Reliability Across 13 Countries

**1. Why this study exists**

When different doctors can look at the same patient and come up with different diagnoses, the system is **unreliable** — and unreliable diagnoses lead to wrong treatment. Reed et al. tested whether the **ICD-11** was actually reliable in the real world across cultures and countries.

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you are a researcher. You recruit **nearly 2,000 patients** and **300+ clinicians** from **13 different countries**. Two independent clinicians each assess the same patient — separately, without talking to each other — using the new ICD-11 criteria. Then you compare: did they reach the same diagnosis?

This is called **inter-rater reliability** testing. It's like having two film critics watch the same movie and then independently writing their reviews — if they both call it "a psychological thriller," the classification worked.

**3. What they found**

| Disorder | Agreement Rate |
|---|---|
| Bipolar Disorder Type 1 | **84%** |
| Recurrent Depressive Disorder | **74%** |
| Bipolar Disorder Type 2 | **62%** |
| Dysthymic Disorder | **45%** |

**4. Why it matters**

A reliable diagnosis is the gateway to the right treatment. 84% agreement on bipolar type 1 is impressive — and an improvement over ICD-10. But 45% for dysthymia is barely better than flipping a coin. This means thousands of people may be misdiagnosed or missed entirely.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Large, international sample** → Because 13 countries were included, findings are more generalisable across cultures and healthcare systems.

✅ **ICD-11 outperforms ICD-10** → Improvements in the new system are real, not just theoretical.

❌ **Low reliability for some disorders** → 45% for dysthymia means clinicians are frequently disagreeing, calling into question whether the criteria are clear enough for some categories.

❌ **Clinician variability** → Clinicians vary in experience, culture, and language. Even if the criteria are good, different clinicians bring different biases to the interpretation.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ Key numbers: **84% (bipolar I), 74% (recurrent depression), 62% (bipolar II), 45% (dysthymia)** — these are what examiners want.

⚡ "ICD-11 is **reliable** for some disorders but **not all**" is a nuanced strength-and-weakness in one.

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Reed et al. (2018) found high inter-rater reliability for bipolar type 1 (84%) but low reliability for dysthymia (45%), suggesting that while ICD-11 has improved on ICD-10, some mood disorder categories remain difficult to diagnose consistently."

## SECTION 2 — The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)

### Beck Depression Inventory (Beck et al., 1996) — Measuring Depression Psychometrically

**1. Why this study exists**

Clinical interviews give qualitative information — they tell the story. But doctors also need **numbers** to track severity and compare across patients. The BDI was developed to give depression a score — a quantitative, standardised measure.

> 💡 *Think of it like a thermometer for depression: it doesn't tell you WHY you have a fever, but it tells you HOW HIGH it is.*

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

A patient sits with their doctor. They are given a questionnaire with **21 items** — things like sadness, pessimism, past failure, self-dislike, and indecisiveness. For each item, they choose from 4 statements scored 0, 1, 2, or 3 (from "not at all" to "most severe"). The whole thing takes **5–10 minutes**.

Their total score tells the clinician:

| Score | Severity |
|---|---|
| 0–13 | Minimal depression |
| 14–19 | **Mild** depression |
| 20–28 | **Moderate** depression |
| 29–63 | **Severe** depression |

**3. What they found**

The BDI has been validated extensively. Scores correlate positively with the **Hamilton Psychiatric Rating Scale for Depression** — a well-respected clinical measure. It also shows good **test–retest reliability** (same scores when taken a week apart).

**4. Why it matters**

Because it's quick, standardised, and scientifically validated, the BDI is used by:
- Researchers comparing treatment outcomes (e.g., does CBT reduce depression scores?)
- Clinicians in busy hospitals with limited time
- Studies like Seligman et al. (1988) to measure symptom severity before and after therapy

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Test–retest reliability** → Scores are stable over a week, meaning the measure is consistent, not random.

✅ **Concurrent validity** → Positively correlated with the Hamilton Scale, suggesting it measures something real and meaningful.

✅ **Quick and practical** → 5–10 minutes means it can be used in real clinical settings, unlike lengthy interviews.

❌ **Self-report bias** → Patients may over-report symptoms to access better care, or under-report due to shame. This is **social desirability bias** and reduces validity.

❌ **Response set** → With many similar-looking rating scale questions, people sometimes stop reading carefully and just tick the same number repeatedly. This makes data invalid.

❌ **Individual approach** → The BDI only collects data about the person's internal state — it doesn't capture their context, situation, culture, or history. This is an **individual vs situational** issue.

❌ **Cannot be used under 13** → Limited applicability for child cases.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ The BDI is the **nomothetic approach** — it applies standardised norms to all individuals.

⚡ Contrast it with **Lucy Johnstone's formulation approach** (idiographic — listen to each person's unique story).

⚡ Always link **self-report data** → **social desirability bias** → reduced **validity**.

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "A strength of the BDI is that it has test–retest reliability, meaning scores are stable over time, suggesting it consistently measures depression severity. However, a weakness is that it relies on self-reported data, which may be distorted by social desirability bias, reducing the validity of the measure."

## SECTION 3 — Biochemical Explanations

### The Monoamine Hypothesis (Schildkraut, 1965; Prange et al., 1974)

**Not a single study — this is a theoretical framework that evolved from clinical observations.**

**1. Why it exists**

Doctors noticed that **reserpine** (a blood pressure drug) caused depression as a side effect. They knew reserpine depletes **noradrenaline**. Conclusion? Low noradrenaline = depression. Later, **serotonin** was found to regulate noradrenaline — so if serotonin is off, noradrenaline can become too low (depression) or too high (mania). Together = the **monoamine hypothesis**.

💡 *Analogy: Serotonin is the thermostat. Noradrenaline is the temperature. If the thermostat breaks, the temperature goes wild — too cold (depression) or too hot (mania).*

**2. Key mechanisms of serotonin imbalance**

| Cause | Effect |
|---|---|
| Low dietary tryptophan | Less serotonin produced |
| High cortisol (from stress) | Serotonin production inhibited |
| Faulty transporter molecules (5-HTT) | Serotonin removed too fast or too slow |
| Too much MAO enzyme | Serotonin broken down too quickly |
| Over-sensitive receptors | Communication goes wrong even when serotonin is present |

**3. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ Supported by experiments (see Moreno et al., 1999 below).

✅ Led to effective drug treatments (SSRIs, MAOIs, Tricyclics).

❌ **Overly deterministic** — assumes all people respond the same way to serotonin changes. Strickland et al. (2002) found depressed women actually had **higher** serotonin than non-depressed women, challenging the simple model.

❌ **Reductionist** — ignores social, psychological, and environmental factors.

❌ **Nature vs nurture issue**: Raleigh et al. (1984) found that a social status change in monkeys caused a 40% serotonin drop — suggesting the environment shapes biochemistry, not just genes.

### Moreno et al. (1999) — Tryptophan Depletion and Depressive Symptoms

**1. Why this study exists**

Scientists wanted to test the monoamine hypothesis **experimentally**. If serotonin really causes depression, then artificially lowering serotonin levels should **increase** depressive symptoms. But you can't remove serotonin directly — so they removed **tryptophan**, the amino acid the body uses to make serotonin.

> 💡 *Think of tryptophan as the raw ingredient for making serotonin. Remove the ingredient, you get less of the product. Less product = brain goes dark.*

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Participants arrive at a lab. Some have a history of depression (currently in remission). Others are healthy controls, matched for age and gender.

They drink a **chocolate-flavoured drink**. Sounds pleasant — but it's been specially formulated to be **tryptophan-free**. It floods the body with other amino acids, which "crowd out" tryptophan from getting into the brain. Without tryptophan reaching the brain, serotonin production drops.

24 hours later, researchers measure depressive symptoms using the **Hamilton Depression Scale (HAM-D)**.

**3. What they found**

- People with a **history of depression** showed a **significant increase in depressive symptoms** within 24 hours of the drink.
- The **matched control group** also showed increased symptoms, though less dramatically.
- This provides a **causal link** — not just a correlation — between serotonin (via tryptophan) and depression.

**4. Why it matters**

This is one of the strongest pieces of experimental evidence for the monoamine hypothesis. By **manipulating** tryptophan (the IV) and **measuring** depression scores (the DV), the researchers could establish **cause and effect** — something correlational studies cannot do.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Experimental design** → Manipulation of an IV (tryptophan depletion) allows cause and effect conclusions. This is rare in depression research.

✅ **Control group** → Age- and gender-matched controls increase **internal validity**. Differences in results cannot be explained by participant variables.

✅ **Objective measure** → HAM-D is a validated, clinician-rated scale (not just self-report), improving validity.

❌ **Lab setting → low ecological validity** → Drinking a tryptophan-free drink in a lab is artificial. Real-world serotonin changes happen more gradually and through complex interactions.

❌ **Small and specific sample** → People with a remitted history of depression are a niche group. Findings may not generalise to first-episode patients or those currently medicated.

❌ **Short-term effects only** → Measuring symptoms 24 hours later doesn't tell us about long-term depression, which involves weeks or months of sustained mood disruption.

❌ **Ethical concerns** → Deliberately inducing depressive symptoms, even temporarily, raises ethical questions about psychological harm.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ This is the go-to study for **supporting the biochemical explanation experimentally**.

⚡ Key phrase: "Moreno et al. used tryptophan depletion to experimentally reduce serotonin, finding increased depressive symptoms — establishing a **causal** link."

⚡ Link to the monoamine hypothesis — this study supports Schildkraut (1965) and Prange et al. (1974).

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Moreno et al. (1999) gave participants a tryptophan-free drink to reduce serotonin production. They found a significant increase in depressive symptoms measured by the Hamilton Scale 24 hours later, in both participants with a history of depression and matched controls. This supports the monoamine hypothesis by demonstrating a causal link between serotonin reduction and depressive symptoms."

### Raleigh et al. (1984) — Social Status Changes Serotonin Levels

**1. Why this study exists**

This study is used as a **counterpoint** to the purely biological explanation. If depression is purely biochemical, serotonin levels should be stable and genetically fixed. But Raleigh found that something environmental — a change in **social rank** — dramatically changed serotonin.

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Adult male vervet monkeys live in social groups. Some are **dominant** (the boss), others are **subordinate** (lower down the hierarchy). Researchers measured serotonin levels in these monkeys, then deliberately changed the social hierarchy — a subordinate monkey was placed in a new group where he became dominant, and the previous dominant monkey was demoted.

They measured serotonin levels before and after the social change.

**3. What they found**

| Condition | Serotonin Change |
|---|---|
| Monkey demoted to subordinate | **−40%** reduction in serotonin |
| Monkey promoted to dominant | **+60%** increase in serotonin |

**4. Why it matters**

Serotonin is not fixed by biology alone. The **social environment** — specifically one's status and sense of control — directly affects brain chemistry. This is a powerful argument for **nurture** and against pure biological determinism.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Experimental control** → Social status was deliberately manipulated, allowing causal conclusions.

✅ **Objective neurochemical measures** → Serotonin levels are biological, not self-reported.

❌ **Animal sample** → Vervet monkeys are not humans. Social hierarchies in monkeys may not map onto the complex social dynamics humans face. **Generalisability is limited**.

❌ **Ethical issues** → Deliberately changing social status of animals may cause distress and raises animal welfare concerns.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ Use this as a **nature vs nurture** point: "Raleigh et al. shows that serotonin is shaped by environment, not just genes — challenging the biological reductionist view."

## SECTION 4 — Genetic Explanations

### Kendler et al. (2006) — Twin Study of Lifetime Major Depression

**1. Why this study exists**

If depression is genetic, **identical twins** (who share 100% of DNA) should both have it far more often than **non-identical twins** (who share ~50% of DNA). Kendler ran the largest twin study of its kind to find out.

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you have access to the **Swedish national twin registry** — a database of more than 42,000 twins. Kendler and colleagues assessed **lifetime major depression** in 15,493 complete twin pairs using diagnostic interviews. They compared **monozygotic (MZ)** twin pairs (identical) with **dizygotic (DZ)** twin pairs (non-identical).

If both twins in a pair had depression, that's a **concordant** pair. The percentage of pairs that are concordant = the **concordance rate**.

> 💡 *Think of it as: if your twin has it and so do you, your DNA probably played a role. If your twin has it but you don't, the environment must account for the difference.*

**3. What they found**

| Group | MZ Concordance | DZ Concordance |
|---|---|---|
| Females | **44%** | **16%** |
| Males | **31%** | **11%** |
| Heritability (females) | **~42%** | — |
| Heritability (males) | **~29%** | — |

Key insight: Depression is **more heritable in women than men**.
Also: **Bipolar disorder** is even more heritable — 62% for MZ vs 8% for DZ (Bertelsen et al., 1977).

**4. Why it matters**

- Even 44% concordance in identical twins means **genetics is not the whole story** — environment matters too.
- The sex difference suggests that the genetic architecture of depression may differ between males and females.
- Bipolar disorder appears far more genetically determined than unipolar depression.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Enormous sample** (n = 42,000+) → Statistical power is very high. Findings are unlikely to be a fluke.

✅ **National registry** → Using a population registry reduces selection bias compared to clinical samples.

❌ **Equal environment assumption** → Twin studies assume MZ and DZ twins experience equally similar environments. But MZ twins look identical and are always the same sex, so they are treated more similarly. This inflates MZ concordance rates and **exaggerates the genetic contribution**.

❌ **Not 100% concordance** → Even MZ twins only reach 44% concordance. This proves environment matters — genetics alone cannot explain depression.

❌ **Doesn't identify specific genes** → Knowing that genes matter is different from knowing which genes matter.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ Key numbers: **44% (female MZ), 16% (female DZ), 31% (male MZ), 11% (male DZ)**.

⚡ Always say: "The fact that concordance is never 100% shows that **environment also plays a role** (nature AND nurture)."

⚡ Compare with bipolar: **62% MZ** vs **8% DZ** — much stronger genetic signal.

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Kendler et al. (2006) found 44% concordance for major depression in female MZ twins versus 16% in female DZ twins in a sample of over 42,000 Swedish twins, suggesting a significant genetic component. However, concordance was not 100%, supporting the role of environmental factors in the development of depression."

### Caspi et al. (2003) — The 5-HTT Gene, Stress, and Depression

**1. Why this study exists**

Not everyone who experiences a stressful life event gets depressed. Why? Caspi et al. wanted to know whether a specific **gene variant** (the short allele of the 5-HTT gene) made people more **sensitive to stress** — so that the same amount of life stress would hit them harder than others.

> 💡 *This is a gene × environment interaction study. The gene is the gun; the stress is the trigger. Without both, nothing fires.*

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Caspi used the **Dunedin longitudinal cohort** — a group of over **1,000 people** in New Zealand who had been tracked from birth. This is a **prospective** study, meaning data was collected over time, not retrospectively.

When participants turned **26**, researchers:
1. Took a **blood/DNA sample** — specifically looking at their **5-HTT gene** (which codes for the serotonin transporter molecule).
2. Each person has two copies of this gene, and each copy can be either a **short allele (s)** or a **long allele (l)**.
3. Asked participants about **stressful life events** experienced in the previous 5 years (e.g., job loss, bereavement, relationship breakdown).
4. Assessed whether they had experienced **depression** (using clinical interview and self-report).

They then grouped participants by genotype: **ll (two long), sl (one of each), ss (two short)**.

**3. What they found**

| Genotype | Depression risk after stress |
|---|---|
| ll (long/long) | **Lowest** risk |
| sl (short/long) | **Intermediate** risk |
| ss (short/short) | **Highest** risk |

The more stressful life events a person had experienced, the stronger the effect of the ss genotype.
People with ss who experienced 4+ stressful events had the **highest rates of depression**.

**4. Why it matters**

- Explains why the **same stressor** hits different people differently — it's partly genetic.
- Supports the **diathesis-stress model**: you need **both** a genetic vulnerability (short allele) AND environmental stress for depression to emerge.
- Has implications for **personalised medicine** — knowing someone's genotype could predict who needs earlier mental health support after trauma.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Prospective longitudinal design** → Because data was collected at age 26 about events in the past 5 years, this reduces retrospective bias compared to asking depressed people to remember their past.

✅ **Large representative cohort** → The Dunedin study followed a birth cohort, not a clinical sample, making findings more generalisable to the general population.

✅ **Gene × environment interaction** → More sophisticated than just studying genes or just studying stress — captures the interaction between the two.

❌ **Replication issues** → Subsequent **meta-analyses** have shown mixed replication results. Some large studies did not find the same interaction, calling the robustness of the finding into question (Risch et al., 2009).

❌ **Self-reported stress** → Participants reported their own life events, which are subjective and may be influenced by their current depressive state (depressed people may perceive or recall more negative events).

❌ **Correlation, not experiment** → Genotype was measured, not manipulated. Cannot prove the short allele **causes** depression — it may be associated with other variables.

❌ **Polygenic complexity** → Depression involves many genes, not just 5-HTT. Focusing on one gene is reductionist.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ Caspi is the go-to study for **gene × environment interaction** and the **diathesis-stress model**.

⚡ Key phrase: "Two copies of the short allele + multiple stressful life events = highest depression risk."

⚡ Weakness to mention: "Large GWAS studies have **failed to consistently replicate** specific gene associations with depression (Hek et al., 2013), suggesting the genetic picture is more complex than any single gene."

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Caspi et al. (2003) tested 847 participants from the Dunedin birth cohort for their 5-HTT genotype and measured exposure to stressful life events at age 26. They found that individuals carrying two copies of the short allele were significantly more likely to develop depression following stressful events than those with two long alleles, supporting a gene–environment interaction model of depression."

## SECTION 5 — Key Study: Oruč et al. (1997) — Candidate Gene Analysis in Bipolar Disorder

**1. Why this study exists**

If serotonin is central to mood disorders (monoamine hypothesis), then genes that **code for serotonin-related proteins** should be different in people with bipolar disorder. Oruč et al. tested two specific candidate genes: the **5-HT2c receptor gene** (codes for postsynaptic serotonin receptors) and the **5-HTT gene** (codes for the serotonin transporter — the molecule that sucks serotonin back into the presynaptic cell after use).

> 💡 *It's like checking if two specific parts of the serotonin machinery are faulty in people with bipolar disorder.*

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

In **Zagreb, Croatia**, researchers recruited two groups from psychiatric hospitals:
- **42 patients with bipolar disorder type I** (25 females, 17 males; age 31–70, average age of onset: 32)
- **40 healthy controls** — hospital staff, friends, and family (25 females, 15 males), matched for age and sex

All bipolar patients were assessed by **two experienced psychiatrists** using the **Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia – Lifetime Version (SADS-L)**, translated into Croatian.

Then: **blood samples** were taken from everyone. DNA was extracted and analysed to identify which **alleles** participants carried for each gene.

For the **5-HTR2c gene**: alleles are called **Cys (C)** and **Ser (S)**.
For the **5-HTT gene**: alleles are called **1** and **2**.

The question: Are certain allele combinations (polymorphisms) more common in people with bipolar?

**3. What they found**

- **No significant overall difference** in allele frequencies between the bipolar and control groups for either gene.
- **But** — when they split results by sex:
  - **Female bipolar patients** had a **slightly higher frequency of the S allele** of the 5-HTR2c gene than female controls (trend: p = 0.051).
  - **Female bipolar patients** were also slightly more likely to carry **allele 1** of the 5-HTT gene (trend: p = 0.049).
- Notably, these trends were only present in **heterozygotic females (CS genotype)**, not in those with two identical alleles.

**4. Why it matters**

- The study **did not prove** these genes cause bipolar disorder — but it **didn't rule them out** for females.
- Suggests **sex differences** in the genetic underpinnings of bipolar disorder — females may be more sensitive to serotonin gene variations.
- Replicates non-significant findings from Gutiérrez et al. (1996) and Kelsoe et al. (1996) — strengthening the **reliability** of the null result.
- Supports the emerging field of **pharmacogenomics** — matching drug treatment to a person's genome.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Rigorous diagnosis** → Each patient was assessed by two experienced psychiatrists using SADS-L, reducing misdiagnosis (important because bipolar is easily confused with schizoaffective disorder).

✅ **Replication of previous studies** → The null results replicate Gutiérrez et al. (1996) and Kelsoe et al. (1996), strengthening the **reliability** of the finding. Replication is a hallmark of science.

✅ **Sex-disaggregated analysis** → By analysing males and females separately, the study revealed patterns that would have been hidden in a combined analysis.

❌ **Very small sample** → Only 42 patients. With only 3 males in the SS genotype group, the study had insufficient **statistical power** to detect small genetic effects. Important effects may have been missed.

❌ **Opportunity sampling** → Participants were recruited from hospital staff, friends, and family — not a random or representative sample. This reduces **generalisability**.

❌ **Biological reductionism** → Focuses on two specific polymorphisms only. Ignores the **polygenic** nature of bipolar disorder, epigenetics, and the complex interaction between genes and environment.

❌ **Croatian sample only** → All participants were Croatian. Genetic populations vary. Results may not generalise to other ethnic groups.

❌ **Correlational design** → Even if the alleles were more common in bipolar patients, this wouldn't prove causation. Many other genes and environmental factors could explain the association.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ Main finding: **No significant association** overall — but **sex-specific trends** in females.

⚡ Key strength: **replication** — replicates two previous null findings, increasing reliability of the null result.

⚡ Key weakness: **small sample** — especially problematic because bipolar is a complex, polygenic condition.

⚡ For determinism vs free will: "Oruč et al. could be used to support a weak determinist view — genes may influence vulnerability, but as Oruč found only trends (not certainties), free will still has a role."

⚡ For reductionism: "Studying only two genes is biologically reductionist — it ignores epigenetics, environmental triggers, and polygenic complexity."

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Oruč et al. (1997) extracted DNA from blood samples of 42 Croatian patients with bipolar disorder type I and 40 matched controls to test for polymorphisms in the 5-HT2c and 5-HTT genes. No significant overall differences were found, but trends emerged in female patients, suggesting these genes may confer a small sex-specific vulnerability to bipolar disorder. The study's strength lies in its rigorous diagnostic assessment, but its small sample size limits power and generalisability."

## SECTION 6 — Psychological Explanations

### Beck (1962) — Cognitive Theory of Depression

**1. Why this study exists**

Beck challenged the dominant (at the time) psychoanalytic view that depression was about repressed anger turned inward. Instead, he observed his depressed patients and noticed something striking: they all **thought in the same distorted, negative ways**. He concluded: **the thoughts cause the depression**, not the other way around.

**2. The theory — told as a story**

Imagine a child named Sam. Sam grows up with a critical parent who says things like "You never do anything right." Sam's teacher ignores him. His peers bully him. Over years, Sam develops a deep **core belief**: "I am worthless."

This belief doesn't disappear — it becomes a **lens** through which Sam sees everything. As an adult, if Sam gets constructive feedback at work, he doesn't hear "room for improvement" — he hears "proof that I'm worthless." He filters out the positive and zooms in on the negative. This is called a **negative cognitive schema**.

> 💡 *Beck called this the negative cognitive triad — three interconnected dark beliefs:*
> - **About the self** → "I am worthless."
> - **About the world** → "Everything is doomed to failure."
> - **About the future** → "Nothing will ever change."

These three fuel each other. The schema activates **faulty thinking patterns** (cognitive biases) automatically and relentlessly.

**Faulty thinking strategies (cognitive distortions):**

| Distortion | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Dichotomous thinking** | "Either I'm perfect or I'm a total failure" — black or white, no grey |
| **Arbitrary inference** | Drawing negative conclusions without evidence ("They didn't text back — they hate me") |
| **Catastrophising** | A tiny problem triggers worst-case-scenario thinking ("I got one question wrong, I'll fail, I'll get no job, I'll die alone") |
| **Personalisation** | Blaming yourself for things outside your control ("I got laid off because I'm useless", not because the company had losses) |

**3. Why it matters**

Beck's theory directly led to **Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)** — one of the most effective treatments for depression in the world. If negative thoughts cause depression, then changing thoughts should reduce depression. This is testable, practical, and empowering.

**4. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Practical application** → CBT is based on Beck's model and has strong evidence of effectiveness, including from Seligman et al. (1988). A theory that produces effective treatment has real-world validity.

✅ **Scientific testability** → Unlike psychoanalysis, cognitive theories generate **testable hypotheses** (e.g., do pessimistic people get more depressed?).

❌ **Cause or effect?** → It's unclear whether negative thinking **causes** depression or is a **symptom** of it. Depressed people may think negatively because they are depressed, not the other way around. This is the biggest weakness of the cognitive model.

❌ **Ignores biology** → Beck's model doesn't account for the genetic, neurochemical, and hormonal factors that clearly play a role (as shown by Moreno et al., Kendler et al., Caspi et al.).

❌ **Reductionist in its own way** → Reducing depression to "faulty thinking" ignores poverty, abuse, social isolation, and systemic factors.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ The **negative cognitive triad** = self, world, future — learn this.

⚡ Beck's theory explains the mechanism by which early childhood experiences create vulnerability → later depression.

⚡ Use Seligman et al. (1988) as **supporting evidence** for the cognitive model.

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Beck (1962) proposed that depression results from negative core beliefs about the self, the world, and the future (the negative cognitive triad), developed through early negative experiences. These beliefs activate cognitive distortions such as catastrophising and personalisation, maintaining and deepening depressive symptoms."

### Maier & Seligman (1976) — Learned Helplessness in Dogs

**1. Why this study exists**

Before Seligman turned to human depression, he made a shocking discovery: when animals are exposed to **uncontrollable** negative events, they stop trying to escape even when escape becomes possible. They "give up." He called this **learned helplessness** — and proposed that human depression works the same way.

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Dogs were divided into two groups.

**Group 1 (experimental):** Dogs were put in a harness and given **electric shocks they could not escape**. Nothing they did stopped the shocks. They learned: "I have no control."

**Group 2 (control):** Dogs could press a panel with their nose to turn off the shocks. They had control.

Next, **both groups** were put in a box with a **low barrier** dividing it. Shocks were delivered through the floor of one side. Any dog could easily jump the barrier to escape.

The control dogs jumped over quickly. The experimental dogs? They **lay down and whimpered**. They didn't even try to escape — even though escape was easy. They had learned that their actions didn't matter.

> 💡 *The experimental dogs had generalised helplessness from one situation to a completely new one. That overgeneralisation is the key.*

**3. What they found**

Experimental dogs (inescapable shock):
- Made no attempt to escape in the new environment
- Showed passive, withdrawn, "depressed" behaviour
- Generalised helplessness from the first enclosure to the new one

Control dogs: jumped the barrier quickly and escaped.

**4. Why it matters**

Seligman proposed that **human depression** works the same way. When people repeatedly experience events they can't control (abuse, poverty, repeated failure), they develop the belief that nothing they do matters. This **learned helplessness** leads to the passivity and hopelessness characteristic of depression.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Experimental control** → The only difference between groups was whether shocks were escapable. Cause and effect can be inferred.

✅ **Led to a testable theory** → The learned helplessness model was extended to humans and tested in clinical settings (Seligman et al., 1988).

❌ **Animal study — generalisation problem** → Dogs are not humans. Human depression involves complex cognitions (self-reflection, language, memory, cultural context) that cannot be modelled in dogs.

❌ **Ethical concerns** → Deliberate infliction of inescapable pain on animals raises serious ethical concerns about animal welfare.

❌ **Later revised** → Seligman later expanded the model to include **attributional style** (how people explain why bad things happen) — because the original model didn't explain why some people become helpless and others don't after the same experience.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ This study is the **foundation** for the attributional style model (Seligman et al., 1988).

⚡ Key concept: **overgeneralisation** from past experience of no control → passive acceptance in new situations.

⚡ Use the animal → human generalisation issue as a weakness consistently.

### Seligman et al. (1988) — Attributional Style, Cognitive Therapy, and Depression

**1. Why this study exists**

The original learned helplessness model didn't explain individual differences — why do some people become depressed after stress and others don't? Seligman refined the model by adding **attributional style**: the habitual way a person explains why bad things happen. He predicted that people with a **pessimistic** attributional style would be more vulnerable to depression.

This study tested:
1. Is pessimism correlated with depression severity?
2. Does therapy change attributional style?
3. Does changing attributional style predict recovery?

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine you are a researcher in 1988. You recruit **51 patients with mood disorders** from a clinical setting:
- 39 with **unipolar depression**
- 12 with **bipolar disorder**

Before therapy begins, everyone completes:
- The **Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)** — a measure of depression severity
- The **Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ)** — 12 hypothetical positive and negative scenarios. Participants explain why each happened. Their explanations are coded as internal/external, global/specific, stable/unstable.
- Additional symptom measures.
- A diagnostic interview using SADS (Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia)

Then all patients undergo **6 months of weekly cognitive therapy** (average: 22.5 sessions). At the **end of therapy**, 32 patients are reassessed. At **12-month follow-up**, 26 are assessed again.

All results are compared with a **matched non-depressed control group**.

**Pessimistic attributional style** = explaining bad events as:
- **Internal** ("It's my fault")
- **Global** ("This affects everything in my life")
- **Stable** ("This will always be the case")

| Dimension | Pessimistic (depressive) | Optimistic (non-depressive) |
|---|---|---|
| Internal vs External | "I wasn't good enough" | "We just weren't compatible" |
| Global vs Specific | "I'm rubbish at everything" | "At least I have my friends and job" |
| Stable vs Unstable | "I'll be single forever" | "I'll meet someone new when I'm ready" |

**3. What they found**

- **Before therapy:** Positive correlation between pessimism and depression severity (r = .56, p < .0002) — the more pessimistic, the more depressed.
- **After therapy:** Pessimism had **decreased significantly**, and depression scores had improved.
- **12-month follow-up:** Improvements were **stable**. Those who remained more pessimistic were more likely to **relapse**.
- **Unipolar vs Bipolar:** No significant difference in pessimism scores — both shared the same cognitive style.
- **Control group:** Attributional style was stable over time in non-depressed people — suggesting pessimism is a **trait**, not just a side-effect of being currently depressed.

**4. Why it matters**

This study provides strong evidence that:
1. Pessimism is a **risk factor** for depression (not just a symptom)
2. Cognitive therapy **changes** attributional style
3. The degree of change in thinking predicts **recovery and relapse risk**

This is the scientific backbone of CBT's effectiveness.

**5. Strengths ✅ and Weaknesses ❌**

✅ **Matched control group** → Allows comparison of how pessimism changes over time in non-depressed people. Shows pessimism is a **stable trait**, not a temporary reaction.

✅ **Method triangulation** → Depression measured via BDI (self-report) AND SADS interview (clinician-rated). Using multiple methods increases **validity**.

✅ **Longitudinal follow-up** → 12-month follow-up allows assessment of whether improvements were maintained — giving ecological depth.

✅ **Clinical relevance** → Directly linked to therapy outcomes. Pessimism at the end of therapy predicts relapse risk — this has real clinical value.

❌ **Gender imbalance** → 31 females vs 20 males. Findings may be more applicable to women than men, limiting generalisability.

❌ **High dropout rate** → Only 26 of the original 51 completed the 12-month follow-up. Those who dropped out may have been the most severely depressed — meaning the final sample is not representative. Results may be **overly optimistic**.

❌ **Correlational** → Even though therapy correlates with improvement, we cannot say therapy *caused* the change. Other factors (life circumstances, social support, medication) may explain recovery.

❌ **No control for therapy effects** → There was no "waiting list" or alternative therapy control group. We cannot rule out the possibility that **any therapy** (not specifically cognitive therapy) would have changed attributional style.

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ Key statistics: **r = .56, .57, .64** (correlations at intake, termination, and follow-up). These show pessimism and depression are consistently linked.

⚡ This study supports Beck's cognitive theory AND demonstrates the effectiveness of CBT.

⚡ The control group finding — pessimism is stable in non-depressed people — is crucial for arguing pessimism is a **cause**, not just an **effect**, of depression.

⚡ Weakness: dropout reduces generalisability — specifically, "may not represent those with most severe depression."

📋 Skeleton answer:
> "Seligman et al. (1988) assessed attributional style (using the ASQ) and depression severity (using the BDI) in 51 patients before and after cognitive therapy, and at 12-month follow-up. They found a significant positive correlation between pessimistic attributional style and depression severity at all time points (r = .56 to .64), and that improvements in pessimism during therapy were associated with reduced symptoms and lower relapse risk. A strength is the use of a matched control group showing that pessimism is a stable trait; a weakness is the high dropout rate (only 26/51 completed follow-up), limiting generalisability."

## EXAM FORMULA — How to Structure a 10-Mark Evaluate Question

1. **Strength 1** → State it → Explain why it's a strength → What does it allow us to conclude?
2. **Strength 2** → (optional for 10 marks)
