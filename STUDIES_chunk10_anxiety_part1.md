## Study 1

### Spitzer et al. (2006) — Developing the GAD-7 scale to quickly screen for Generalised Anxiety Disorder

---

### 1. Why this study exists

**Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD)** affects roughly **2–5% of people** (up to 9% of women in some samples).
It is characterised by intense, persistent, **free-floating anxiety** — worry that attaches to almost anything: money, family, health, work.

Before 2006, no quick and precise screening tool for GAD existed for busy primary care settings.
Doctors needed something **short enough to use in a 10-minute appointment** but accurate enough to tell mild from severe cases.

> 💡 Think of it like a smoke alarm: it needs to be sensitive enough to detect real fires, but not so sensitive it goes off every time someone makes toast.

The researchers' goal was to **develop and validate** a seven-item questionnaire that could do exactly that.

---

### 2. The procedure — told as a story

**Setting the scene:**
It is 2004. You are a patient walking into one of **15 primary care clinics across Missouri, USA**.
Before you even see your doctor, you are handed a sheet of paper with questions.

**Step 1 — Building the item pool.**
The research team started with **13 candidate questions**, all based on the DSM-IV criteria for GAD and existing anxiety measures.
They covered things like: *"Are you feeling on edge? Are you worrying uncontrollably? Do you feel restless?"*

**Step 2 — Collecting data at scale.**
A total of **2,740 adult patients** completed the 13-item questionnaire.
Within **one week**, 965 of those patients also completed a **telephone interview** with a trained mental health professional — this was the gold-standard check against which the questionnaire was measured.

**Step 3 — Cutting it down.**
The researchers ran statistical analyses to find the **7 items** that best correlated with the full 13-item scale.
The weakest, most redundant questions were cut.

**Step 4 — Test-retest reliability check.**
Participants completed the questionnaire **twice, one week apart**.
This tested whether scores stayed consistent over time (i.e., was it measuring something stable?).

**Step 5 — Scoring the final tool.**
Each of the 7 items is rated on a **4-point scale**:

| Response | Score |
|---|---|
| Not at all | 0 |
| Several days | 1 |
| More than half the days | 2 |
| Nearly every day | 3 |

Maximum total score: **21 points**.

**Step 6 — Interpreting the score.**

| Score | Severity |
|---|---|
| 0–4 | Minimal/no GAD |
| 5–9 | **Mild GAD** |
| 10–14 | **Moderate GAD** → refer to psychiatrist |
| 15–21 | **Severe GAD** → prioritise urgently |

---

### 3. What they found

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Diagnosed GAD patients' average score | **14.4 / 21** |
| Non-diagnosed patients' average score | **4.9 / 21** |
| Test-retest reliability | **r = +0.83** (strong positive correlation) |
| Sensitivity (catching true cases) | **89%** |
| Specificity (correctly excluding non-cases) | **82%** |

> 🧠 A correlation of **+0.83** is very strong — scores barely changed from one week to the next, which means the tool measures something stable.

The GAD-7 also showed **strong concurrent validity** — its scores correlated highly with scores from other respected anxiety measures.

---

### 4. Why it matters

- Gives doctors a **fast, evidence-based screening tool** (takes ~2 minutes).
- Helps **prioritise** who needs urgent psychiatric referral vs. who can be monitored.
- **Prevents unnecessary referrals**, reducing waiting lists for those who genuinely need urgent help.
- Used globally in research AND clinical practice.
- The questionnaire was developed from a **huge sample (n = 2,740)**, making it highly generalisable.

---

### 5. Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths ✅**

✅ **Strong test-retest reliability (r = +0.83)**
→ WHY it matters: Scores taken one week apart were nearly identical.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Clinicians can trust that a score reflects the patient's actual stable anxiety level, not just a bad day.

✅ **Quick and practical**
→ Just 7 items, yet retains excellent validity.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Doctors with limited time can still gather clinically useful data to triage patients appropriately.

✅ **Large and diverse sample (n = 2,740)**
→ Drawn from 15 different clinics across Missouri.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Findings generalise more widely than studies with small, narrow samples.

✅ **Strong concurrent validity**
→ GAD-7 scores correlated strongly with established anxiety measures.
→ CONSEQUENCE: We can be confident the tool measures what it claims to measure (GAD severity).

**Weaknesses ❌**

❌ **Only two assessments, one week apart**
→ WHY this is a problem: GAD is diagnosed only after **several months** of symptoms.
→ CONSEQUENCE: The GAD-7 provides a **snapshot**, not a longitudinal picture. It cannot confirm whether symptoms are chronic or just a temporary reaction to a stressful event.

❌ **Self-report format — subjective**
→ Patients rate their own feelings.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Social desirability bias, mood on the day, or poor self-awareness may distort scores. Scores may not reflect objective symptom severity.

❌ **Sample from Missouri only**
→ Cultural, economic and demographic factors vary by region.
→ CONSEQUENCE: The cut-off scores may not be equally valid in other countries or cultures.

❌ **Gender imbalance in prevalence data**
→ Spitzer et al. found 9% prevalence in women vs. 4% in men.
→ CONSEQUENCE: If the scale was normed on a predominantly female sample, it may not perform equally well for men.

---

### 6. Exam tips

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- GAD-7 = **7 items**, scored **0–21**, developed from original pool of **13 items**
- n = **2,740** participants from **Missouri, USA**
- Test-retest reliability: **r = +0.83** (one week apart)
- Diagnosed GAD = avg **14.4**; controls = avg **4.9**
- Cut-offs: 5 = mild, 10 = moderate (refer), 15+ = severe (prioritise)
- Weakness: only a **snapshot**, not longitudinal; self-report = subjective

📋 **Skeleton exam answer (evaluate the GAD-7):**

> "A strength of the GAD-7 is its **test-retest reliability**. Spitzer et al. (2006) found a strong positive correlation of **r = +0.83** between scores taken one week apart. This is important because a reliable test gives consistent results, meaning clinicians can trust the score reflects a genuine stable condition rather than a transient mood state. However, a weakness is that the study only assessed participants **twice, one week apart**, rather than over several months. Since a GAD diagnosis requires symptoms persisting for several months, it is unclear whether a GAD-7 score accurately reflects the **chronicity** of symptoms over time."

## Study 2

### Mas et al. (2010) — Creating the Blood Injection Phobia Inventory (BIPI) for Spanish speakers

---

### 1. Why this study exists

**Blood-injection-injury (BII) phobia** affects roughly **3.5% of the population**.
It is unusual among phobias because — instead of just raising heart rate — it can cause people to **faint** (vasovagal syncope) through a two-stage biological response.

Before 2010, virtually all validated phobia assessment tools were in **English**.
Spanish-speaking populations had no reliable, culturally appropriate instrument.

> 💡 Imagine trying to diagnose colour-blindness using a test that only works under certain lighting conditions — if the tool doesn't fit the population, the diagnosis is meaningless.

Mas et al.'s aim: **develop and validate** a blood and injection phobia questionnaire specifically for **Spanish-speaking people**.

---

### 2. The procedure — told as a story

**Step 1 — Recruiting two groups.**
The researchers recruited participants from **Andalusia, Spain** (average age: 24).

- **Group A (phobic group):** **39 people** with a confirmed diagnosis of blood phobia.
- **Group B (control group):** **135 matched controls** — people without blood phobia, matched to Group A on age, marital status and educational level.

> 💡 Matching is like choosing a comparison group carefully so that any differences in scores can be attributed to the phobia, not to the fact that one group is older or better educated.

**Step 2 — The questionnaire.**
Both groups completed a large questionnaire containing **50 situations** related to:
- Agoraphobia (e.g., being in crowds)
- Social phobia (e.g., speaking in public)
- Blood-injection phobia (e.g., seeing an injured person bleed)

For each situation, participants rated how often they experienced **27 different reactions** across three categories:

| Category | Example response |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | "I think I'm going to faint." |
| **Biological** | "My palms sweat." |
| **Behavioural** | "My legs shake." |

Ratings used a **4-point Likert scale** (0 = never, 3 = always).

**Step 3 — The phobic group did extra work.**
Group A (the phobic group) also:
- Completed a **structured interview** about their phobia history.
- Completed the **Fear Questionnaire (FQ)** — an established English-language phobia measure — to provide a comparison benchmark.

**Step 4 — Cutting the questionnaire down.**
The researchers used **statistical analysis** to remove items:
- **10 items removed** first: these showed no significant difference between phobic and control groups (useless items).
- Further **factor analysis** applied: kept only the **18 situations** that best distinguished phobic from non-phobic individuals.

> 💡 Factor analysis is like a filter. Imagine you have 50 different alarm systems. Factor analysis finds which ones actually go off only when there's a burglar — and which ones go off randomly. You keep only the reliable ones.

**Final BIPI structure:**
- **18 situations** (stimuli)
- **27 phobic responses** to rate per situation
- Total: up to **486 data points per person**

---

### 3. What they found

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Internal reliability (Cronbach's α) | **0.98** — extremely high |
| Factor analysis | One dominant factor — blood phobia is a **unitary construct** |
| Factor 1 explains | **76%** of stimulus variance; **74%** of phobic response variance |
| Concurrent validity | Strong positive correlation between BIPI and blood sub-scale of the FQ |
| Group difference | Phobic group scored **significantly higher** than controls on the BIPI |

> 🧠 A Cronbach's alpha of **0.98** means the 18 items are extremely consistent with each other — they all measure the same underlying thing.

---

### 4. Why it matters

- Gives Spanish-speaking clinicians a **culturally valid** diagnostic tool.
- The BIPI's detail (27 reactions × 18 situations) lets therapists **tailor treatment** to the individual — e.g., is the patient more scared of their own blood or someone else's?
- Helps **identify** people with BII phobia who might otherwise avoid medical care and risk their health.
- Comparable to English-language tools, enabling cross-cultural research.

---

### 5. Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths ✅**

✅ **Strong concurrent validity**
→ BIPI scores correlated strongly with the blood sub-scale of the established Fear Questionnaire (FQ).
→ CONSEQUENCE: Clinicians can trust BIPI results translate meaningfully across languages and cultures.

✅ **Rigorous item selection using factor analysis**
→ Items were statistically tested, not chosen by guesswork.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Every item in the final BIPI earns its place — it adds diagnostic value and removes noise.

✅ **Comprehensive coverage**
→ 27 reactions × 18 situations = 486 data points per person.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Therapists get a detailed, individualised profile of each patient's specific fears and reactions.

✅ **Matched control group**
→ Controls matched on age, marital status and education.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Group differences in scores are more likely due to the phobia itself, not confounding variables.

**Weaknesses ❌**

❌ **Gender imbalance in phobic group**
→ 30 women vs. 9 men in the phobic group (3:1 ratio).
→ WHY it matters: Men were severely underrepresented.
→ CONSEQUENCE: The BIPI may be better calibrated for detecting phobia in women. Male patients may be under-identified.

❌ **Self-report data — subjective**
→ Participants rated hypothetical reactions ("how would you feel if...") rather than actual measured responses.
→ CONSEQUENCE: There may be a mismatch between imagined and real responses. People may underestimate or overestimate their reactions.

❌ **Sample limited to Andalusia, Spain**
→ All participants were from one Spanish region.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Findings may not generalise to all Spanish-speaking populations (e.g., Latin American cultures may differ).

❌ **Small phobic group (n = 39)**
→ Very small clinical sample compared to control group (n = 135).
→ CONSEQUENCE: Statistical power to detect differences is limited; results may not be stable if replicated.

---

### 6. Exam tips

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- BIPI = **18 situations** × **27 responses** = 486 data points
- Groups: **39 phobic** vs. **135 matched controls**, all from Andalusia, Spain
- Cronbach's α = **0.98** (excellent reliability)
- Factor analysis identified **one factor** explaining ~75% of variance
- Validated against the **Fear Questionnaire (FQ)**
- Key weakness: **gender imbalance** (30F : 9M in phobic group); self-report = subjective

📋 **Skeleton exam answer (evaluate the BIPI):**

> "A strength of the BIPI is its **concurrent validity**. Mas et al. (2010) found a strong positive correlation between BIPI scores and the blood sub-scale of the Fear Questionnaire (FQ). This is important because if two established measures of the same construct correlate strongly, it increases confidence that the BIPI is genuinely measuring blood-injection phobia. However, a weakness is the **gender imbalance**: the phobic group contained three times as many females (n=30) as males (n=9). This matters because the BIPI may be better at detecting phobia symptoms in women, meaning male patients with BII phobia could be under-identified in clinical settings."

## Study 3

### Chapman and DeLapp (2013) — Nine sessions of CBT + Applied Tension cured a 42-year-old man of severe BII phobia

---

### 1. Why this study exists

**Blood-injection-injury (BII) phobia** is not just unpleasant — it is dangerous.
People with BII phobia **avoid doctors, blood tests, vaccinations and medical procedures**.
Left untreated, this avoidance can cost lives.

BII phobia is harder to treat than most phobias because of the **diphasic (two-stage) biological response**:

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | Heart rate and blood pressure **spike** (normal fear response) |
| **Stage 2 (vasovagal syncope)** | Blood pressure and heart rate **crash** → patient faints |

> 💡 It's like a car that revs its engine and then immediately stalls. The crash in blood pressure cuts oxygen to the brain — unconsciousness follows.

Standard exposure therapy (face your fear gradually) doesn't work if the patient **keeps fainting** during it — you can't learn that blood is safe if you keep blacking out.

Chapman and DeLapp (2013) wanted to show how **Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) combined with Applied Tension (AT)** — a technique that prevents fainting — could successfully treat a severe, long-standing BII phobia.

---

### 2. The procedure — told as a story

**Meet T.**
T is a **42-year-old Hispanic male**. For over **20 years**, he has been terrified of medical situations.
He faints during blood tests. He perceives doctors' waiting rooms as threatening.
His child has **autism spectrum disorder** and needs regular medical appointments — but T cannot go with him.
He feels guilt and shame. He has not seen a regular doctor in years (only a psychiatrist for anti-anxiety medication).
He exercises obsessively (running, cycling, weight training, swimming) to feel in control of his body.

> 🧠 T's history includes early vulnerability: his mother joked about his heart problems, his grandmother listened to ambulance scanners daily, and he witnessed the deaths of his grandfather, uncle and aunt from illness. Nurture, not just nature, shaped his phobia.

**Method: Single case study.**
Data collected using:
- **Quantitative:** self-report questionnaires (BAI, FSS-II, BISS, SUDS scores during sessions)
- **Qualitative:** diagnostic interview; T's verbal comments throughout treatment

**Baseline assessment (before treatment):**

| Measure | What it measures | T's baseline score |
|---|---|---|
| Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) | General anxiety (0–63) | **41 — severe anxiety** |
| Fear Survey Schedule II (FSS-II) | Fear of 51 objects/situations (7-point scale) | **6 ("terror")** for blood; **5 ("very much fear")** for needles, death, illness |
| Blood-Injection Symptom Scale (BISS) | 17 physical/psychological symptoms | **All 17 items endorsed** |

**The treatment: 9 sessions over 9 weeks.**

> 💡 Think of the 9 sessions as a climbing wall. T starts on the easiest holds at the bottom (just looking at a photo of a needle) and slowly works his way up to the hardest holds at the top (getting his own blood drawn at a clinic).

**Sessions 1–3: Build the foundation**
- **Psychoeducation:** T learns what BII phobia is, why he faints (diphasic response), what vasovagal syncope is.
- **Self-monitoring:** T starts completing the **Phobic Encounter Record (PER)** between sessions — every time he faces blood/medical stimuli, he writes down his thoughts, feelings and anxiety score (0–100, called the **SUDS — Subjective Units of Discomfort Scale**).
- **Cognitive restructuring:** T identifies **thinking errors** (e.g., "medical procedures are dangerous") and learns to challenge them.
- **Progress note:** T uses his new knowledge at the hairdresser — he feels panicky but stays seated, challenges his thoughts and calms down. First win.

**Session 4: Build the fear ladder**
- T creates a **10-item fear hierarchy**, each item rated 0–100 for distress:
  - Lowest: reading about blood tests
  - Highest: having his own blood drawn at a clinic
- The therapist explains SUDS scoring.
- **Progress note:** T feels optimistic. He buys a blood pressure cuff and a heart rate monitor to prepare himself.

**Session 5: Learn Applied Tension**
- The therapist **explains and demonstrates Applied Tension (AT)**:

> **Applied Tension (AT) — step by step:**
> 1. Sit in a comfortable chair.
> 2. Tense the muscles of your **arms, torso and legs** simultaneously.
> 3. Hold the tension for **10–15 seconds**.
> 4. Release for **20–30 seconds**.
> 5. Repeat **five times**.
>
> 💡 Tensing large muscle groups **raises blood pressure**, counteracting the vasovagal crash that causes fainting. It is the physiological opposite of fainting.

- T is asked to practise AT **five times a day** at home.
- **Progress note:** T hasn't faced phobic situations yet but feels more aware of early fainting cues.

**Session 6: First real exposure**
- In session: T watches **YouTube videos of people having blood tests**.
  - SUDS starts at 30, rises to 35 at the moment blood enters the tube.
  - He uses AT. SUDS stabilises at 35, then drops to 20.
  - Says: *"Not bad... slight nausea but not a big deal."*
  - Closer camera angle videos: similar SUDS.
- **Progress note (homework):** T completed item 1 on his hierarchy at home — SUDS was 40 but said *"it was fine."* He called this an *"immense success."*

**Session 7: His own blood**
- In session: T **pricks his finger** to check blood sugar and looks at his own blood.
  - SUDS before: 45. Drops to 20. No AT needed after first prick.
  - Two more finger pricks: SUDS stays at 20. He says *"about there."*
- **Progress note (homework):** T's wife checks his blood sugar three times using the finger-prick test.
  - SUDS = 45; used AT for first two tests; did the third without AT.
  - Looked at his blood for **five minutes**.

**Session 8: Watching blood donors**
- In session: T and therapist visit a **blood bank** (donation centre).
  - SUDS before: 65. Drops to 45 within 5 minutes, then to 30 within 10 minutes.
  - No AT used. No fainting.
  - Watches a **second donor**: SUDS = 0.
- T agrees to book his own blood test for the coming week.
- **Progress note:** T makes the appointment.

**Session 9: Blood pressure and solo exposure**
- In session: T takes his own blood pressure.
  - SUDS = 55, drops to 20. Second measurement: SUDS stays at 20.
- Plans to book a blood pressure test with a nurse during the week.
- **Progress note (between sessions):** T **has his own blood drawn at a clinic**.
  - Used AT only at the start. SUDS = 40, drops quickly to 0.
  - Second blood draw from other arm: SUDS peaks at 25.

**After session 9:**
T takes his blood pressure at a pharmacy. A nurse takes it. A doctor examines him.
No AT needed. SUDS: *"rather low."*
T says: **"I never felt better in my life."**

**Follow-up:**

| Time point | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **4 months post-treatment** | T thanks therapist; multiple doctor's appointments booked |
| **10 months post-treatment** | Still doing well |
| **12 months post-treatment** | Completes follow-up questionnaires |

**12-month follow-up scores vs. baseline:**

| Measure | Baseline | 12-month follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| BAI (anxiety) | **41 (severe)** | **7 (low)** |
| FSS-II (blood) | **6 ("terror")** | No items rated "very much fear" or "terror" |
| BISS (symptoms) | **17/17 endorsed** | **4/17 symptoms** (anxious, heart pounding, nauseous, sweating) |

---

### 3. What they found

> T went from **severe, 20-year BII phobia** — unable to attend his child's medical appointments — to **voluntarily booking his own doctor's visits** within 9 weeks.

- SUDS scores **consistently declined** across sessions and tasks.
- Qualitative data showed T moved from terror to confidence.
- At 12-month follow-up, **all major anxiety measures had dropped dramatically**.
- The combination of **psychoeducation + cognitive restructuring + graduated exposure + applied tension** was effective for a severe, complex case.

---

### 4. Why it matters

- Demonstrates CBT+AT can work for **severe, long-standing BII phobia** (20+ years).
- Shows treatment is possible in **outpatient community settings** — not just specialised hospital clinics.
- Highlights the importance of **applied tension** as a unique addition to standard CBT for BII (unlike other phobias, fainting is a real barrier to exposure).
- Provides a **detailed clinical roadmap** other therapists can adapt for their own clients.
- Improved T's **quality of life** and potentially saved his life by enabling routine medical check-ups.

---

### 5. Strengths and Weaknesses

**Strengths ✅**

✅ **Triangulation of data (quantitative + qualitative)**
→ SUDS scores tracked anxiety numerically across sessions; interviews captured T's subjective experience.
→ CONSEQUENCE: The combination increases the **credibility** of the findings — the numbers and the words tell the same story.

✅ **Detailed case history provided**
→ T's full background (family history, depression, OCD, social anxiety, panic disorder) is documented.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Clinicians reading the study can judge for themselves whether T's profile matches their own clients, helping them decide whether CBT+AT is appropriate for a given patient. This supports **transferability** despite the lack of a control group.

✅ **Longitudinal follow-up (12 months)**
→ Data collected at baseline, throughout treatment and 12 months later.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Shows the treatment had **lasting** effects, not just short-term improvements that faded.

✅ **Ethically strong**
→ T's anonymity protected (referred to only as "T").
→ He set the pace of exposure — nothing was forced.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Minimal psychological harm; beneficence demonstrated.

**Weaknesses ❌**

❌ **Single case study (n = 1) — cannot generalise**
→ Only one patient treated by one experienced therapist.
→ WHY it matters: We cannot know if results would replicate with a different patient, a different therapist or a different setting.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Findings have **low external validity** — the therapy may not work as well for everyone.

❌ **No control group**
→ There is no comparison group (e.g., a sham-treatment group).
→ WHY it matters: We cannot rule out that T recovered due to **expectancy effects** (believing he would get better) or natural remission rather than the therapy itself.
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cause and effect cannot be firmly established.

❌ **Self-reported SUDS scores — potentially invalid**
→ T rated his own distress aloud, in the presence of his therapist.
→ WHY it matters: T had **social anxiety** — he may have reported lower scores than he genuinely felt to please the therapist or avoid embarrassment.
→ CONSEQUENCE: The quantitative data may not accurately reflect T's genuine anxiety levels, inflating the apparent success of the treatment.

❌ **Idiographic, not nomothetic**
→ The study provides rich detail about one person but no group-level statistics.
→ WHY it matters: Average SUDS scores across a large sample would allow us to understand individual differences in treatment efficacy.
→ CONSEQUENCE: It is impossible to know whether T's exceptional outcome is typical or an outlier.

❌ **Practical barriers to access**
→ T had to travel **2.5 hours** each way for sessions.
→ WHY it matters: Most patients cannot access specialist BII clinics, especially in rural areas.
→ CONSEQUENCE: The treatment model shown here may not be replicable for many patients due to geographic and financial barriers.

---

### 6. Exam tips

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- T = 42-year-old Hispanic male, **20+ years** of BII phobia
- Method = **single case study**; collected quantitative (SUDS, BAI, FSS-II, BISS) + qualitative (interview, verbal comments)
- **9 sessions** using the *Mastering Your Fears and Phobias (MYFP)* manual
- **Diphasic response**: heart rate/BP spike, then crash (vasovagal syncope = fainting)
- **Applied Tension**: tense major muscle groups for 10–15 sec, release 20–30 sec, repeat 5× → raises BP → prevents fainting
- **Fear hierarchy**: 10 items, rated 0–100 SUDS; sessions moved from videos of blood tests → watching blood donors → giving own blood
- BAI: **41 (baseline) → 7 (12 months)**
- Key strengths: triangulation, detailed case history, 12-month follow-up
- Key weaknesses: no control group, n=1 (idiographic, can't generalise), self-reported SUDS (social desirability bias), practical barriers to access

📋 **Skeleton exam answer (evaluate Chapman & DeLapp, 2013):**

> "A strength of Chapman and DeLapp (2013) is the use of **triangulation** — both quantitative SUDS scores and qualitative interview data were collected. This is important because the two types of data corroborate each other, increasing the credibility of the findings. However, a critical weakness is the **absence of a control group**. Without a comparison group (e.g., a sham-treatment group or a waitlist control), it is impossible to determine whether T's improvement was due to the CBT+AT programme specifically, or simply the result of expectancy effects and his personal motivation to change his behaviour for the sake of his family. This limits the **internal validity** of the study."

> "A further weakness is the **idiographic** nature of the design: with only one participant, findings cannot be generalised (nomothetic). A larger-scale RCT including multiple patients would allow researchers to calculate average improvement scores and quantify how much individual differences affect treatment outcomes."

## Key Terms — Quick Reference Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **GAD** | Generalised Anxiety Disorder — persistent, excessive anxiety about everyday things |
| **Free-floating anxiety** | Anxiety not attached to a specific cause; "general dread" |
| **GAD-7** | 7-item questionnaire scoring 0–21 to screen for GAD severity |
| **Test-retest reliability** | Consistency of scores when the same test is repeated after a short interval |
| **Concurrent validity** | A test is valid if it correlates strongly with another established measure of the same thing |
| **BII phobia** | Blood-Injection-Injury phobia — irrational fear of blood, needles and injury |
| **Diphasic response** | Two-stage biological reaction in BII: first BP rises, then crashes → fainting |
| **Vasovagal syncope** | Fainting caused by sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure |
| **Applied Tension (AT)** | Tensing major muscle groups to raise blood pressure and prevent fainting during exposure |
| **BIPI** | Blood Injection Phobia Inventory — 18-situation × 27-response assessment tool for Spanish speakers |
| **Likert scale** | Rating scale with numbered points (e.g., 0–3 or 0–6) |
| **Factor analysis** | Statistical technique that identifies which items cluster together measuring the same underlying construct |
| **Cronbach's alpha** | Measure of internal consistency (reliability); 0.98 = near-perfect |
| **SUDS** | Subjective Units of Discomfort Scale — 0–100 anxiety self-rating used during exposure |
| **Graduated exposure** | Systematic, step-by-step confrontation of feared stimuli, from least to most feared |
| **Fear hierarchy** | Ordered list of feared situations from least to most distressing |
| **Idiographic** | Focusing on the individual (e.g., case study) |
| **Nomothetic** | Focusing on groups to establish general laws |
| **Psychoeducation** | Teaching a patient about their disorder and treatment rationale |
| **Cognitive restructuring** | Identifying and challenging irrational/unhelpful thoughts |
| **Agoraphobia** | Fear and avoidance of situations where escape seems difficult or help unavailable |
