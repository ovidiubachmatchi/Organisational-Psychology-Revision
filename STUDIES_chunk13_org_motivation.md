## Overview: What is This Chapter About?

This chapter asks one simple question: **why do people work hard?**

Psychologists have proposed three families of answers:

| Family | Core Idea | Theorists |
|---|---|---|
| **Need theories** | People work to satisfy internal needs | Maslow, McClelland |
| **Cognitive theories** | People calculate expected rewards and set goals | Locke & Latham, Vroom |
| **Motivators at work** | The *type* of reward matters, not just its size | Deci & Ryan, Landry et al. |

## STUDY 1

### Maslow (1943) — The Hierarchy of Needs

**1. Why This Study Exists**

In 1943, Abraham Maslow asked a deceptively simple question: **"What do human beings fundamentally need?"**
He wasn't just curious. He wanted a theory that could explain *why* some people seem driven and others do not.
His answer: we are all climbing the same ladder. You can't climb to the top if the bottom rungs are broken.

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

Maslow did **not** run a controlled experiment. His was a **theoretical paper** — he synthesised observations from clinical practice, biography, and earlier philosophical work.

Think of him as an architect drawing blueprints for a building he has observed thousands of people trying to build.

He proposed a pyramid of five needs, always bottom-up:

```
         [5] SELF-ACTUALISATION
             (become your best self)
        [4] ESTEEM
            (respect, achievement, status)
       [3] LOVE & BELONGINGNESS
           (friendship, family, feeling accepted)
      [2] SAFETY
          (job security, stable income, safe environment)
     [1] PHYSIOLOGICAL
         (food, water, warmth, sleep)
```

💡 **Analogy:** Think of a smartphone. It won't run Spotify (self-actualisation) if the battery is dead (physiological). It won't connect to Instagram (belongingness) if there's no signal (safety/wi-fi). Fix the base first.

**Deficiency needs (levels 1–4):** You are *pushed* toward them by a lack. The emptier you feel, the stronger the drive.
**Growth needs (level 5):** Once you have enough, you are *pulled* toward becoming more. The more you grow, the more you want.

Key rule: **you must fully satisfy a lower level before the next level becomes motivating.**

---

**3. What They Found**

This is a theory, not an experiment — so "findings" = key claims:

| Level | Workplace Example | If NOT met... |
|---|---|---|
| Physiological | Enough salary to eat and stay warm | Employee cannot focus on anything else |
| Safety | Job security, pension, safe conditions | Fear overrides all other motivation |
| Belongingness | Team culture, social interaction | Isolation, disengagement |
| Esteem | Promotion, recognition, autonomy | Resentment, stagnation |
| Self-actualisation | Creative challenges, growth projects | No deep engagement, just compliance |

> "If all these needs are met, then workers will be happy and secure within their workplace and have high levels of motivation." — Textbook

---

**4. Why It Matters**

Maslow gave managers a **diagnostic tool**. Instead of guessing why someone is unmotivated, a manager can ask: *which rung of the ladder is missing?*

- Workers worried about money? Fix pay (rung 1–2) before expecting passion (rung 5).
- Workers feel isolated? Fix belonging (rung 3) before expecting top performance (rung 5).
- Workers seem bored despite good pay? They may need self-actualisation opportunities (rung 5).

This was revolutionary. It said: **motivation is not one-size-fits-all. It is sequential.**

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Holistic** — Unlike purely cognitive theories, Maslow considers physiological AND psychological needs together. Consequence: gives a fuller picture of human motivation.

✅ **Practical application** — Employers can use it directly. Consequence: widely adopted in HR and management training.

✅ **More humanistic than behaviourism** — Treats people as complex, growth-seeking beings. Consequence: influenced positive psychology and employee wellbeing movements.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Strict hierarchy is questionable** — Maslow assumes you *must* satisfy lower needs before higher ones. But Mother Teresa lived in poverty and arguably achieved self-actualisation. Consequence: the theory's core mechanism may be invalid.

❌ **Cultural bias (ethnocentrism)** — Maslow defined self-actualisation through an **individualistic Western lens** (personal achievement, autonomy). In **collectivist cultures** (Japan, China), self-actualisation might mean serving the group. Consequence: the theory cannot be universally applied.

❌ **Hard to test scientifically** — "Self-actualisation" is vague and subjective. How do you measure it? Consequence: low **scientific validity** — you can't falsify the theory easily.

❌ **Snapshot not dynamic** — Life events (divorce, redundancy) can plunge someone from level 4 back to level 1 overnight. Maslow acknowledged this but didn't integrate it systematically. Consequence: the model can feel static.

---

**6. Exam Tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- 5 levels in order (bottom to top)
- Deficiency needs vs growth needs
- Application to the workplace (one example per level)
- Key evaluation points: cultural bias, Mother Teresa counterexample, holism

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe + evaluate, 16 marks):**
> Maslow (1943) proposed a five-level hierarchy of needs. The most basic are physiological needs [define], followed by safety [define], belongingness [define], esteem [define], and self-actualisation [define]. He argued that lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones become motivating. In the workplace, this means... [apply each level]. One strength is... [holism/application]. One weakness is... [cultural bias / invalid rigid hierarchy / not scientific].

## STUDY 2

### Saeednia (2011) — Generating a Scale to Measure Maslow's Hierarchy in Children

**1. Why This Study Exists**

Maslow's theory is elegant, but there was a problem: **no one had a reliable, validated tool to actually measure it** — especially not in children.

Saeednia's goal was to build one: the **Basic Needs Satisfaction Scale (BNSS)**.

Without a scale, Maslow's theory remains philosophical. With a scale, it becomes science.

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

Imagine you are a researcher in Tehran. You want to know: are children's basic needs being met? But you can't just ask children "do you feel self-actualised?" They'd stare at you blankly.

**Phase 1 — Qualitative interviews (exploratory)**

- Saeednia sat down with **13 children, their parents, and their teachers** in wealthy areas of Tehran.
- He used **open-ended questions** — no tick boxes, just conversation.
- Themes explored: the child's wishes, how much independence the family allowed, how supportive the family was.
- The data was **coded** (sorted into themes) using Maslow's five categories as a framework.

💡 **Analogy:** He was mining for themes like a geologist looking for gold seams — using Maslow's map to know where to dig.

**Phase 2 — Building the Scale**

- The themes from the interviews became **questionnaire items** in the BNSS.
- A **pilot study** refined the items.

**Phase 3 — Testing the Scale**

- The BNSS was distributed to **300 participants** randomly chosen from an initial pool of **457 people**.
- Results were analysed for **reliability** (Cronbach's alpha = 0.83, which is high).

---

**3. What They Found**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Scale reliability | Cronbach's alpha = **0.83** (high — above 0.7 is acceptable) |
| Children's tendency | Children tend to **"unconsciously pretend"** needs are met if their environment won't help them satisfy them |
| Cultural pattern | In **poorer nations**, life satisfaction linked to safety needs; in **wealthier nations**, to love and esteem |
| Method insight | **Qualitative methods** are more appropriate for capturing subtle needs in children |

> The BNSS is described as "an indicator of life satisfaction" that varies by culture — consistent with Maslow's framework.

---

**4. Why It Matters**

Before Saeednia, studying Maslow scientifically with children was nearly impossible.

This study:
- **Operationalised** Maslow's abstract concepts (made them measurable)
- Showed Maslow's hierarchy **holds cross-culturally** (at least partially)
- Gave future researchers a **validated tool** to use

It also raised an uncomfortable finding: children can *mask* unmet needs. This matters for teachers and child psychologists — a child who appears fine may be suppressing genuine deprivation.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Mixed methods** — Combined qualitative interviews AND quantitative questionnaires. Consequence: richer, more valid data; each method compensates for the other's weaknesses.

✅ **High reliability** — Alpha of 0.83 is strong. Consequence: the BNSS produces consistent results across different participants, making it trustworthy.

✅ **Foundation for future research** — First validated child-specific Maslow scale. Consequence: opened the door for empirical testing of the hierarchy.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Sampling bias (affluent area only)** — All participants came from **wealthy areas of Tehran**. Consequence: results cannot be generalised to poorer communities or other cultures — dramatically limits **external validity**.

❌ **Age of participants** — Children have limited ability to accurately report their own psychological states. Consequence: even with parent and teacher interviews, the **validity** of children's self-reports is questionable.

❌ **Social desirability / masking** — The study itself found children may pretend needs are met. Consequence: the very data the scale collects may be systematically distorted.

---

**6. Exam Tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Aim: develop and validate the BNSS for children
- Method: open-ended interviews (13 children + parents/teachers) → questionnaire → 300-participant pilot
- Key finding: reliability = 0.83; children mask unmet needs; cultural variation in what satisfies needs
- Main weakness: affluent Tehran sample = low generalisability
- Main strength: mixed methods → higher validity

📋 **Skeleton answer:**
> Saeednia (2011) aimed to develop a reliable scale [BNSS] to measure basic need satisfaction in children. Initially, 13 children plus parents and educators were interviewed using open-ended questions [qualitative]. These themes were used to construct the BNSS, which was tested on 300 participants [quantitative]. The study found reliability of 0.83, suggesting it is a consistent measure. One strength is the use of mixed methods... One weakness is the sample [affluent Tehran] limiting generalisability...

## STUDY 3

### McClelland (1961) — Theory of Achievement Motivation

**1. Why This Study Exists**

Maslow said *everyone* climbs the same five-rung ladder. McClelland disagreed.

He asked: **what if different people are driven by fundamentally different motivational profiles?**

His answer: at work, three distinct needs drive us — and one of them will always dominate.

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

McClelland's theory emerged from both **clinical observation** and use of the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** — a projective psychological tool created by Murray & Morgan in the 1930s.

**The TAT in practice:**

Imagine you are shown a black-and-white drawing: a person sitting alone at a desk, staring into space. A researcher says: *"Make up a story about this. What's happening? What led to this? What happens next?"*

You might say: *"She's planning how to beat her competitors and win the contract."* — High **achievement** need.
Or: *"She's lonely and wishing her colleagues would come talk to her."* — High **affiliation** need.
Or: *"She's deciding how to restructure the team so everyone reports directly to her."* — High **power** need.

💡 **The logic:** Because the picture is ambiguous, your story reveals *your* unconscious priorities. You project your own motivational world onto it.

McClelland devised a **standardised scoring system** to categorise responses across ~20 pictures per participant.

He proposed that the **dominant need** — whichever of the three scores highest — shapes behaviour at work.

---

**3. What They Found**

McClelland identified three universal work-related needs:

| Need | Key Behaviour | Best Job Fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Achievement (nAch)** | Seeks moderate challenge; wants feedback; avoids extremes (too easy = no thrill; too hard = luck, not skill) | Entrepreneurs, surgeons, individual contributors |
| **Affiliation (nAff)** | Wants to be liked; conformist; avoids conflict; uncomfortable with solo praise | Customer service, team roles, counselling |
| **Power (nPow)** | Wants control and influence; two types: personal vs institutional | Leaders (institutional power = best leaders) |

**Power distinction (important for exams):**

| Type | Focus | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal power** | Control *over* individuals | Less effective leader |
| **Institutional (social) power** | Coordinate *group* toward org goals | More effective leader |

> "The most effective managers have a need for institutional power rather than personal power." — Textbook

---

**4. Why It Matters**

McClelland's theory gave organisations a practical screening tool.

If you know a candidate's dominant need before hiring:
- High nAch → give them challenging solo projects with clear feedback loops
- High nAff → put them in team-facing, collaborative roles
- High nPow (institutional) → consider them for management

This is **person-job fit** in practice: right person, right role, less turnover, better productivity.

He also made an important claim: **these needs are *learned*, not innate.** Cultural upbringing and life experience shape which need dominates — which means, in theory, needs can be trained.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Practical application** — Enables targeted recruitment and role assignment. Consequence: improves productivity and staff retention.

✅ **Acknowledges individual differences** — Unlike Maslow, McClelland recognises that people differ fundamentally in what drives them. Consequence: more nuanced, realistic model of workplace motivation.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Gender and cultural bias** — Theory was constructed using **Western male workers**. Consequence: may not apply to women or to collectivist cultures (e.g., Japan) where the need for individual power may not exist in the same form — limits **generalisability**.

❌ **TAT has questionable validity** — Measuring unconscious motives from ambiguous story interpretations is inherently subjective. Even with McClelland's scoring system, you are measuring something *unobservable*. Consequence: results cannot be easily **falsified** — if the TAT says someone has high nAch and they don't succeed, you can always say the situation was wrong.

❌ **Ignores situational factors** — Having a high need for power is useless unless others are willing to be led. The theory focuses on the individual's needs but not the social context that must cooperate. Consequence: **incomplete** explanation of motivation.

---

**6. Exam Tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Three needs: Achievement, Affiliation, Power
- Characteristics of each (especially high vs low nAch; personal vs institutional power)
- TAT: projective test, ~20 ambiguous pictures, participant tells a story, responses coded
- Key evaluation: cultural/gender bias; TAT validity; ignores situational factors

📋 **Skeleton answer:**
> McClelland (1961) proposed that motivation at work is driven by three learned needs: achievement [define], affiliation [define], and power [define — include personal vs institutional]. He believed one need is dominant and shapes workplace behaviour. He measured this using the TAT [describe]. One strength is practical application [explain]. One weakness is cultural bias [explain — Western male sample].

## STUDY 4

### Locke et al. (1968) / Locke & Latham (1990) — Goal-Setting Theory

**1. Why This Study Exists**

Need theories say *what* people want. But they don't tell you *how* to channel that wanting into performance.

Locke's insight: **the goal itself is a motivator.** How you frame a goal determines how hard someone works.

His 1968 work identified that **specific, hard goals** outperform vague ones. Locke and Latham's 1990 refinement (built on 50+ years of research) codified *five characteristics* of effective goals.

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

Locke's original work was **laboratory-based** — he gave participants tasks with different goal types and measured performance:
- Group A: "Do your best."
- Group B: "Complete 12 units in 20 minutes."

Group B consistently outperformed Group A. Specific, measurable targets worked.

Locke and Latham (1990) then synthesised **decades of research** (secondary data — a meta-analytic approach) to build a comprehensive theory.

💡 **Analogy:** "Do your best on the motorway" is useless. "Drive at 70mph, arrive in Bristol by 3pm" is a goal.

---

**3. What They Found**

**SMART Goals** (Locke's formulation):

| Letter | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**pecific | Clear, not vague | "Increase slipper sales by 50%" not "sell more slippers" |
| **M**easurable | Has criteria to track | "50% increase" = measurable |
| **A**chievable | Realistic but not trivial | Stretching but possible |
| **R**elevant | Linked to broader goals | Matters to the employee/org |
| **T**ime-bound | Has a deadline | "In 28 days" |

**Locke & Latham's (1990) Five Characteristics of Effective Goals:**

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Clarity** | Unambiguous, specific, measurable |
| **Challenge** | Hard enough to motivate, not impossible |
| **Commitment** | Employee must believe in and accept the goal |
| **Feedback** | Regular updates keep workers on track |
| **Task complexity** | Realistic timescales; sub-goals for complex tasks |

> Key finding: **the best goals are ones you are not 100% sure you can achieve, but have a realistic chance with hard work.**

---

**4. Why It Matters**

Goal-setting theory is among the **most widely applied** in organisational psychology:
- Used in annual performance reviews
- Underpins KPI (Key Performance Indicator) frameworks in business
- Applied in sports coaching and educational psychology

It gives managers a concrete, actionable system: set clear goals → give regular feedback → watch motivation rise.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Strong evidence base** — Built on 50+ years of research across many studies. Consequence: high **reliability** — the theory has been replicated consistently.

✅ **Directly applicable** — Managers can implement SMART goals immediately. Consequence: ethically sound (improves both worker and org outcomes); genuinely useful.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Reductionist** — Reduces motivation to cognitive goal-setting alone. Ignores physiological needs (Maslow) and past experiences (learning approach). Consequence: **incomplete** — a worker whose safety needs are unmet won't be fixed by a SMART goal.

❌ **Cultural bias** — Based on **individualistic Western values** (personal achievement, individual targets). In **collectivist cultures** (e.g., Japan), group goals and collective harmony may be more motivating. Consequence: cannot be universally generalised.

❌ **Secondary data dependency (1990 paper)** — Locke & Latham synthesised existing studies. Consequence: any biases or flaws in those original studies are inherited — **garbage in, garbage out**.

---

**6. Exam Tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- SMART acronym + what each letter means
- Five characteristics of Locke & Latham (1990) — **Clarity, Challenge, Commitment, Feedback, Task complexity**
- 1968 finding: specific + hard goals > vague "do your best" goals
- Evaluation: evidence-based strength; reductionist + cultural bias weaknesses

📋 **Skeleton answer:**
> Locke et al. (1968) suggested that specific, measurable goals are more effective than vague ones. Locke and Latham (1990) developed this further, identifying five characteristics of effective goals: clarity [define], challenge [define], commitment [define], feedback [define], task complexity [define]. One strength is that the theory is supported by extensive research [expand]. One weakness is that it is reductionist [expand].

## STUDY 5

### Vroom (1964) — Expectancy (VIE) Theory of Motivation

**1. Why This Study Exists**

Vroom observed something that other theories missed: **two people can face the same reward and respond completely differently.**

Why? Because motivation is not just about the reward itself — it's about what the person *expects* and *values*.

His theory treats people as **rational calculators** who weigh up: "Is it worth it?"

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

Like Maslow and McClelland, Vroom's work was largely **theoretical** — a conceptual model rather than a single experiment.

He proposed a **multiplicative formula:**

```
Motivation = Valence × Instrumentality × Expectancy
```

💡 **Analogy:** Imagine you're offered a £1,000 bonus (V = high) for completing a project, but your manager has broken every promise before (I = low), and you've never done this type of project (E = low).

Even with a huge reward, if any one factor is near zero — **motivation collapses.**

---

**3. What They Found**

Vroom's three components:

| Component | Definition | Workplace Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Valence (V)** | How much you *value* the expected reward | A cash bonus may matter less to a wealthy employee than to one in debt |
| **Instrumentality (I)** | Belief that good performance *will actually* lead to the reward | "Will my manager actually give me the bonus if I hit the target?" |
| **Expectancy (E)** | Belief that effort *will* lead to good performance | "Do I have the skill/resources to do this job well?" |

> If **any** of the three = 0, motivation = 0. All three must be present.

**Redmond (2009)** found that all three components correlated highly with each other and with motivation predictability — supporting Vroom's model.

---

**4. Why It Matters**

Vroom's theory tells managers that motivation is not just about *giving* rewards — it's about:
1. **Making rewards desirable** (valence): tailor rewards to the individual
2. **Being trustworthy** (instrumentality): follow through on promises; make the process transparent
3. **Building capability** (expectancy): train workers, give resources, provide mentoring

It is one of the few motivation theories that explicitly accounts for **individual differences** — the same reward system works differently for different people.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Accounts for individual differences** — Unlike Maslow (assumes we're all the same), Vroom acknowledges that people value rewards differently. Consequence: more **realistic** and **valid** model of motivation.

✅ **Predictive power** — Redmond (2009) found strong correlational support for all three VIE components. Consequence: the theory can be used to **predict** employee choices and behaviours.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Assumes rational, conscious choice** — Vroom's model requires people to consciously calculate expected rewards. But humans are often irrational — swayed by emotion, habit, short-term bias. Consequence: the theory **overestimates** rational decision-making, reducing its **validity**.

❌ **Rewards not always linked to performance in real workplaces** — In many organisations, pay is determined by seniority, politics, or budget, not purely performance. Consequence: the instrumentality component is frequently violated in real life — **low ecological validity**.

❌ **Environmental determinism** — The theory suggests motivation is determined by external reward expectations, leaving little room for **intrinsic motivation** or **free will**. Consequence: ignores the person who works hard purely out of passion regardless of reward.

---

**6. Exam Tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- VIE = Valence, Instrumentality, Expectancy — know definitions of all three
- Formula: M = V × I × E — if any = 0, M = 0
- Individualistic (vs Maslow's universal hierarchy)
- Key weakness: assumes rational choice; real-world rewards not always performance-linked
- Redmond (2009) as supporting evidence

📋 **Skeleton answer:**
> Vroom (1964) proposed that motivation = Valence × Instrumentality × Expectancy. Valence refers to [define], Instrumentality refers to [define], Expectancy refers to [define]. If any factor is zero, motivation is zero. One strength is that it accounts for individual differences [expand — unlike Maslow]. One weakness is it assumes rational, conscious choice [expand].

## STUDY 6

### Deci & Ryan (1985) — Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

**1. Why This Study Exists**

By the 1980s, research was showing something alarming: **giving people rewards for things they already enjoyed doing could make them enjoy it less.**

Deci and Ryan wanted to understand *why* — and to build a theory that captured the full complexity of human motivation beyond just "carrots and sticks."

Their answer: **autonomy, competence, and relatedness** are the three psychological soil conditions that allow intrinsic motivation to grow.

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

SDT is a **theoretical framework** built from decades of experimental research (including Deci's own famous 1971 puzzle study, where rewarding people for playing with puzzles they loved caused them to play less once rewards stopped).

The theory distinguishes two types of motivation:

| Type | Source | Feel Like |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | Internal satisfaction, curiosity, growth | "I do this because I love it" |
| **Extrinsic** | External rewards/punishments | "I do this to get the bonus / avoid trouble" |

💡 **Analogy:** Intrinsic motivation is like eating because you're hungry and enjoy food. Extrinsic motivation is eating only when someone pays you. Once the money stops, you may not eat even if you're hungry — the natural drive has been replaced.

SDT proposes three basic psychological needs that must be met for intrinsic motivation to flourish:

| Need | Definition | Workplace application |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Self-governance; control over your own work | Allow flexible working, involve staff in decisions |
| **Competence** | Feeling capable and effective | Matched task difficulty, positive and constructive feedback |
| **Relatedness** | Belonging; caring relationships | Team culture, active listening, respect |

---

**3. What They Found**

**Deci & Ryan (1999)** reviewed **128 experiments** on extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation:
- **Task-contingent rewards** (you get paid *for completing* a task) → **decreased** intrinsic motivation
- **Positive feedback** → **increased** intrinsic motivation and autonomy

**Kohn (1994):** If organisations use rewards to *control* behaviour, more surveillance and competition follow → intrinsic motivation decreases further.

**Haggar et al. (2013):** Cultural differences matter:
- **Individualistic cultures**: intrinsic motivation higher when given *personal choice*
- **Collectivist cultures**: intrinsic motivation higher when task assigned by a *trusted manager*

---

**4. Why It Matters**

SDT changed how organisations think about rewards.

The message: **don't just ask "how big is the reward?" — ask "how is the reward being delivered?"**

A small cash bonus framed as "we trust and value you" can be more motivating than a large bonus framed as "hit the target or else."

Practical implications:
- Replace pure target-based bonuses with autonomy-supportive recognition
- Build team environments that satisfy relatedness
- Match task difficulty to skill level to maintain competence
- Use constructive (not punitive) feedback

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Research support** — Deci & Ryan (1999) meta-analysis of 128 studies is extremely robust. Consequence: high **reliability** — the pattern holds across many different contexts.

✅ **Applied directly to workplaces** — Managers can adjust *how* rewards are delivered to boost motivation. Consequence: actionable, ethically beneficial.

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Cultural bias (ethnocentrism)** — SDT's emphasis on individual autonomy reflects **Western individualistic** values. Haggar et al. (2013) showed autonomy works differently in collectivist cultures. Consequence: theory cannot be universally applied.

❌ **Deterministic framing** — SDT implies motivation is *determined* by the type of rewards received. Doesn't fully account for individuals who remain intrinsically motivated *regardless* of reward structure. Consequence: ignores individual personality differences.

---

**6. Exam Tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Three needs: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness (ACR)
- Intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation definitions
- Deci & Ryan (1999) — 128 experiments; task-contingent rewards decrease intrinsic motivation
- Kohn (1994) — controlling rewards → surveillance → less intrinsic motivation
- Haggar et al. (2013) — cultural differences in autonomy

📋 **Skeleton answer:**
> Deci and Ryan (1985) proposed self-determination theory, which states that intrinsic motivation depends on three basic needs: autonomy [define], competence [define], and relatedness [define]. Intrinsic motivation comes from internal satisfaction, while extrinsic motivation comes from external rewards. Deci & Ryan (1999) reviewed 128 experiments and found that task-contingent rewards decreased intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback increased it. One strength is strong research support [expand]. One weakness is cultural bias [expand — Haggar et al. 2013].

## STUDY 7 — KEY STUDY

### Landry et al. (2019) — Applying Self-Determination Theory to Motivational Rewards (Study 1)

**1. Why This Study Exists**

Everyone knows money motivates. Or does it?

SDT theory had suggested the *way* a reward is given matters as much as the reward itself. But most evidence for this came from **theory**, not controlled experiments.

Landry et al. (2019) designed a **laboratory experiment** to test this directly: can a $10 coffee shop gift card motivate people more or less depending on *how it is presented*?

> "Monetary rewards may only lead to a temporary improvement of performance as employees focus on the external rewards rather than internal enjoyment." — Textbook

---

**2. The Procedure — Told as a Story**

Picture yourself as a university student in Canada. You've signed up for a psychology study through your Organisational Behaviour course.

**Step 1: Read the instructions.**

You are shown a short paragraph describing a task and a reward. But there are two versions of this paragraph:

- **Version A (Autonomy-supportive):** Warm and encouraging. The $10 coffee gift card is described as *"a token of appreciation for your contribution."* It feels like a thank-you.

- **Version B (Controlling):** Pressurising. The gift card is described as there *"to reinforce the performance standards of the task."* It feels like surveillance.

You've been randomly assigned to one. You don't know there's another version.

**Step 2: Do the task.**

You have **2 minutes** to correctly solve **25 four-letter anagrams** (e.g., RABT → BRAT). A standardised performance score is calculated.

**Step 3: Fill in questionnaires.**

You complete three scales on a 7-point Likert scale:
1. **Basic Psychological Needs Satisfaction Scale** — Did you feel autonomous, competent, connected?
2. **Psychological Needs Thwarting Scale** — Did you feel frustrated, controlled?
3. **Situational Motivation Scale** — "Why are you doing this activity?" — intrinsic or extrinsic reasons?

**Control measures:** You also rate how valuable the reward was to you personally, and your emotional state — so researchers can rule out alternative explanations.

**The sample:**
- **123 student volunteers** (mean age = 23)
- Recruited from one 'Intro to Organisational Behaviour' course at a Canadian university
- Randomly assigned: 65 to autonomy-supportive; 58 to controlling condition
- 60% female

---

**3. What They Found**

▼ Table: Results by Condition

| Measure | Autonomy-Supportive (n=65) | Controlling (n=58) |
|---|---|---|
| Needs satisfaction | **5.79** | 5.39 |
| Needs frustration | **2.41** | 2.83 |
| Intrinsic motivation | **5.31** | 4.56 |
| Extrinsic motivation | 3.10 | **3.95** |
| **Performance** | **0.76** | **0.54** |

**Reading the table:**
- Higher needs satisfaction in autonomy condition → higher intrinsic motivation → **better performance (0.76 vs 0.54)**
- Controlling condition → more needs frustration → more extrinsic motivation → **but extrinsic motivation did NOT predict performance**

> Key finding: **Extrinsic motivation did not predict performance.** Only needs satisfaction → intrinsic motivation → performance chain worked.

**Mediation analysis:** Psychological needs satisfaction *mediated* the relationship between condition and both intrinsic motivation and performance. This means: the condition affected performance *through* needs satisfaction — not directly.

**Conclusions:**
- Presenting rewards in an **autonomy-supportive** (informational) way leads to greater needs satisfaction → higher intrinsic motivation → better performance.
- Presenting rewards in a **controlling** way frustrates needs → increases extrinsic motivation → no performance improvement.

---

**4. Why It Matters**

This study has a direct, clear message for every manager and HR department:

**It's not the $10. It's the sentence you say when you give it.**

A gift card that says "well done, we appreciate you" outperforms the same gift card that says "you earned this because you hit your target."

This is practically significant and counterintuitive. It means that expensive reward *systems* built around control and performance standards may be actively undermining motivation. A culture of appreciation and support is a better investment.

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Control measures used** — Participants rated the personal value of the reward and their emotional state. Consequence: individual differences in reward perception were controlled for, increasing **internal validity** — we can be more confident the condition caused the result.

✅ **Quantitative data on multiple measures** — Likert scales + standardised performance score. Consequence: objective data allows statistical testing and comparisons, increasing **reliability**.

✅ **Random allocation** — Participants randomly assigned to conditions. Consequence: reduced **selection bias** — groups were comparable at the start.

✅ **Ethical** — No deception; full informed consent; no distress. Consequence: ethically clean, and the findings may benefit both workers (greater wellbeing) and organisations (better performance).

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Sampling bias (CRITICAL)** — All participants were from *one course at one Canadian university* (n=123). Consequence: **low generalisability** — cannot apply findings to other cultures, age groups, or real workplaces.

❌ **Low ecological validity** — Solving 25 anagrams in 2 minutes in a lab is *nothing like* real workplace tasks. Consequence: we cannot be sure these findings translate to genuine job performance.

❌ **Demand characteristics** — Participants knew they were in a study. Reading an "autonomy-supportive" paragraph may have made them behave more motivated simply because it *felt* more positive. Consequence: results may reflect response to social cues, not genuine motivational change.

❌ **Reductionist** — The study treats performance as determined by self-determination variables alone, ignoring personality, prior competence, experience, and age. Consequence: **incomplete** picture of workplace performance.

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe 6 marks):**
> Landry et al. (2019) aimed to test whether monetary rewards could improve motivation and performance when presented in an autonomy-supportive versus controlling way. 123 student volunteers were randomly assigned to one of two conditions. In the autonomy-supportive condition, participants read an encouraging paragraph describing the reward as a "token of appreciation." In the controlling condition, the paragraph emphasised performance standards. All participants then completed 25 four-letter anagrams in 2 minutes. Self-report questionnaires measured needs satisfaction, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Results showed that the autonomy-supportive group scored higher on performance (0.76 vs 0.54) and intrinsic motivation, while extrinsic motivation did not predict performance.
