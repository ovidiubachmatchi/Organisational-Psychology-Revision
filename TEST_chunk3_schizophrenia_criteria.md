# 5.2.7 Validity

## 🎯 Why this matters

If a study **isn't actually measuring what it claims to measure**, the entire conclusion is meaningless.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Validity** = are we measuring what we think we are?
> * **Ecological validity** = behaviour reflects real life
> * **Subjectivity** reduces validity
> * **Demand characteristics** distort behaviour
> * **Generalisability** and **temporal validity** determine how widely results apply

---

## What is Validity?

🔑 **Validity** = the extent to which a study **measures what it claims to measure** and produces **meaningful findings**.

⚡ If a study lacks validity → conclusions are unreliable for real-world understanding.

---

### Example: Invalid IQ Testing

🔑 **Yerkes (1917)** created **Army Alpha and Beta IQ tests** for US army recruits.

Problem:

* Many recruits were **immigrants unfamiliar with US culture**
* Questions included:

  * “What is **Crisco**?”
  * “Who is **Christy Mathewson**?”

⚠️ These questions measured **cultural knowledge**, not intelligence.

💡 Memory hook
Imagine being tested on **Romanian celebrity gossip to measure intelligence**.
If you fail, are you less intelligent — or just unfamiliar?

🧠 Quick Check
What was the **validity problem** with the Army IQ tests?

---

## Ecological Validity

🎯 Psychology should explain **real human behaviour**, not behaviour that only exists in a lab.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Ecological validity** = how well behaviour in research reflects behaviour in real life
> * **Field experiments** often higher in ecological validity
> * **Laboratory experiments** can still be ecologically valid
> * If ecological validity is low → findings may not generalise

---

### Definition

🔑 **Ecological validity** = the extent to which participants’ behaviour in research **reflects real-life behaviour**.

---

### Example

Testing driving skills in a lab:

| Method                               | Ecological Validity |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------- |
| Using a keyboard                     | Low                 |
| VR headset + steering wheel + pedals | Higher              |

💡 Memory hook
If your **driving test uses arrow keys**, that’s not real driving.

## Subjectivity vs Objectivity

🎯 If a researcher’s **opinions influence results**, the study becomes less valid.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Subjectivity** = researcher interpretation influences results
> * **Objectivity** = results independent of researcher opinion
> * **Qualitative data** often more subjective
> * **Quantitative data** usually more objective
> * Scientific equipment increases objectivity

---

### Subjectivity

🔑 **Subjectivity** occurs when results are influenced by the **researcher’s personal views or interpretations**.

Example situations:

* Misinterpreting behaviour during **observations**
* Analysing **qualitative interviews**

Example:

* **Milgram (1963)** interpreted participant behaviour while delivering shocks.
* His expectations from pilot studies may have influenced interpretation.

💡 Memory hook
Subjectivity = **researcher wearing “opinion glasses.”**

---

### Objectivity

🔑 **Objectivity** = data not influenced by researcher opinion.

Examples:

| Objective Method    | Example Study     |
| ------------------- | ----------------- |
| **EEG** brain scans | Dement & Kleitman |
| **MRI** scans       | Hölzel et al.     |

⚠️ Even brain scans still require **human interpretation**.

---

### Case Study Example: Little Hans

Freud analysed **Hans’ dreams and fears** using interpretation.

Problem:

* Hans’ father interpreted dreams
* Freud interpreted father’s reports

Result → **high subjectivity → low validity**

---

### Quantitative Data Can Still Be Biased

Example:

* **Burger (2009)** replication of **Milgram**
* Highlighted that participants **did not continue after the 4th prod**
* Milgram did **not emphasise this disobedience result**

⚠️ Even numerical data can be **selectively reported**.

---

🧠 Quick Check
Why is **qualitative data more vulnerable to subjectivity**?

---

## Demand Characteristics

🎯 Participants often behave differently if they **figure out the aim of the study**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Demand characteristics** = cues revealing study aim
> * Participants change behaviour accordingly
> * Reduces validity
> * Deception can reduce demand characteristics
> * Animals rarely show demand characteristics

---

### Definition

🔑 **Demand characteristics** = features of a research setting that **give away the study’s aim**.

Participants may:

* Act how they think researchers expect
* Try to **confirm the hypothesis**

---

### Example: Milgram

Participants were deceived to hide the aim.

However critics suggested:

* The experimenter showed **no concern for the learner**
* Participants might have suspected **shocks were fake**

(Orne & Holland, 1968)

---

### Animals and Demand Characteristics

Strength of animal studies:

* Animals **do not consciously guess the aim**

However:

⚠️ Animals may still respond to **subtle cues from researchers**.

---

🧠 Quick Check
Why might **animal research reduce demand characteristics**?

---

## Generalisability

🎯 A study is useless if findings **only apply to the sample studied**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Generalisability** = applying findings to wider population
> * Depends on **representative sampling**
> * Random samples increase generalisability
> * Cultural differences may limit generalisation
> * Also called **population validity**

---

### Example: Piliavin Subway Study

Studied helping behaviour in **New York subway passengers**.

Issue:

* Helping behaviour may differ across **cultures**

⚠️ Results may not generalise globally.

---

🧠 Quick Check
Why might **cultural differences affect generalisability**?

---

## Temporal Validity

🎯 Human behaviour changes over time.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Temporal validity** = whether results apply to different time periods
> * Cultural changes can reduce validity
> * Social psychology especially vulnerable
> * Biological studies often less affected

---

### Example

**Bandura (1960s)** aggression study.

Issues today:

* Children now exposed to **digital media**
* **Gender roles** have changed

Result → findings may be **era-bound**.

---

### Contrast Example

**Hassett monkey toy study**

* Based on **biological sex differences**
* Less affected by cultural change

---

# 5.2.8 Reliability and Replicability

---

## 🎯 Why this matters

If a study cannot be **repeated with the same results**, we cannot trust it.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Reliability** = consistency of results
> * **Replicability** = ability to repeat a study
> * **Standardisation** increases reliability
> * Reliability can be tested using specific techniques

---

## Reliability

🔑 **Reliability** = the extent to which findings are **consistent**.

Two ways to check:

* Same participants tested again
* Different participants in a replication

⚡ Same results = reliable findings.

---

## Replicability

🔑 **Replicability** = the ability for other researchers to **repeat the study exactly**.

This allows researchers to check whether results occurred **by chance**.

---

## Standardisation

🎯 Standardised procedures make studies **replicable and reliable**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Same instructions for all participants
> * Same procedure
> * Same scoring methods
> * Only the **independent variable** changes

---

### Example: Bandura Bobo Doll Study

Highly standardised:

| Element           | Standardisation         |
| ----------------- | ----------------------- |
| Model behaviour   | Same sequence each time |
| Toys              | Arranged identically    |
| Instructions      | Same wording            |
| Behaviour scoring | Standard checklist      |

Result → **easy replication → reliable findings**

---

🧠 Quick Check
Why does **standardisation increase reliability**?

---

## Techniques to Increase Reliability

| Technique                      | Description                                                            |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Test-retest reliability**    | Participants repeat test later; consistent scores indicate reliability |
| **Inter-rater reliability**    | Two researchers score tests; results compared                          |
| **Inter-observer reliability** | Two observers independently record behaviour                           |

---

### Inter-rater Reliability

Scores from two raters should show a **positive correlation**.

Closer to **1.0 = stronger agreement**.

---

### Inter-observer Reliability

Two observers record behaviour in observations.

Higher agreement → greater reliability.

---

🧠 Quick Check
Which reliability technique involves **two observers coding behaviour**?

---

## ⚠️ Confusing Pair

| Concept           | Meaning                         |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **Reliability**   | Consistency of results          |
| **Replicability** | Ability to repeat the procedure |

---

# 5.2.9 Data Analysis

---

## 🎯 Why this matters

Raw data is messy — psychologists use statistics to **identify patterns and conclusions**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Measures of central tendency** describe averages
> * **Measures of spread** show variation
> * Graphs visualise results
> * Different graphs suit different data types

---

# Measures of Central Tendency

🔑 **Measures of central tendency** summarise data using a **single value**.

---

### Types

| Measure    | Calculation                      | Example                |
| ---------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| **Mean**   | Sum of scores ÷ number of scores | Andrade memory recall  |
| **Median** | Middle value in ordered list     | Piliavin helping time  |
| **Mode**   | Most frequent score              | Hassett toy preference |

---

💡 Memory hook
Think of a **school hallway**:

* **Mean** → average height
* **Median** → middle student
* **Mode** → most common height

---

🧠 Quick Check

---

# Measures of Spread

🎯 Two groups may have the **same average but very different variability**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Range** = highest − lowest score
> * **Standard deviation** = spread around the mean
> * Larger spread → greater variation

---

| Measure                | Meaning                                     | Calculation             |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| **Range**              | Difference between highest and lowest score | Highest − lowest        |
| **Standard deviation** | Average distance from the mean              | Statistical calculation |

---

### Example Studies

| Study                    | Statistic Used         |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------- |
| Hölzel mindfulness study | Range of practice time |
| Baron-Cohen Eyes Test    | Standard deviation     |

---

💡 Memory hook
Imagine **students around a teacher**:

* Tight circle → **small standard deviation**
* Spread around room → **large standard deviation**

---

🧠 Quick Check

# Graphs in Psychology

🎯 Graphs make patterns **instantly visible**.

---

## Bar Charts

Used for **categorical data**.

Features:

* Bars **do not touch**
* Compare groups or conditions

Example:

* Perry personal space study

---

## Histograms

Used for **continuous data**.

Features:

* Bars **touch**
* Shows **frequency distribution**

Example:

* Reaction times

---

## Scatter Graphs

Used for **correlational data**.

Features:

* Shows relationship between **two co-variables**
* Each point = one participant

Example:

* Baron-Cohen study: **foetal testosterone vs social sensitivity**

---

## ⚠️ Confusing Pair

| Feature   | Bar Chart        | Histogram    |
| --------- | ---------------- | ------------ |
| Data type | Categorical      | Continuous   |
| Bars      | Do not touch     | Touch        |
| Shows     | Group comparison | Distribution |

# Key Research Methods Summary

---

## Experimental Methods

| Feature     | Laboratory Experiment     | Field Experiment           |
| ----------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Setting     | Controlled environment    | Real-world setting         |
| Reliability | High                      | Lower                      |
| Validity    | Lower ecological validity | Higher ecological validity |
| Ethics      | Easier to manage          | Harder to control          |

---

## Experimental Designs

| Design                   | Strength                       | Weakness              | Solution          |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------ | --------------------- | ----------------- |
| **Independent measures** | No order effects               | Participant variables | Random allocation |
| **Repeated measures**    | No participant variables       | Order effects         | Counterbalancing  |
| **Matched pairs**        | Controls participant variables | Time-consuming        | Large sample      |

---

## Non-Experimental Methods

| Method                   | Strength               | Weakness            |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------- |
| **Questionnaires**       | Quick large data       | Limited detail      |
| **Interviews**           | Rich qualitative data  | Social desirability |
| **Case studies**         | Deep insight           | Not generalisable   |
| **Observations**         | Natural behaviour      | Subjectivity        |
| **Correlations**         | Study unethical topics | No causation        |
| **Longitudinal studies** | Track development      | Participant dropout |

---

# Core Methodological Concepts

---

### Variables

| Term                          | Meaning                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **Independent variable (IV)** | Variable manipulated                        |
| **Dependent variable (DV)**   | Variable measured                           |
| **Operationalisation**        | Clearly defining how variables are measured |

---

### Control Variables

| Type                      | Meaning                         |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **Participant variables** | Differences between individuals |
| **Situational variables** | Environmental factors           |

---

### Sampling Methods

| Method          | Advantage              | Disadvantage         |
| --------------- | ---------------------- | -------------------- |
| **Opportunity** | Quick                  | Similar participants |
| **Volunteer**   | Motivated participants | Self-selection bias  |
| **Random**      | Representative         | Difficult to obtain  |

---

### Types of Data

| Type             | Description               |
| ---------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Qualitative**  | Opinions and descriptions |
| **Quantitative** | Numerical data            |

---

### Ethics

Researchers must follow:

* **Informed consent**
* **Right to withdraw**
* **Confidentiality**
* **Minimising harm**
* **Avoiding deception**

Animal research must follow:

* **Replacement**
* **Species knowledge**
* **Numbers**
* **Procedures** (housing, reward, deprivation, aversive stimuli)

# Cultural Differences

🎯 **Psychology often studies WEIRD populations (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic).**
But do the findings apply **everywhere?**

---

> **TL;DR**
>
> * **Culture** = shared values, beliefs, behaviours
> * **Cultural identity** forms through **enculturation**
> * Many people are **bi-cultural**
> * **Hofstede (1980)** created **6 cultural dimensions**
> * Ignoring culture leads to **ethnocentrism**

---

## Culture

🔑 **Culture**
Shared **attitudes, beliefs, values and norms** within a group.

Definition (Hofstede, 1980):

> “The **collective programming of the mind** distinguishing groups.”

Examples of cultural influence:

* behaviour
* social norms
* beliefs
* emotional expression

---

## Cultural Identity

🔑 **Cultural identity**

The **culture or social group a person feels they belong to**.

Influenced by:

* family
* education
* religion
* media
* migration

---

### Enculturation

🔑 **Enculturation**

The **process of learning culture**.

Stages:

1️⃣ **Family interactions**
2️⃣ **School & education**
3️⃣ **Religion & media**

Over time identity can **change**.

---

### Bi-cultural identity

🔑 **Bi-cultural**

Identifying with **two cultures**.

Common causes:

* parents from different cultures
* migration
* minority groups living in dominant culture

💡 **Memory Hook**

Imagine someone speaking **two languages, celebrating two holidays, and switching behaviour depending on context**.

That’s **bi-cultural identity**.

---

# Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions (1980)

🎯 **Cultures differ in predictable ways.**
Hofstede created dimensions to compare them.

---

> **TL;DR**
>
> * **Individualism vs collectivism**
> * **Power distance**
> * **Uncertainty avoidance**
> * **Long-term orientation**
> * **Indulgence vs restraint**
> * **Masculinity vs femininity**

---

## 1️⃣ Individualism vs Collectivism

| Individualist  | Collectivist  |
| -------------- | ------------- |
| Personal goals | Group goals   |
| Independence   | Cooperation   |
| Competition    | Loyalty       |
| Autonomy       | Group harmony |

Examples:

* **Individualist cultures** → USA, UK
* **Collectivist cultures** → Japan, China

💡 **Memory Hook**

Picture:

* 🧍 **Individualist** → person standing alone saying “my goals first”
* 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 **Collectivist** → family pulling together like a rowing team

---

## 2️⃣ Power Distance

🔑 **Power distance**

How much people **accept unequal power**.

| High Power Distance | Low Power Distance   |
| ------------------- | -------------------- |
| Hierarchy accepted  | Equality valued      |
| Authority respected | Authority questioned |

Examples:

* **Malaysia, UAE** → high power distance

---

## 3️⃣ Uncertainty Avoidance

🔑 **Uncertainty avoidance**

How comfortable a culture is with **uncertainty or ambiguity**.

High:

* strict rules
* dislike uncertainty

Low:

* flexible
* tolerant of ambiguity

---

## 4️⃣ Long-Term Orientation

🔑 **Long-term orientation**

Focus on **future rewards**.

Examples:

* saving money
* investing in education

Short-term cultures focus more on **tradition and immediate outcomes**.

---

## 5️⃣ Indulgence vs Restraint

| Indulgence    | Restraint       |
| ------------- | --------------- |
| Enjoy life    | Control desires |
| Gratification | Self-discipline |

---

## 6️⃣ Masculinity vs Femininity

| Masculine cultures | Feminine cultures |
| ------------------ | ----------------- |
| Competition        | Care for others   |
| Achievement        | Quality of life   |
| Success            | Cooperation       |

---

## Ethnocentrism

🔑 **Ethnocentrism**

When psychologists **assume findings apply to all cultures**.

Example:

* therapy developed in **Western individualist cultures**
* may not work in **collectivist cultures**

---

### Example – Therapy

Many therapies are **person-centred**:

* therapist and patient are **equal partners**
* patient explores **their own solutions**

But in **high power distance cultures**:

* doctors are **authority figures**
* patients expect **direct advice**

⚠️ **Potential problem**

Therapy style may **not fit cultural expectations**.

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                              | ❌ Weakness                          |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Encourages **cross-cultural awareness** | Hard to test experimentally         |
| Improves **global validity**            | Cultures themselves vary internally |

---

🔗 **Connection**

Clinical Psychology treatments may show **cultural bias**.

---

📝 **Exam Tip**

In evaluation questions say:

> “The theory may be **ethnocentric**, as research was conducted in **Western individualist cultures**.”

# Reductionism vs Holism

🎯 **Human behaviour is extremely complex.**
Should psychology **simplify it** or **study the full picture**?

---

> **TL;DR**
>
> * **Reductionism** → break behaviour into parts
> * **Holism** → study behaviour as a whole
> * Experiments often **reductionist**
> * Holism uses **multiple methods**

---

## Reductionism

🔑 **Reductionism**

Breaking complex behaviour into **simpler components**.

Example:

* studying **genes**
* studying **neurotransmitters**
* studying **specific stimuli**

---

### Example in Psychology

**Wundt**

Tried to break **consciousness** into:

* **thoughts**
* **sensations**

---

### Experiments = Reductionist

Experiments isolate:

| Variable | Purpose     |
| -------- | ----------- |
| **IV**   | manipulated |
| **DV**   | measured    |

Goal:

Identify **cause and effect relationships**.

💡 **Memory Hook**

Reductionism = **LEGO psychology**

Break behaviour into **tiny bricks**.

---

## Holism

🔑 **Holism**

Behaviour should be studied **as a complete system**.

Principle:

> **“The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.”**

---

### Holistic psychology considers

Multiple influences:

* **biological**
* **cognitive**
* **learning**
* **social**

---

### Methods often used

Holistic psychologists may use:

* **interviews**
* **participant observation**
* **case studies**

Goal:

Understand **full life context**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> Why might pain research require a holistic approach?

Answer idea:

Pain depends on **biology, emotions, cognition and context**.

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                                 | ❌ Weakness                       |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Reductionism allows **scientific testing** | May oversimplify behaviour       |
| Holism captures **real complexity**        | Harder to measure scientifically |

---

🔗 **Connection**

Pain research in **Health Psychology** requires holistic understanding.

---

# Determinism vs Free Will

🎯 **Are humans in control of their behaviour — or controlled by causes?**

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Hard determinism** → behaviour fully caused
> * **Biological determinism** → genes & hormones
> * **Environmental determinism** → learning history
> * **Free will** → humans make choices
> * **Soft determinism** → both influence behaviour

---

## Hard Determinism

🔑 **Hard determinism**

Every behaviour has a **cause**.

Nothing happens by chance.

---

### Biological determinism

Behaviour caused by:

* **genes**
* **neurotransmitters**
* **hormones**

---

### Environmental determinism

Behaviour caused by:

* **rewards**
* **punishments**
* **learning history**

Example:

Behaviourism.

---

💡 **Memory Hook**

Determinism = **domino chain**

Every action is **knocked over by something before it**.

---

## Free Will

🔑 **Free will**

Humans can **choose actions consciously**.

Implications:

* moral responsibility
* criminal justice systems

Survey finding:

* **70% of 40,000 people across 34 countries**
* believe **“we make our own fate.”**

---

## Soft Determinism

🔑 **Soft determinism**

Behaviour influenced by:

* genes
* environment
* situation

BUT people still feel they **choose actions**.

---

### Example – Consumer Psychology

Shopping behaviour influenced by:

* colours
* smells
* music
* store layout

These increase **probability of purchases**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> Which concept suggests behaviour is **influenced but not fully controlled**?

Answer: **Soft determinism**

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                                     | ❌ Weakness                  |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Determinism supports **scientific prediction** | Ignores human choice        |
| Free will explains **responsibility**          | Hard to test scientifically |

---

🔗 **Connection**

Consumer psychology shows **situational determinism**.

# Idiographic vs Nomothetic

🎯 **Should psychology study unique individuals — or general laws of behaviour?**

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Idiographic** → study individuals
> * Uses **qualitative methods**
> * Often **case studies**
> * **Nomothetic** → study groups
> * Uses **quantitative data & averages**

---

## Idiographic Approach

🔑 **Idiographic**

Focus on **unique individual experiences**.

Methods:

* case studies
* interviews
* qualitative data

---

### Key features

* small samples
* subjective experiences
* deep understanding

Goal:

Understand **personal meaning**.

💡 **Memory Hook**

Idiographic = **psychological biography**.

---

## Nomothetic Approach

🔑 **Nomothetic**

Goal:

Create **universal laws of behaviour**.

---

### Methods

| Feature           | Description       |
| ----------------- | ----------------- |
| Large samples     | many participants |
| Quantitative data | numbers           |
| Averages          | typical behaviour |

---

Example:

Comparing **groups or conditions**.

Result:

Find behaviour typical of **population**.

---

⚠️ Important

Nomothetic results describe the **average person**.

Not necessarily a **real individual**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> Which approach is more likely to use **case studies**?

Answer: **Idiographic**

🧬 Genetic Explanation
🎯 Why this matters

If schizophrenia runs in families, understanding the genes involved could help explain why some people are more vulnerable than others.

TLDR

Evidence from Twin Studies
Key concept	Explanation
Heritability	Degree to which a disorder is influenced by genetic inheritance
Concordance rate	Probability that both twins have the disorder
Monozygotic (MZ)	Identical twins → 100% shared DNA
Dizygotic (DZ)	Non-identical twins → ≈50% shared DNA

Classic Study

Study	Finding
Gottesman & Shields (1966)	42% concordance (MZ) vs 9% (DZ)

🔑 Key idea:
Higher concordance in MZ twins → evidence schizophrenia has a genetic component.

Researchers compare entire genomes of people with vs without schizophrenia.

Term	Meaning
Polymorphism	Gene exists in different forms
Allele	Specific version of a gene
Polygenic	Disorder caused by many genes

Findings:

Thousands of gene variants linked to schizophrenia

Many genes affect neurotransmitters such as dopamine

⚡ Exam fact:
Schizophrenia is considered a polygenic disorder.

---

## TLDR

> * Developed in the **1950s**
> * Work as **dopamine antagonists**
> * Block **dopamine receptors** on postsynaptic neurons
> * Reduce **positive symptoms** (e.g., hallucinations, delusions)

---

### Mechanism

| Step | What happens                      |
| ---- | --------------------------------- |
| 1    | Drug blocks **dopamine receptor** |
| 2    | Dopamine cannot bind              |
| 3    | Synaptic signalling decreases     |
| 4    | Positive symptoms reduce          |

These drugs are called **dopamine antagonists**.

---

### Examples

| Drug               | Action                      |
| ------------------ | --------------------------- |
| **chlorpromazine** | first typical antipsychotic |
| **haloperidol**    | blocks dopamine receptors   |

---

💡 **Memory Hook**

Picture a **bouncer (the drug)** standing in front of a **dopamine nightclub door** → dopamine cannot get in.

---

> What neurotransmitter do **typical antipsychotics target**?

# Atypical Antipsychotics (Second Generation)

## 🎯 Hook

Atypical drugs **block two neurotransmitters instead of one**.

---

## TLDR

> * Developed **1980s onwards**
> * Block **dopamine AND serotonin receptors**
> * Often fewer side effects
> * Used widely today

### Mechanism

| Neurotransmitter | Role                                                  |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **dopamine**     | linked to positive symptoms                           |
| **serotonin**    | inhibitory neurotransmitter affecting mood, alertness |

Blocking both helps regulate:

* mood
* arousal
* neural firing

---

### Examples

| Drug            | Notes                                |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **clozapine**   | effective but dangerous side effects |
| **risperidone** | commonly prescribed                  |

---

💡 **Memory Hook**

Imagine **two switches controlling brain activity**:

* Dopamine switch
* Serotonin switch

Atypical drugs **turn both down**.

## 🎯 Hook

## TLDR

> * Start with **atypical antipsychotic**
> * If ineffective → try **another atypical or typical**
> * If still ineffective → **clozapine**
> * Final step → **combine drugs or use ECT**

| 3    | **Clozapine trial**                                                 |
| 4    | Combine with other drugs (e.g., **lithium mood stabiliser**)        |

# Evaluating Biochemical Treatments

---

## Study: Zhao et al. (2016)

🔑 **One-liner**

A meta-analysis showed **most antipsychotics reduce relapse rates compared to placebo**.

---

### Study Overview

| Feature           | Details                                                 |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Aim**           | Assess effectiveness of antipsychotics                  |
| **Sample**        | Data from **10,000+ patients**                          |
| **Method**        | **Meta-analysis of 56 RCTs**                            |
| **Drugs studied** | **18 antipsychotics**                                   |
| **Results**       | **17 drugs reduced relapse vs placebo**                 |
| **Conclusion**    | Antipsychotics effective alternative to hospitalisation |

However:

* **4 drugs showed no improvement vs placebo**.

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                                    | ❌ Weakness                                |
| --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Very **large sample**                         | Some drugs ineffective                    |
| Used **randomised control trials**            | Variability in relapse measurement        |
| **Nomothetic approach** allows generalisation | May ignore individual patient differences |

---

🔗 **Issues & Debates**

Nomothetic vs idiographic:

* **Nomothetic:** large samples, statistical generalisations
* **Idiographic:** individual experiences (e.g., therapy alliance)

---

📝 **Exam Tip**

> When evaluating treatments, always mention:
>
> * **RCT evidence**
> * **side effects**
> * **relapse rates**

# Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

## 🎯 Hook

ECT deliberately causes a **controlled seizure in the brain to reset neural activity**.

---

## TLDR

> * Electrical pulses **70–150 volts** applied to brain
> * Causes **brief seizure (~1 minute)**
> * Sessions **2–3 times per week** initially
> * Used for **treatment-resistant cases**

---

### Procedure

| Step | Description                 |
| ---- | --------------------------- |
| 1    | Electrodes placed on scalp  |
| 2    | Electrical pulse delivered  |
| 3    | Seizure triggered           |
| 4    | Neurons fire simultaneously |

Treatment schedule:

* **2–3 sessions weekly** for first month
* **maintenance sessions** monthly or fortnightly for up to **1 year**

---

### Modified ECT

Modern ECT uses:

* **general anaesthesia**
* **muscle relaxants**

This prevents:

* injury
* memory of procedure

---

💡 **Memory Hook**

Think of ECT like **rebooting a frozen computer** — a shock resets neural circuits.

---

# Evaluating ECT

---

## Study: Petrides et al. (2015)

🔑 **One-liner**

Combining **ECT with clozapine reduced symptoms significantly in treatment-resistant patients**.

---

### Study Overview

| Feature    | Details                                          |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Aim        | Test **ECT + clozapine** effectiveness           |
| Sample     | **39 patients**                                  |
| Method     | **Single-blind study**                           |
| Results    | **50% showed ≥40% symptom reduction in 8 weeks** |
| Conclusion | ECT may help treatment-resistant patients        |

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                         | ❌ Weakness                                       |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Rapid improvement for severe cases | Small sample                                     |
| Could prevent suicide risk         | Participants knew treatment → expectancy effects |
| Useful when drugs fail             | Possible **placebo effect**                      |

---

## Placebo Evidence

**Melzer-Ribeiro et al. (2017)**

| Group    | Sample |
| -------- | ------ |
| Fake ECT | n = 10 |
| Real ECT | n = 13 |

Result:

* **no significant difference**

⚠️ Suggests improvement may be due to **expectancy effects**.

## Cultural Differences

Study: **Wang et al. (2018a)**

| Feature  | Details                      |
| -------- | ---------------------------- |
| Method   | Meta-analysis of **18 RCTs** |
| Location | **17 studies in China**      |
| Result   | ECT + clozapine effective    |

However:

* China = **high power-distance culture**
* Patients may trust doctors more → stronger placebo effects.

---

🔗 **Issues & Debates**

**Cultural bias**

* Results from China may **not generalise to Western cultures**.

# ## 6.2.1 Diagnostic Criteria (ICD-11)

## 🎯 Why this matters

Understanding **how disorders are diagnosed** helps explain **treatment, research findings, and exam evaluation questions**.

> **TLDR**

> * Mood disorders involve long-lasting **extreme moods**.
> * Diagnosis is based on **episodes** (depressive, manic, hypomanic).
> * **Unipolar depression** = only low mood episodes.
> * **Bipolar disorder** = both **high (mania)** and **low (depression)**.
> * Diagnosis depends on **duration + intensity** of symptoms.

# ## Biochemical Explanation

## 🎯 Why this matters

This explains depression through **brain chemistry changes**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Focus on **neurotransmitters**
> * Key chemicals = **serotonin + noradrenaline**
> * Called **monoamines**
> * Imbalance may cause depression or mania
> * Known as **monoamine hypothesis**

---

## Monoamine Hypothesis

Developed from research on **reserpine**.

| Finding                           |
| --------------------------------- |
| Drug lowered **noradrenaline**    |
| Patients developed **depression** |

Therefore:

**Low noradrenaline → depression**

---

### Role of Serotonin

Serotonin regulates noradrenaline levels.

| Scenario       | Result                           |
| -------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Low serotonin  | noradrenaline drops → depression |
| High serotonin | noradrenaline rises → mania      |

---

💡 Memory hook
Think of serotonin as the **“volume knob” controlling noradrenaline**.

---

## Serotonin Imbalances

Serotonin levels can change due to:

| Factor                      | Effect                               |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **Diet**                    | tryptophan levels change             |
| **Stress**                  | cortisol blocks serotonin production |
| **Receptors**               | postsynaptic receptor sensitivity    |
| **Transporters**            | serotonin reuptake                   |
| **Monoamine oxidase (MAO)** | breaks down serotonin                |

---

💡 Memory hook
Imagine serotonin as **letters passing between neurons**.

Problems happen when:

* letters destroyed
* mailboxes broken
* mail trucks (transporters) too fast

---

🧠 Quick check
What enzyme **breaks down serotonin**?

---

| ✅ Strength                             | ❌ Weakness                                          |

| Supported by **experimental evidence** | Overly **biologically deterministic**               |

| Depressive symptoms increased          | depressed women sometimes show **higher serotonin** |

# Beck’s Cognitive Theory of Depression (1962)

## 🎯 Why this matters

Depression may not start with **chemistry** — it may start with **how we think about ourselves, the world, and the future**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Depression caused by **negative thinking patterns**
> * Based on **dysfunctional core beliefs**
> * Key concept: **Negative Cognitive Triad**
> * Thoughts become **biased through cognitive distortions**
> * Therapy aims to **restructure these thoughts**

---

## The Negative Cognitive Triad

| Component                   | Example Thought             |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| **Negative view of self**   | "I am worthless"            |
| **Negative view of world**  | "Everything goes wrong"     |
| **Negative view of future** | "Nothing will ever improve" |

🔑 **Triad = Self + World + Future**

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine someone wearing **three dark glasses**:

* Glass 1 = Self
* Glass 2 = World
* Glass 3 = Future

Everything they see becomes **dark and hopeless**.

---

## Origins of Negative Core Beliefs

Negative beliefs often develop from **childhood experiences**.

| Experience                   | Example            |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------ |
| **Criticism**                | harsh parenting    |
| **Rejection**                | peer exclusion     |
| **Bullying**                 | social humiliation |
| **Neglect/abuse**            | emotional trauma   |
| **Bereavement**              | early loss         |
| **Overprotective parenting** | lack of autonomy   |

⚡ These experiences create **dysfunctional schemas**.

---

## Cognitive Biases (Faulty Thinking)

People vulnerable to depression show **automatic negative thinking patterns**.

### Common Cognitive Distortions

| Bias                     | Meaning                              | Example                                |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| **Dichotomous thinking** | All-or-nothing thinking              | "If I fail once, I'm a total failure." |
| **Arbitrary inference**  | Negative conclusion without evidence | "They didn't reply → they hate me."    |
| **Catastrophising**      | Expecting worst outcome              | "This freckle must be cancer."         |
| **Personalisation**      | Blaming self for events              | "I lost my job because I'm useless."   |

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine a **brain with a broken filter**:

* Only **negative evidence passes through**
* Positive evidence gets **blocked**

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> What are the **three parts** of the negative cognitive triad?

---

## Information Processing Bias

Depressed individuals process information differently.

| Cognitive Stage    | Bias                             |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| **Attention**      | focus on negative events         |
| **Memory**         | recall negative experiences more |
| **Interpretation** | interpret events pessimistically |

🔗 **Connection**

# Learned Helplessness & Attributional Style

## 🎯 Why this matters

Depression may occur when people believe **nothing they do can change bad outcomes**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Originated from **animal research**
> * Individuals learn **lack of control**
> * Leads to **passive behaviour**
> * Linked to **pessimistic attributional style**
> * Explains depressive thinking patterns

---

## Animal Research (Maier & Seligman, 1976)

Dogs were conditioned to expect **unavoidable electric shocks**.

### Experimental Setup

| Stage        | Description                                  |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Conditioning | Light paired with shock                      |
| Testing      | Dogs placed in enclosure with escape barrier |

### Results

| Group             | Behaviour                  |
| ----------------- | -------------------------- |
| Experimental dogs | Did **not attempt escape** |
| Control dogs      | Escaped easily             |

🔑 Dogs **learned helplessness**.

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine being locked in a room where **nothing you try works**.
Eventually you stop trying — even when the **door becomes open**.

---

## Attributional Style (Human Depression)

People explain negative events differently.

### Pessimistic vs Optimistic Attribution

| Attribution              | Pessimistic (Depressed)        | Optimistic                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| **Internal vs External** | "It's my fault"                | "Circumstances caused it"    |
| **Global vs Specific**   | "Everything in my life is bad" | "Only this situation is bad" |
| **Stable vs Unstable**   | "It will never change"         | "Things can improve"         |

⚡ Depression linked to **internal + global + stable attributions**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> Which **three attribution styles** predict depression?

# Study: Seligman et al. (1988)

## 🔑 One-line Summary

People with **pessimistic attribution styles** show **more severe depressive symptoms**, but therapy can change these patterns.

---

## Study Overview

| Feature            | Details                                                              |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Aim**            | Test correlation between attributional style and depression severity |
| **Sample**         | 51 patients (39 unipolar, 12 bipolar)                                |
| **Measures**       | Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)                                      |
|                    | Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ)                              |
| **Procedure**      | 6 months weekly cognitive therapy (avg 22.5 sessions)                |
| **Diagnosis tool** | SADS interview                                                       |
| **Follow-ups**     | 32 reassessed post-therapy; 26 after 12 months                       |

Control group = **matched non-depressed participants**

---

## Results

| Finding                   | Explanation                                  |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Positive correlation      | pessimistic thinking ↔ depressive symptoms   |
| Therapy reduced pessimism | symptoms improved                            |
| Similar effect            | unipolar & bipolar depression                |
| Trait anxiety             | higher pessimism in anxious bipolar patients |
| Relapse link              | pessimism predicted relapse                  |
| Control group             | pessimism stable over time                   |

⚡ Suggests pessimism may be a **personality trait**.

---

## Conclusion

People with mood disorders show **internal, global, stable attributions**, but therapy can **modify this thinking style**.

---

## Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                                      | ❌ Weakness                  |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Matched control group                           | Gender imbalance (31F, 20M) |
| Method triangulation (BDI + clinical interview) | High dropout rate           |
| Longitudinal follow-up                          | Reduced generalisability    |

# Biological Treatments for Depression

## 🎯 Why this matters

Many treatments target **brain chemistry**, particularly **monoamine neurotransmitters**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Antidepressants increase **serotonin / noradrenaline / dopamine**
> * Three main types: **SSRIs, MAOIs, Tricyclics**
> * Aim to increase neurotransmitter activity
> * Supported by large-scale research
> * But effectiveness debated

# Types of Antidepressants

## Tricyclic Antidepressants

### Mechanism

| Action                      | Effect                    |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Block **SERT transporters** | more serotonin in synapse |
| Block **NET transporters**  | more noradrenaline        |

⚡ Neurotransmitters remain longer in the synapse.

❗ Side effects common because they bind to **many receptors**.

---

## MAOIs

### Mechanism

| Process                  | Explanation                         |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Monoamine oxidase enzyme | breaks down neurotransmitters       |
| MAOI drugs               | block enzyme activity               |
| Result                   | increased serotonin & noradrenaline |

### Risk

| Problem          | Explanation        |
| ---------------- | ------------------ |
| Tyramine buildup | from certain foods |
| Consequence      | risk of stroke     |

⚠️ Therefore used **only if other drugs fail**.

---

## SSRIs

### Mechanism

| Action                                      | Result                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Block serotonin reuptake transporter (SERT) | serotonin remains in synapse |

Example drug:

**Fluoxetine (Prozac)**

⚡ More **targeted** than tricyclics.

---

## Evaluation of Antidepressants

### Research Evidence

| Study                  | Finding                      |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Cipriani et al. (2018) | 21 antidepressants > placebo |
| Sample size            | 116,477 participants         |

Strength → strong evidence from **522 double-blind trials**

---

### Limitations

| Problem                     | Explanation                       |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Small clinical improvement  | often only 2 points on HDRS       |
| Individual drug differences | some drugs poorly tolerated       |
| Sample bias                 | mild/moderate cases often studied |

---

### Symptom-specific Evidence

| Study                     | Finding                                    |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Hieronymous et al. (2016) | 90% improvement when mood symptom isolated |

---

### Everyday Life Issues

Common side effects:

* anxiety
* insomnia
* nausea
* dizziness
* fatigue
* weight gain

⚠️ These cause **non-compliance** early in treatment.

---

### Relapse

Symptoms often return after stopping medication.

Recommendation:

## 🎯 What are Impulse Control Disorders?

# Biological Treatment: Opiate Antagonists

## Key Study: **Grant et al. (2008)** – Treating Gambling Disorder with Drugs vs Placebo

### 🎯 Why this matters

> Gambling disorder can destroy lives — understanding **who responds to medication** helps clinicians choose the best treatment.

---

## TLDR

> * **Opiate antagonists** (e.g. **nalmefene**, **naltrexone**) block **opioid receptors**
> * This prevents **dopamine-driven euphoria** from gambling
> * **Family history of alcoholism** predicted the **best treatment response**
> * **Strong gambling urges** slightly predicted better response to higher doses
> * **Younger participants** showed stronger **placebo effects**

---

## Biological mechanism behind the treatment

| Step | What happens                                                             |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1    | **Opiates** bind to **opioid receptors** in the nervous system           |
| 2    | This **inhibits GABA release**                                           |
| 3    | Reduced GABA → **dopamine activity increases**                           |
| 4    | Dopamine increase → **euphoria / reward feeling**                        |
| 5    | **Opiate antagonists** occupy receptors but **do NOT activate them**     |
| 6    | Result → **reduced dopamine reward → gambling becomes less reinforcing** |

💡 **Memory hook**

> Imagine a **casino jackpot machine wired to your brain**.
> Normally → dopamine fireworks 🎆
> **Opiate antagonists unplug the jackpot machine.**

---

### 🧠 Quick check

> Why would blocking opioid receptors reduce gambling behaviour?

# Psychological Therapy: Covert Sensitisation

## 🎯 Why this matters

> Instead of blocking brain chemistry, this therapy **trains the brain to feel disgust toward the behaviour**.

---

## TLDR

> * Based on **classical conditioning**
> * Client imagines **disgusting or shameful consequences**
> * These become associated with the **impulse behaviour**
> * Urges decrease due to **negative emotional associations**

---

## Core concept

| Element                    | Description                                    |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **Unconditioned stimulus** | Disturbing imagery (vomiting, shame)           |
| **Unconditioned response** | Disgust / anxiety                              |
| **Conditioned stimulus**   | Target behaviour (stealing, gambling)          |
| **Conditioned response**   | Negative emotion when thinking about behaviour |

💡 **Memory hook**

> Imagine reaching to steal an item and suddenly **projectile vomiting everywhere in the shop** 🤢

Your brain quickly learns:

**stealing = disgust**

# Psychological Therapy: Imaginal Desensitisation

## 🎯 Why this matters

> This therapy helps people **mentally rehearse resisting their impulse**, reducing urges before behaviour happens.

---

## TLDR

> * Uses **relaxation + guided imagery**
> * Based on **Behaviour Completion Mechanism**
> * Clients imagine the **full behaviour sequence**
> * Ends with **negative outcomes and alternative behaviour**

---

## The theory

### Behaviour Completion Mechanism (McConaghy, 1980)

| Concept                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Compulsive behaviour continues because the behavioural sequence is never fully completed |

Imaginal desensitisation:

* Mentally **complete the sequence**
* Reduce **tension and urges**

---

# Key Techniques

## Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)

| Step | Description                      |
| ---- | -------------------------------- |
| 1    | Tense muscle group               |
| 2    | Breathe in                       |
| 3    | Relax muscle while breathing out |

Goal:

* Reduce **physiological arousal**
* Improve focus during imagery

---

## Guided Imagery

Clients listen to **scripts** describing situations that trigger impulses.

Scripts include **6 stages**.

---

# 6.5 Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

## Measures, Explanations, and Treatments

---

# ## Measuring OCD Symptoms (Psychometrics)

🎯 **Hook:**
Before you can treat OCD, you first need a **reliable way to measure how severe it is.**

> **TLDR**
>
> * **MOCI** → self-report questionnaire measuring **types of OCD symptoms**
> * **Y-BOCS** → clinician **semi-structured interview** measuring **severity**
> * **MOCI** focuses on **symptom categories**
> * **Y-BOCS** focuses on **symptom intensity**
> * Weak correlation between the two tests (Goodman et al., 1989b)

---

# ## The Maudsley Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory (MOCI)

### 🎯 Why it matters

Researchers needed a **standard way to classify OCD symptoms** for research and treatment.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Developed by **Hodgson & Rachman (1977)**
> * **Self-report questionnaire**
> * **True / False items**
> * Identifies OCD **symptom types**
> * Major types: **cleaning** and **checking**

---

### Development of the MOCI

| Step                | Detail                                               |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Interviews          | **30 people with OCD** interviewed                   |
| Initial items       | **65 true/false statements**                         |
| Participants        | Patients from **Maudsley Hospital (London)**         |
| Groups              | OCD obsession group vs **anxiety without obsession** |
| Final questionnaire | **30 items** selected                                |

Example items:

* **“I avoid using public telephones because of contamination.”**
* **“I frequently get nasty thoughts and have difficulty getting rid of them.”**

---

### OCD types identified

| Major Types  | Minor Types  |
| ------------ | ------------ |
| **Cleaning** | **Slowness** |
| **Checking** | **Doubting** |

💡 **Memory hook**

> Imagine a person **scrubbing their hands while checking the door 20 times** —
> OCD = **Clean → Check → Doubt → Slow**.

---

### 🧠 Quick Check

> What type of data does the **MOCI collect?**

Answer: **Self-report true/false questionnaire data**

---

## Evaluating the MOCI

| ✅ Strength                                                                    | ❌ Weakness                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **High test-retest reliability**                                              | **Forced-choice answers reduce validity**                  |
| 89% of **1500 score pairs identical** after 1 month (Hodgson & Rachman, 1977) | People must choose **true/false only**                     |
| Reliable tests reduce risk of **misdiagnosis**                                | People may not know what counts as **“average” behaviour** |

⚠️ **Common mistake**

People assume **reliable = valid**
→ A test can be **consistent but still inaccurate**.

---

### 🔗 Link to Research Methods

* **Test-retest reliability**
* **Validity of questionnaires**
* **Psychometrics**

---

### 📝 Exam tip

When evaluating **psychometric tests**, always mention:

* **Reliability**
* **Validity**

---

# ## Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)

🎯 **Hook:**
The Y-BOCS measures **how severe OCD is**, not just what type.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Developed by **Goodman et al. (1989a)**
> * **Semi-structured interview**
> * **10 items total**
> * Measures **severity of obsessions and compulsions**
> * Score range **0–40**

---

### Structure of the Y-BOCS

| Section         | Items   |
| --------------- | ------- |
| **Obsessions**  | 5 items |
| **Compulsions** | 5 items |

Each item scored **0–4**

---

### Severity categories

| Score     | Severity |
| --------- | -------- |
| **8–15**  | Mild     |
| **16–23** | Moderate |
| **24–31** | Severe   |
| **32–40** | Extreme  |

---

### Example questions

**Time spent on obsessive thoughts**

| Score | Meaning       |
| ----- | ------------- |
| 0     | None          |
| 1     | < 1 hour/day  |
| 2     | 1–3 hours/day |
| 3     | 3–8 hours/day |
| 4     | > 8 hours/day |

---

**Effort to resist compulsions**

| Score | Meaning                 |
| ----- | ----------------------- |
| 0     | Always resist           |
| 2     | Resist most of the time |
| 3     | Reluctantly give in     |
| 4     | Completely give in      |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> Think of a **doctor with a checklist scoring your OCD from 0–40**
> like a **“severity meter.”**

---

### 🧠 Quick Check

> How long does a typical **Y-BOCS interview** take?

Answer: **≈30 minutes**

---

## Evaluating Y-BOCS

| ✅ Strength                                              | ❌ Weakness                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **High inter-rater reliability**                        | Focuses only on symptoms **in the past week** |
| Multiple interviewers agree on scores                   | Symptoms may fluctuate across the week        |
| Goodman et al. (1989a): **40 patients, 4 interviewers** | Lack of qualitative context                   |

---

### 🔗 Link to Research Methods

Concepts involved:

* **Inter-rater reliability**
* **Semi-structured interviews**

---

### 🧠 Quick Check

> What type of reliability does the Y-BOCS demonstrate?

Answer: **Inter-rater reliability**

# ## MOCI vs Y-BOCS (Exam Comparison)

⚠️ **Do NOT confuse these**

| Feature         | **MOCI**                        | **Y-BOCS**               |
| --------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Type            | Self-report questionnaire       | Clinician interview      |
| Format          | True / False                    | 0–4 rating scale         |
| Measures        | **Type of symptoms**            | **Severity of symptoms** |

2️⃣ **Difference**

The **MOCI is a self-report questionnaire**, whereas the **Y-BOCS is a semi-structured interview conducted by a clinician**.

# ## Genetic Explanation

🎯 **Hook:**
OCD can run in families — suggesting **genes influence vulnerability**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * OCD shows **family patterns**
> * Supported by **twin studies**
> * Disorder is **polygenic**
> * Several genes linked to **serotonin and dopamine**
> * Examples: **5-HTT, DRD4, COMT**

---

## Family evidence

### Lewis (1936)

| Finding                        |
| ------------------------------ |
| **37%** had a parent with OCD  |
| **22%** had a sibling with OCD |

🔑 Suggests OCD **runs in families**.

---

## Twin evidence

### Carey & Gottesman (1981)

| Twin type            | Concordance |
| -------------------- | ----------- |
| **Monozygotic (MZ)** | **87%**     |
| **Dizygotic (DZ)**   | **47%**     |

⚡ Large difference suggests **genetic influence**.

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> Identical twins share **100% genes → 87% OCD match**

---

## Candidate genes

OCD involves **many genes**.

### Taylor (2013)

* **230 genes** linked to OCD

⚡ Shows OCD is **polygenic**.

---

### Serotonin gene

**5-HTT**

* Mutation linked to **OCD vulnerability**
* Also linked to **depression**

⚠️ Problem:
Mutation linked to **many psychiatric disorders**.

---

### Dopamine genes

| Gene     | Function                             |
| -------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **DRD4** | Codes for **D4 dopamine receptors**  |
| **COMT** | Enzyme that **breaks down dopamine** |

Key findings:

* **Allele 2** of DRD4 less common in OCD → may **protect**
* **Low activity COMT allele** → **more dopamine**

---

### SLITRK5 and BDNF

| Gene        | Function                      |
| ----------- | ----------------------------- |
| **SLITRK5** | Development of **synapses**   |
| **BDNF**    | Maintains **neural networks** |

Problems affecting these may cause **abnormal cognition and behaviour**.

---

### 🧠 Quick Check

> What does **polygenic** mean?

Answer: **A disorder influenced by many genes**

---

## Evaluation of Genetic Explanation

| ✅ Strength                                                            | ❌ Weakness                        |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Supported by **animal research**                                      | Cultural differences ignored      |
| **Shmelkov et al. (2010)** knock-out mice showed compulsive behaviour | OCD symptoms vary across cultures |

---

### Shmelkov et al. (2010)

| Finding                                     |
| ------------------------------------------- |
| SLITRK5 gene silenced in **knock-out mice** |
| Mice showed **compulsive grooming**         |
| Also **marble burying behaviour**           |

⚡ Suggests **genes influence compulsive behaviour**

---

### Nature vs Nurture

### Fontenelle et al. (2004)

| Country   | Aggression obsessions |
| --------- | --------------------- |
| Brazil    | **70%**               |
| Singapore | **16%**               |

Shows **culture influences OCD symptoms**.

---

### 📝 Exam tip

Use **Issues & Debates**

* **Biological determinism**
* **Nature vs nurture**

# ## Biochemical Explanation

🎯 **Hook:**
OCD may be caused by **imbalances in brain chemicals**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Key neurotransmitters: **serotonin, dopamine**
> * **BDNF protein** maintains neural networks
> * **Oxytocin** also linked to symptoms
> * Gene changes may affect neurotransmitters
> * Treatments target these chemicals

---

## Serotonin

Possible issues:

* **Transport problems**
* **Receptor abnormalities**
* **MAO-A enzyme issues**

Result → **serotonin imbalance**

---

## Dopamine

Abnormal **D4 dopamine receptors** may increase vulnerability.

---

## Oxytocin

### Humble et al. (2013)

Finding:

Higher **oxytocin levels → more severe OCD**

Explanation:

**Upregulation**

* Fewer receptors
* Body releases **more hormone**

---

### Supporting research

**Cappi et al. (2016)**

* Increased **methylation of OXTR gene**
* Reduces **oxytocin receptor production**

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> OCD brain chemicals:
> **Serotonin – Dopamine – Oxytocin**

---

⚠️ Biological explanations may overlook:

# ## Cognitive Explanation (Thinking Errors)

🎯 **Hook:**
People with OCD **misinterpret normal thoughts as dangerous**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Everyone has intrusive thoughts
> * OCD sufferers **over-interpret them**
> * Key thinking errors increase anxiety
> * Leads to obsessions and compulsions

---

### Key thinking errors

| Thinking error              | Meaning                                 |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **Personal responsibility** | Belief you must prevent harm            |
| **Thought-action fusion**   | Thinking something makes it happen      |
| **Thought-event fusion**    | Imagining an event increases likelihood |

---

Example:

> Thinking **“I might hurt someone”** → believing you actually **will**.

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> OCD brain says:
> **“If I think it… it might happen.”**

---

### 🧠 Quick Check

> According to cognitive theory, what causes obsessions?

Answer: **How thoughts are interpreted**

---

# ## Behavioural Explanation

🎯 **Hook:**
Compulsions continue because they **reduce anxiety**.

---

### Operant conditioning

| Process              | Effect                              |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Obsessive thought    | Anxiety                             |
| Compulsive behaviour | Reduces anxiety                     |
| Outcome              | Behaviour **negatively reinforced** |

---

Example

Obsessive thought → **“My hands are contaminated”**

Compulsion → **washing hands**

Result → anxiety drops → **behaviour reinforced**

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> OCD ritual = **anxiety relief button**

# 8.5.3 Individual Factors in Changing Health Beliefs

## Unrealistic Optimism

🎯 **Hook:** If you believe illness happens to *other people*, you might ignore health advice — and that can literally affect your survival.

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Unrealistic optimism** = believing **negative events are less likely for you** than others
> * People think **positive events are more likely for themselves**
> * Can cause **riskier health behaviour**
> * But can also **reduce worry and stress**
> * Important factor in **why people ignore health advice**

---

### What is Unrealistic Optimism?

🔑 **Unrealistic optimism:** a **cognitive belief** that:

| Belief type         | Explanation                              |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Negative events** | Seen as **less likely to happen to you** |
| **Positive events** | Seen as **more likely to happen to you** |

Example beliefs:

* “Cancer happens to **other people**, not me.”
* “I won’t get seriously ill.”
* “Bad health outcomes happen to **stereotypical people**, not me.”

⚡ **Exam core:** Unrealistic optimism = **optimism bias about personal risk**

---

### Why it matters for health behaviour

| Effect                    | Explanation                                                                   |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ignore health advice**  | If people believe they are **not at risk**, they don't follow recommendations |
| **Risky behaviour**       | Less motivation to change unhealthy habits                                    |
| **False invulnerability** | People feel **immune to disease**                                             |

Example research:

| Study                    | Finding                                                                               |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Gassen et al. (2021)** | People at **high Covid-19 risk** were **not more cautious** than low-risk individuals |

💡 **Memory Hook**

> Imagine a guy named **Optimistic Oliver** running into a burning building saying:
> *“Fire happens to OTHER people.”*
> Meanwhile everyone else is wearing fire suits.

Your brain now links **optimism bias → ignoring danger**.

---

### Potential benefits of unrealistic optimism

| Benefit                     | Explanation                         |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Reduced worry**           | Less anxiety about possible illness |
| **Better mental wellbeing** | Can help coping during crises       |

Example:

* During **Covid-19**, optimism helped some people **reduce stress and fear**

⚠️ **Don't confuse**

| Concept                  | Meaning                                |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| **Realistic optimism**   | Positive thinking grounded in reality  |
| **Unrealistic optimism** | **Biased belief** ignoring actual risk |

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

1. What is **unrealistic optimism**?
2. Why might it cause people to **ignore health advice**?

## Key Study: Weinstein (1980)

### 🔑 One-liner

People believe **good things are more likely and bad things less likely to happen to them** compared with others.

---

### Study Overview

| Category   | Details                                                                                   |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Aim**    | Investigate whether people think **negative events are less likely for them** than others |
|            | Also test whether **perceived control increases optimism bias**                           |
| **Sample** | American **college students**                                                             |
| **Method** | Comparative rating of **42 life events**                                                  |
| **Design** | Two groups: **comparative rating group** and **event characteristics group**              |

---

### Comparative Rating Group (Study 1)

Participants estimated their chance of events compared with classmates.

| Feature             | Details                                           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Life events**     | 42 total                                          |
| **Positive events** | 18 (e.g., owning your own home)                   |
| **Negative events** | 24 (e.g., being fired)                            |
| **Rating scale**    | From **100% less likely** to **100% more likely** |

Instructions:

> “Compared to other students **of the same sex**, what are the chances this event will happen to you?”

---

### Randomisation Procedure

The 42 events were divided into **two rating forms**.

| Form       | Content   |
| ---------- | --------- |
| **Form A** | 21 events |
| **Form B** | 21 events |

These were **randomly distributed** to students.

💡 **Memory Hook**

> Imagine **42 cards**:
> 18 green (good events)
> 24 red (bad events)
> Students draw them and predict their **personal destiny**.

---

### Event Characteristics Group

Another group rated the events differently.

| Feature          | Details                                                      |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Participants** | 120 female psychology students                               |
| **Task**         | Estimate **probability events occur to university students** |

They also rated each event on:

| Variable                | Scale                                               |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Controllability**     | 1 = no control → 5 = completely controllable        |
| **Desirability**        | 1 = extremely undesirable → 5 = extremely desirable |
| **Personal experience** | 1 = never known → 5 = happened to me multiple times |

---

### Results

| Finding                               | Explanation                                        |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **37/42 events showed optimism bias** | Positive events seen as **more likely**            |
|                                       | Negative events seen as **less likely**            |
| **Variation between events**          | Not all events showed bias                         |
| **No difference for some events**     | e.g., *not ill all winter* or *victim of burglary* |

Correlations found:

| Relationship                              | Meaning                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Positive events ↔ desirability**        | More desirable events judged more likely |
| **Negative events ↔ perceived control**   | More control → lower perceived risk      |
| **Negative events ↔ personal experience** | Experience influenced risk judgments     |

Participants often compared themselves to **stereotypical victims**.

Example thinking:

> “People who get fired are irresponsible — I’m not like that.”

---

### Conclusion

Unrealistic optimism occurs when:

| Condition                 | Effect                                 |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **Event is controllable** | People believe they can **avoid it**   |
| **Emotional investment**  | People want the outcome to be positive |

⚡ **Important:**
**Desirability did NOT influence unrealistic optimism for negative events**, even **life-threatening ones**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

1. How many life events did Weinstein use?
2. What factor increased unrealistic optimism for negative events?

---

## Evaluation of Weinstein (1980)

### Strengths and Weaknesses

| ✅ Strength                                                                             | ❌ Weakness                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Nomothetic approach** allows identification of **general patterns** in optimism bias | Sample only **American university students**        |
| Demonstrates unrealistic optimism as **systematic cognitive bias**                     | **Age bias** — young adults may be more optimistic  |
| Allows **generalisation within similar populations**                                   | Cultural bias — may differ in collectivist cultures |

---

### Cultural Differences

Research suggests optimism bias varies by culture.

| Culture                                     | Typical tendency              |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Individualistic cultures** (e.g., Canada) | More **unrealistic optimism** |
| **Collectivistic cultures** (e.g., Japan)   | Less optimism bias            |

(Heine & Lehman, 1995)

💡 **Memory Hook**

> Individualistic cultures say:
> **“I control my destiny.”**

> Collectivistic cultures say:
> **“The situation controls outcomes.”**

---

### Evaluation of Unrealistic Optimism

| ✅ Strength                                        | ❌ Weakness                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reduces **stress and worry**, improving wellbeing | Debate whether optimism bias reflects **true belief or wishful thinking** |
| Helpful during crises like **Covid-19**           | Difficult to measure whether beliefs are actually **irrational**          |
| Can improve **mental resilience**                 | Measurement validity may be **questionable**                              |

(Jefferson et al., 2017)

> If asked about **why people ignore health advice**, always mention:
>
> * **Unrealistic optimism**

> * leads to **risk behaviour**

# Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (1943)

🎯 **Hook:**
Why do people sometimes quit jobs even if the salary is good? Maslow says it’s because **different needs drive motivation**.

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Maslow (1943)** proposed a **hierarchy of five needs**.
> * Lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones.
> * First four = **deficiency needs**.
> * Top level = **self-actualisation (growth need)**.
> * Used to understand **workplace motivation**.

---

## The five needs

| Level | Need                   | Meaning                       |
| ----- | ---------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 5     | **Self-actualisation** | reaching full potential       |
| 4     | **Esteem**             | respect, recognition          |
| 3     | **Belongingness**      | friendships, group acceptance |
| 2     | **Safety**             | security, stability           |
| 1     | **Physiological**      | food, warmth, sleep           |

---

### Physiological needs

🔑 **Physiological needs**

Basic survival needs.

Examples:

* food
* sleep
* warmth
* air

⚡ Without these **people cannot function properly**.

💡 **Memory hook**

> Imagine trying to finish a report while **starving and freezing**.
> Your brain says: “Forget work. Get food.”

---

### Safety needs

🔑 **Safety needs**

Need for a **safe and secure environment**.

Examples:

* financial security
* stable home
* safe workplace

---

### Love and belongingness needs

🔑 **Belongingness**

Need to feel part of a group.

Examples:

* friendships
* relationships
* workplace social interaction

💡 **Memory hook**

> Imagine an employee eating lunch **alone every day** while everyone else sits together.
> Belongingness needs are **not satisfied**.

---

### Esteem needs

🔑 **Esteem needs**

Feeling respected and valued.

Two types:

| Type            | Example                 |
| --------------- | ----------------------- |
| Self-esteem     | achievement             |
| External esteem | recognition from others |

Workplace examples:

* promotion
* job title
* praise
* involvement in decisions

---

### Self-actualisation

🔑 **Self-actualisation**

Reaching **full potential** and achieving personal goals.

Examples:

* mastering skills
* personal growth
* creative work

⚡ **Growth need**

Unlike others, it increases motivation **as it is fulfilled**.

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Picture a **pyramid made of pizza boxes**:

1️⃣ food
2️⃣ safety
3️⃣ friends
4️⃣ respect
5️⃣ becoming the **ultimate pizza master**

You can't reach the top without the bottom.

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Which four needs must be satisfied before self-actualisation?

---

## Deficiency vs Growth Needs

| Type                 | Meaning                             |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Deficiency needs** | motivated to gain something missing |
| **Growth needs**     | motivated to grow and develop       |

Deficiency needs:

* physiological
* safety
* belongingness
* esteem

Growth need:

* self-actualisation

---

### Movement in the hierarchy

Maslow believed:

* people move **up the hierarchy**
* but life events can cause movement **down**

Examples:

* job loss
* divorce

This may stop someone reaching self-actualisation.

---

## Application to the workplace

Maslow applied his theory to motivation at work.

---

### Basic needs

Employees first seek:

| Need          | Workplace example   |
| ------------- | ------------------- |
| Physiological | sufficient salary   |
| Safety        | secure job, pension |

If unmet:

⚠️ **Demotivation occurs**

Workers may **leave the job**.

---

### Social needs

If basic needs are satisfied:

Employees want:

* social interaction
* acceptance in organisation

---

### Higher needs

Next come:

| Need                   | Workplace examples         |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------- |
| **Esteem**             | promotion, recognition     |
| **Self-actualisation** | autonomy, challenging work |

⚡ When all needs are met → **high motivation**.

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Why might low salary reduce motivation according to Maslow?

---

## Evaluation of Maslow's theory

| ✅ Strength                                  | ❌ Weakness                                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Useful in workplaces to increase motivation | Lower needs may not always come first            |
| Explains many human motivations             | Some people achieve higher needs despite poverty |
| More **holistic** than cognitive theories   | Cultural differences ignored                     |

Example criticism:

Many poor individuals still show:

* **love**
* **belongingness**
* **self-actualisation**

Example:

Mother Teresa lived in **poverty** but achieved **self-actualisation**.

---

### Issues & Debates

---

#### Cultural differences

Maslow assumes **same definition of self-actualisation everywhere**.

But cultures differ:

| Culture               | View of success      |
| --------------------- | -------------------- |
| Individualistic (USA) | personal achievement |
| Collectivist (China)  | group harmony        |

---

#### Reductionism vs Holism

Maslow's theory is **holistic**.

It considers:

* physiological
* psychological
* social needs

Other theories may focus on only **one factor**.

---

#### Determinism vs Free Will

Humanistic psychology supports **free will**.

Example:

If job does not meet needs → person **chooses another job**.

However external factors (e.g. unemployment) may limit this choice.

### Generating a Scale Measuring Hierarchy of Basic Needs

---

🔑 **One-liner**

# 🧭 9.2.1 Traditional & Modern Theories of Leadership

## 🎯 Why this matters

Understanding **why some people become leaders and others don’t** helps organisations recruit, train, and evaluate managers — a major topic in organisational psychology.

---

# 🔄 Universalist Theories of Leadership

## 🎯 Core Idea

Universalist theories try to find **traits all good leaders share**, assuming these traits work **in any situation**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Leaders share universal **traits or characteristics**
> * These traits predict leadership success
> * Leadership is mostly about the **individual**, not the situation
> * Strong focus on **nature over nurture**
> * Includes **Great Person**, **Charismatic**, **Transformational** leadership

---

⚡ **Exam definition**

🔑 **Universalist theories** = theories that attempt to identify **a universal set of characteristics or traits that make someone an effective leader regardless of situation**.

---

## ⚠️ Big debate link

🔗 **Reductionism vs Holism**

Universalist theories are usually **reductionist** because they reduce leadership to **a set of traits**.

They ignore:

* learning
* training
* workplace environment
* situational factors

---

# 👑 Great Person Theory

## 🎯 Hook

The idea that **history is shaped by heroic individuals born to lead.**

> **TLDR**
>
> * Leaders are **born not made**
> * Leadership traits are **inherited**
> * Great leaders emerge during **crisis or upheaval**
> * Developed by **Thomas Carlyle (1841)**
> * Leadership seen as almost **divinely inspired**

---

### Core assumptions

| Idea                       | Explanation                                  |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Leaders are born**       | Leadership ability is inherited              |
| **Traits are fixed**       | People either have them or don’t             |
| **Crisis creates leaders** | Great leaders appear when society needs them |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine **a baby wearing a crown 👶👑**.

The baby is born already holding a **royal sceptre shouting “I LEAD!”**.

That’s **Great Person Theory**:
leaders are **literally born leaders**.

---

### Historical origins

| Researcher                | Idea                                     |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Thomas Carlyle (1841)** | “History is the biography of great men.” |
| Example figures           | Martin Luther, Shakespeare               |

Carlyle believed great leaders were **sent by God with divine inspiration**.

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                                               | ❌ Weakness                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Real-life support — some leaders emerge without training | Ignores **learning and training**                                   |
| Some people naturally rise to leadership roles           | Cannot explain why many people with traits **don’t become leaders** |

---

🔗 **Issues & Debates: Nature vs Nurture**

Great Person Theory focuses entirely on:

⚡ **Nature**

Leadership traits are **“gifts from God”**.

It ignores **nurture** (training and experience).

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Why is Great Person Theory considered **nature-based**?

---

# ⭐ Charismatic Leadership Theory

## 🎯 Hook

Some leaders inspire people so strongly that followers **admire and obey them almost automatically**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Leaders have strong **charisma**
> * Followers **respect and admire** them
> * Leaders communicate inspiring **visions**
> * They detect **threats and opportunities**
> * High expectations for themselves and followers

---

### Key characteristics

| Trait                       | Explanation                                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Charisma**                | Ability to influence and attract followers |
| **Popularity**              | Builds strong relationships                |
| **Environmental awareness** | Recognises risks and opportunities         |
| **Communication skill**     | Inspires followers through vision          |
| **High standards**          | Expects high performance                   |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine a leader with **a glowing aura ✨**.

Everyone around them suddenly says:

> “Wow… I suddenly want to work harder.”

That glowing aura = **charisma**.

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                               | ❌ Weakness                            |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Can be studied **quantitatively**        | Ignores situational factors           |
| More scientific than Great Person Theory | Focuses only on **individual traits** |

---

### Research support

# 🧠 Adaptive Leadership — Heifetz Model

## 🎯 Hook

Modern workplaces face **constant change**, so leaders must help organisations **adapt**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Organisations face **adaptive challenges**
> * Leaders mobilise people to respond
> * Workers must help solve problems
> * Leaders manage **distress during change**
> * Model includes **six leadership principles**

---

## What are adaptive challenges?

Situations where:

* old solutions stop working
* values and behaviours must change
* organisations must **adapt**

Example:

* new technology
* market changes
* restructuring

---

⚠️ Key point

Solutions often come from:

👥 **the workforce**

not just leaders.

---

# Heifetz’s Six Leadership Principles

| Principle                           | Meaning                                      |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Get on the balcony**              | Step back and analyse the system             |
| **Identify the adaptive challenge** | Recognise threats quickly                    |
| **Regulate distress**               | Manage stress levels during change           |
| **Maintain disciplined attention**  | Encourage open discussion                    |
| **Give work back to employees**     | Workers take responsibility                  |
| **Protect voices from below**       | Support whistleblowers and creative thinkers |

---

💡 **Memory story**

Imagine a leader:

1️⃣ climbs to a **balcony**
2️⃣ sees the **problem**
3️⃣ calms the **panic**
4️⃣ forces people to **face reality**
5️⃣ makes workers **solve it themselves**
6️⃣ protects the **truth-tellers**

That’s **adaptive leadership**.

---

### Evaluation

| ✅ Strength                                     | ❌ Weakness                         |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Real-world application (e.g., British Airways) | Reductionist                       |
| Includes situational factors                   | May not generalise across cultures |

# 🧠 Leadership Styles

## Muczyk & Reimann (1987)

## 🎯 Hook

The best leadership style **depends on the situation**.

> **TLDR**
>
> * Two key factors: **participation** & **direction**
> * Leadership style depends on **culture and context**
> * Four main leadership styles
> * Organisations shift styles as they develop

---

### Leadership dimensions

| Dimension         | Meaning                         |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **Participation** | Worker involvement in decisions |
| **Direction**     | Control over execution          |

---

### Four leadership styles

| Style                   | Characteristics                 |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **Directive autocrat**  | No participation, high control  |

# Claypoole & Szalma (2019) — Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)

## 🎯 Why this matters

Modern workplaces constantly monitor employees (webcams, software, metrics). Does **being watched actually make people work better?**

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)** = technology used to monitor workers
> * Based on **social facilitation** → performance changes when observed
> * Tested during a **vigilance (sustained attention) task**
> * **Electronic presence improved performance**
> * Participants monitored detected **more signals, faster, with fewer errors**

---

## Key Concepts

### **Electronic Performance Monitoring (EPM)**

* **EPM** = electronic systems used to track worker performance
* Example systems:

  * webcams
  * activity tracking software
  * screen monitoring

📊 In the USA, **over 70% of organisations** use electronic monitoring (American Psychiatric Association, 2007).

---

### **Contradictory research**

| Finding                           | Explanation                           |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| EPM can **increase productivity** | Social facilitation                   |
| EPM can **impair performance**    | Evaluation anxiety on difficult tasks |

Example: **Aiello & Svec (1993)**

* Monitoring (human or electronic) **reduced performance on complex tasks**

---

### **Social facilitation explanation**

Observation → **evaluation apprehension**

Effects:

| Task type           | Effect of being watched |
| ------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Simple / boring     | Performance improves    |
| Complex / difficult | Performance worsens     |

💡 **Memory hook**

> Imagine someone watching you sweep the floor (easy task) — you work faster.
> But imagine someone watching you solve **hard maths** — suddenly your brain freezes.

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Why might monitoring improve performance on boring tasks but worsen complex ones?

# Key Study: Claypoole & Szalma (2019)

### 🔑 One-liner

**Electronic monitoring increased accuracy, reduced errors, and sped up responses in a sustained attention task.**

---

## Aim

To determine whether **social facilitation effects occur with electronic monitoring** during a **vigilance task**.

---

## Methodology

**Laboratory experiment**

Two conditions:

| Condition           | Description             |
| ------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Control             | No monitoring           |
| Electronic presence | Webcam + video recorder |

---

## Sample

| Feature      | Details                       |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Participants | 106                           |
| Age          | Mean = 20.57 (range 18–37)    |
| Gender       | 65 female, 41 male            |
| Recruitment  | Psychology experiment website |
| Type         | Undergraduate volunteers      |
| Incentive    | Course credit                 |
| Allocation   | Random assignment             |

⚠️ **Important**

All participants were **university students in the Southern USA**.

---

## Procedure

### Preparation

Participants:

* surrendered **phones and watches**
* randomly assigned to condition

💡 **Memory hook**

> Researchers removed phones so nobody could secretly check TikTok during the attention task.

---

### Conditions

| Control            | Electronic Presence                 |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Alone in room      | Webcam + video recorder             |
| No cameras         | Webcam sends **live feed**          |
| No social presence | Recording said to be reviewed later |

Important:
⚠️ **Nothing was actually recorded → deception**

---

### Vigilance task

Participants watched a screen with **two-digit numbers**.

They pressed **spacebar** when a **critical signal** appeared.

Critical signal examples:

| Example | Reason         |
| ------- | -------------- |
| 77      | difference = 0 |
| 65      | difference = 1 |
| 01      | difference = 1 |

---

### Timing of task

| Stage             | Duration   |
| ----------------- | ---------- |
| Practice          | 3 minutes  |
| Main task         | 24 minutes |
| Signals per 6 min | 5          |
| Total signals     | 20         |

Numbers displayed for **1000 ms**.

Participants could respond anytime in that window.

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Why did researchers remove phones and watches before the experiment?

---

# Results

| Measure                | Electronic presence | Control |
| ---------------------- | ------------------- | ------- |
| **Correct detections** | 0.6906              | 0.6038  |
| **False alarms**       | 0.0059              | 0.0201  |
| **Response time (ms)** | 767.34              | 802.02  |

---

### Main findings

Participants **being monitored**:

✔ detected **more signals**
✔ made **fewer false alarms**
✔ responded **faster**

---

### Vigilance decline

Across both groups:

* **performance decreased over time**

This matches **previous vigilance research**.

💡 **Memory hook**

> Think of airport security staff after hours of scanning luggage — attention slowly fades.

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Which condition had the fastest response times?

---

# Conclusion

Electronic monitoring:

✔ **Improves performance on sustained attention tasks**
✔ Works through **social facilitation**

Video monitoring:

* **effective**
* **less intrusive** than other monitoring types

---

* medical screening

may produce **different results**.

# The Hawthorne Effect

## 🎯 Why this matters

Researchers may think a **variable causes behaviour change**, when the real reason is **people know they’re being observed**.

---

## TLDR

> * **Hawthorne effect** = productivity rises when workers **know they are being studied**
> * Original studies conducted **1920s at Western Electric Hawthorne plant**
> * Lighting changes **did not affect productivity**
> * Being **observed increased performance**

---

## Definition

**Hawthorne Effect**

→ **Performance increases because people know they are part of a study.**

⚠️ This means results may be caused by **researcher presence**, not the variable being studied.

---

## Study: Hawthorne Studies (Mayo et al.)

### 🔑 One-liner

Workers increased productivity **regardless of lighting changes**, suggesting **observation itself changed behaviour**.

---

### Study summary

| Aim               | Test effect of **lighting on productivity**                          |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sample            | Workers at **Western Electric Hawthorne plant**, Chicago             |
| Method            | **Field experiment**                                                 |
| Groups            | Experimental (lighting changed) vs Control (lighting constant)       |
| Result            | Productivity **increased in both groups**                            |
| Extreme condition | Productivity only dropped when lighting reached **moonlight levels** |
| Conclusion        | Productivity increase due to **being observed**                      |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> Workers suddenly become **super productive because “the boss is watching” 👀**.

---

## Relay Test Room Experiment

Follow-up study with **five women workers**.

Researchers manipulated:

* Rest breaks
* Work hours
* Physical conditions

### Result

No matter what changed:

**Productivity increased**

Interpretation → **Hawthorne effect**

Workers worked harder because:

* They were **observed**
* They received **attention**

---

### Alternative explanations

**Wikstrom & Bendix (2000)** suggested other explanations:

| Possible factor     | Explanation                       |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Supervisor change   | Relief from strict supervision    |
| Increased attention | Workers felt valued               |
| Workday changes     | New schedules improved conditions |
| Participation       | Workers felt they had influence   |

---

🧠 **Quick check**

> Why might the Hawthorne effect threaten **research validity**?

# Kompier (2006) — Hawthorne Effect is a Myth

## 🔑 One-liner

Kompier argued the **Hawthorne effect is exaggerated and based on weak evidence**.

---

### Study summary

| Aim        | Re-examine Hawthorne studies                                   |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Method     | Critical analysis                                              |
| Conclusion | Hawthorne effect is a **legend rather than a scientific fact** |

---

### Five reasons Hawthorne effect may be a myth

| Reason                      | Explanation                                                                 |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scientific worth myth       | Poor methodology and small samples                                          |
| Continuous improvement myth | Productivity sometimes **declined but was ignored**                         |
| Social factors myth         | **Pay incentives** also increased productivity                              |
| Cooperation myth            | Workers sometimes **resisted management**                                   |
| Neurotic worker myth        | Worker complaints blamed on **mental health instead of workplace problems** |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

> The Hawthorne effect is like a **psychology urban legend 👻**.

---

# Evaluation of Hawthorne Studies

| ✅ Strength          | Explanation                                               |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mixed methods       | Used **interviews, observations, physiological measures** |
| Collaboration       | Partnership between **factory + university**              |
| Ecological validity | Conducted in **real workplace**                           |

---

| ❌ Weakness               | Explanation                                      |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Limited generalisability | Conducted in **one electrical plant in Chicago** |
| Cultural bias            | May only apply to **Western workplaces**         |

---

🔗 **Connection**

Links to **Social Approach**

Presence of others changes behaviour.

Example:

* **Authority presence** → obedience (Milgram)

# Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory (1959)

## 🎯 Why this matters

If a company only increases **pay and benefits** but productivity stays low, Herzberg explains **why workers still aren’t motivated**.

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Job satisfaction ≠ job dissatisfaction** (not one continuum).
> * **Hygiene factors** prevent dissatisfaction but **don’t motivate**.
> * **Motivational factors** create **true satisfaction and productivity**.
> * **Extrinsic = hygiene**, **Intrinsic = motivation**.
> * Organisations need **both types** to motivate workers.

---

## Background of the Theory

🔑 **Herzberg et al. (1959)** proposed that satisfaction and dissatisfaction come from **different sources**.

### Research Programme

* **5-year research programme** investigating **job attitudes**
* Conducted during a period of **strikes and slowdowns**

### Stage 1: Literature Review

* Reviewed **2000 studies (1900–1955)**
* Findings were **contradictory**

⚡ **Key hypothesis:**
**Satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of the same scale.**

---

### Study Method

| Feature          | Details                                                  |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sample**       | **203 accountants**                                      |
| **Location**     | **9 workplaces near Pittsburgh, USA**                    |
| **Method**       | **Semi-structured interviews**                           |
| **Technique**    | **Critical incident technique**                          |
| **Task**         | Describe a **very good** and **very bad** job experience |
| **Rating scale** | **1–21** (1 = little effect, 21 = major effect)          |

💡 **Memory image:**
Imagine a worker holding **two separate thermometers**:
one for **happiness**, one for **misery** → they are **not the same scale**.

---

## Two Categories of Job Factors

| Category                 | Type of motivation | Effect                  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| **Hygiene factors**      | **Extrinsic**      | Prevent dissatisfaction |
| **Motivational factors** | **Intrinsic**      | Create satisfaction     |

---

# Hygiene Factors (Context of the Job)

## 🎯 Why this matters

Without hygiene factors, workers become **angry and dissatisfied**, but **adding them doesn’t motivate extra effort**.

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Extrinsic factors**
> * Provided by **organisation or other people**
> * Prevent **dissatisfaction**
> * Do **NOT increase motivation**

---

### Types of Hygiene Factors

| Factor                          | Explanation                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **Pay**                         | Fair and competitive salary                    |
| **Company policies**            | Fair, flexible rules (hours, breaks, holidays) |
| **Physical working conditions** | Safe environment, maintained equipment         |
| **Interpersonal relations**     | Good relationships with peers and managers     |

⚡ **Exam one-liner:**
**Hygiene factors reduce dissatisfaction but do not increase motivation.**

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine workers standing in **dirty water**.

Hygiene factors = **removing the dirty water**
But motivation = **giving them a jetpack to fly**.

Removing the problem **does not create excitement**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> If a company increases **salary and benefits**, why might productivity stay the same?

# Motivational Factors (Content of the Job)

## 🎯 Why this matters

Real motivation comes from **meaningful work and psychological growth**.

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Intrinsic rewards**
> * Create **job satisfaction**
> * Improve **productivity and performance**
> * Support **psychological growth**

---

### Types of Motivational Factors

| Factor                 | Explanation                          |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **Recognition**        | Praise from managers                 |
| **Achievement**        | Feeling of accomplishment            |
| **Promotion / growth** | Opportunities for career advancement |
| **Responsibility**     | Autonomy and accountability for work |

⚡ **Key idea:**
**Intrinsic factors improve quality of work.**

---

### Relationship Between Intrinsic & Extrinsic

| Factor Type           | Effect                                 |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **Extrinsic factors** | Workers willing to work                |
| **Intrinsic factors** | Determine **quality and productivity** |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Think of **two switches**:

* Switch 1: **“Not miserable”** → hygiene
* Switch 2: **“Actually excited to work”** → motivation

Both switches must be **ON**.

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> Which factor increases **long-term productivity**: hygiene or motivation?

# Evaluation of Herzberg’s Theory

| ✅ Strength                        | Explanation                                                          |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Theoretical support**           | Links with **Maslow’s hierarchy of needs**                           |
| **Intrinsic motivation research** | Many studies support **intrinsic motivation improving productivity** |

---

| ❌ Weakness                         | Explanation                                           |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ignores individual differences** | Not everyone values recognition or responsibility     |
| **Different personalities**        | Some people perform well without intrinsic motivators |

---

## Cultural Differences

| Study                    | Findings                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Edward & Teoh (2009)** | Workers in **Kuala Lumpur universities** studied           |
| Result                   | **Extrinsic factors increased satisfaction**               |
| Result                   | Lack of intrinsic factors **actively demotivated workers** |

⚡ **Conclusion:**
Herzberg’s theory **may not apply equally across cultures**.

---

🔗 **Connection to other topics**

* Links to **Maslow’s hierarchy of needs**
* Related to **intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation**
* Connects to **Issues & Debates: cultural bias**

---

* **Maslow support**
* **Individual differences**
* **Cultural differences**

# Hackman & Oldham’s Job Characteristics Theory (1970s)

## 🎯 Why this matters

Some jobs naturally create **high motivation**, while others cause boredom — this theory explains **why**.

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * Uses **person-fit approach**
> * **5 core job characteristics**
> * Create **3 psychological states**
> * Lead to **high motivation and satisfaction**

---

## Person-Fit Approach

🔑 A **person-fit approach** means:

Jobs should match:

* **Personality**
* **Values**
* **Beliefs**
* **Ethnicity**
* **Behaviours**

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Think of **jobs like shoes**:

A job that fits **perfectly** → comfortable and productive
Wrong size → painful and ineffective.

---

# Psychological States

These determine job satisfaction.

| Psychological State            | Meaning                            |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| **Experienced meaningfulness** | Job feels valuable                 |
| **Experienced responsibility** | Worker feels accountable           |
| **Knowledge of results**       | Worker knows how well they perform |

---

🧠 **Quick Check**

> Which psychological state involves knowing how well you are performing?

---

# Five Core Job Characteristics

## Characteristics → Psychological States

| Job Characteristic    | Meaning                                   | Psychological State  |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| **Skill variety**     | Different tasks and skills                | Meaningfulness       |
| **Task identity**     | Completing whole job from start to finish | Meaningfulness       |
| **Task significance** | Job impacts other people                  | Meaningfulness       |
| **Autonomy**          | Freedom to make decisions                 | Responsibility       |
| **Feedback**          | Clear performance feedback                | Knowledge of results |

---

⚡ **Outcome**

If psychological states are satisfied → workers have:

* **High internal motivation**
* **High job satisfaction**
* **High work effectiveness**

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine a **perfect job machine**:

5 job features → trigger **3 psychological buttons** → produce **motivation**.

---

# Evaluation of Job Characteristics Theory

| ✅ Strength                       | Explanation                                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Supported across cultures**    | **Ayandele & Nnamseh (2014)** supported the model in **African civil service** |
| **Useful in real organisations** | Can act as a **checklist when designing jobs**                                 |

---

| ❌ Weakness                        | Explanation                                   |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Possible cultural limitations** | Developed mainly in **Western organisations** |
| **Autonomy not universal**        | Collectivist cultures may value it less       |

---

## Issues & Debates: Holism vs Reductionism

| Theory               | Approach                                             |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Herzberg**         | Focuses mainly on job factors                        |
| **Hackman & Oldham** | **Holistic** (includes personality, culture, values) |

⚡ **Conclusion:**
Hackman & Oldham provide a **more holistic theory of job satisfaction**.

# Techniques of Job Design

*(Belias & Sklikas, 2013)*

## 🎯 Why this matters

Companies can **design jobs differently** to increase **motivation and productivity**.

---

> **TLDR**
>
> * **Job design** = structuring jobs to motivate workers
> * 3 key techniques:
> * **Job rotation**
> * **Job enlargement**
> * **Job enrichment**

---

## Job Design

🔑 Definition (Armstrong, 2003)

> **Job design:** specification of **job contents, methods and relationships** to satisfy **organisational and personal needs**.

---

# 1. Job Rotation

Workers **rotate between different jobs**.

---

### Factors to Consider

| Factor                    | Explanation             |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| **Gender**                | Avoid stereotypes       |
| **Physical demands**      | Must match ability      |
| **Skills and knowledge**  | Workers must be capable |
| **Task demands and time** | Jobs must be manageable |

---

### Benefits

* Increased **training opportunities**
* Reduced **boredom**
* Improved **productivity**
* Prevents **musculoskeletal disorders**

---

### Problem

| ❌ Issue            | Explanation                               |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Skill gaps         | Workers may lack training                 |
| Gender stereotypes | Some jobs associated with certain genders |

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Think of workers on a **carousel of jobs** → constantly moving to avoid boredom.

---

# 2. Job Enlargement

Expanding a job to include **more tasks**.

---

| Type                       | Explanation                    |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| **Horizontal enlargement** | Add tasks at same skill level  |
| **Vertical enlargement**   | Worker decides how job is done |

Goal:

* Increase **variety**
* Improve **self-fulfilment**

---

# 3. Job Enrichment

Increasing **challenge and responsibility** in a job.

---

### Key Features

| Feature      | Effect                |
| ------------ | --------------------- |
| **Variety**  | More interesting work |
| **Feedback** | Improves motivation   |

⚡ **Result:**
Motivation increases → **better performance and effectiveness**.

---

💡 **Memory hook**

Imagine a boring job turning into a **video game with levels and feedback**.

---

# Evaluation of Job Design

| ✅ Strength                | Explanation                          |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **Real-world usefulness** | Improves motivation and productivity |

Evidence:

| Study                | Findings                                           |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Chen & Lu (2012)** | Job design improved **motivation and performance** |

---

| ❌ Weakness                 | Explanation                             |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **Too idealistic**         | Some jobs cannot rotate or expand       |
| **Individual differences** | Some workers may find variety stressful |

---

## Issues & Debates: Idiographic vs Nomothetic

⚡ **Key problem**

Job design assumes **one system works for everyone**.

But:

* **Personality**
* **Family life**
* **Personal circumstances**

may change what motivates people.

---

🔗 **Connection**

Links to:

* **Idiographic vs nomothetic debate**
* **Case study methods** (e.g. Saavedra & Silverman)
