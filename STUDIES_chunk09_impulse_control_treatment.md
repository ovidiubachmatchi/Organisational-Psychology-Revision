# Studies: Impulse Control Disorders — Treatment (6.3.3)

> Chapter 6.3.3 covers two main treatment types for impulse control disorders (kleptomania, pyromania, gambling disorder):
> **Biological** (opiate antagonists) and **Psychological** (covert sensitisation, imaginal desensitisation).
> Three formal studies / focus cases are covered: Grant et al. (2008), Glover (1985), and Blaszczynski & Nower (2003).

---

## Background: The Biology You Need First

Before the studies make sense, you need to understand the **opioid-dopamine reward loop**. Here it is as a chain:

| Step | What happens |
|------|-------------|
| 1 | You gamble / steal / start a fire |
| 2 | Brain releases **endorphins** (natural opioids) |
| 3 | Endorphins bind to **opioid receptors** |
| 4 | Opioid receptors suppress **GABA** |
| 5 | Less GABA = less inhibition of **dopamine** |
| 6 | Dopamine floods the reward system → **euphoria** |
| 7 | Euphoria reinforces the behaviour (operant conditioning) |

> 💡 **Opiate antagonists** (e.g., nalmefene, naltrexone) **block opioid receptors**. No receptor activation = no GABA suppression = no dopamine spike = no euphoria = no reward = behaviour fades.

## Study 1: Grant et al. (2008) — Predicting who responds to opiate antagonists for gambling disorder

### [Grant et al. (2008)] — Which gamblers benefit most from opiate-blocker drugs?

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Gambling disorder was known to involve the opioid-dopamine reward system, and drugs like **nalmefene** and **naltrexone** had already been trialled as treatments.
But doctors had a problem: not everyone responds to these drugs.
Some people take them and improve dramatically. Others see barely any effect.

Grant et al. wanted to figure out: **who is most likely to benefit?**

If you can predict who will respond, you can give the right drug to the right person rather than wasting time and money on treatments that won't work.

> 🧠 Think of it like asking: "Before we prescribe chemotherapy, which cancer patients are most likely to respond?" — personalised medicine logic.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Imagine a clinic waiting room. 284 Americans walk in, all diagnosed with **pathological gambling** (DSM-IV) and scoring 5+ on the **South Oaks Gambling Screen (SOGS)** — a validated scale for gambling severity.

They are split into two separate drug trials happening at the same time:

- **Trial 1:** 207 outpatients from 15 psychiatric centres across the USA. They will take **nalmefene** (or placebo) every day for **16 weeks**.
- **Trial 2:** 77 participants from one centre in Minnesota. They will take **naltrexone** (or placebo) for **18 weeks**.

**Within each trial**, participants are randomly assigned to one of four groups:

| Group | What they receive |
|-------|--------------------|
| Placebo | Sugar pill — no active drug |
| Low dose | Nalmefene 25 mg OR Naltrexone 50 mg |
| Medium dose | Nalmefene 50 mg OR Naltrexone 100 mg |
| High dose | Nalmefene 100 mg OR Naltrexone 150 mg |

The study is **double-blind**: neither the participants nor the researchers measuring outcomes know who got the real drug.
This is critical — if the researcher knew you had the drug, their voice, eye contact, or tone of questioning could subtly shift, inflating how much "progress" they detect.

> 💡 Think of a blind taste test. If the judge knows which glass is the expensive wine, they will likely rate it higher — even subconsciously. Double-blinding removes this bias entirely.

**What they measured (before and after treatment):**

- Severity of gambling symptoms: a modified **Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)** — a clinically validated questionnaire
- A drop of >35% in Y-BOCS score = **positive response to treatment**
- Daily functioning, anxiety, depression (questionnaires)
- Family history of alcoholism in first-degree relatives (semi-structured interview)
- Type of gambling (strategic like poker vs non-strategic like slots)
- Comorbid diagnoses (structured clinical interview)

After the 16/18 weeks, the two datasets were analysed separately — but the results were so similar the researchers **combined them** to increase statistical power.

---

**3. What they found**

**Who responded to the real drug?**

| Predictor | Strength of association | Finding |
|-----------|------------------------|---------|
| **Family history of alcoholism** (first-degree relative) | **Strongest** (p = 0.006) | Robustly predicted positive response to opiate antagonists |
| **Intensity of gambling urges at baseline** | Mild trend (higher doses only) | Stronger urges slightly predicted better response to drug |
| Age | Predicted placebo response, not drug response | Younger participants were 30% more likely to respond to placebo per decade younger |

**Demographics of note:**
- Average age of onset: **29 years old**
- Average gap from starting gambling to diagnosis: **11 years** (range: 1–40 years)
- Only **19%** had previously sought professional help
- **30%** had attended Gamblers Anonymous
- **42%** were regular tobacco users
- **24%** had a comorbid mood disorder

> ⚡ The single most important finding: **family history of alcoholism in a first-degree relative is the strongest predictor of a positive response to opiate antagonists.**

---

**4. Why it matters**

This study has direct **clinical application**.

Before Grant et al. (2008), doctors choosing between opiate antagonists and other treatments (antidepressants, antipsychotics) were essentially guessing.

Now, a doctor can ask two quick questions:
1. Does anyone in your immediate family have alcoholism?
2. How strong are your urges to gamble?

These answers — cheap and fast to obtain — significantly improve the probability of selecting the right drug.

> 🧠 It also explains **why placebos work more for younger patients**: younger people may be more susceptible to the belief that they are being treated, which itself reduces gambling behaviour. This is why placebos must always be controlled for.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Double-blind, placebo-controlled design**
→ WHY: Neither participant nor researcher knew who received real drug
→ CONSEQUENCE: Reduces experimenter bias and demand characteristics, increasing **internal validity**

✅ **Large, diverse sample for nalmefene trial (N = 207, 15 centres)**
→ WHY: Recruited from different geographic locations, ages 19–72, varied ethnicity and employment
→ CONSEQUENCE: Increases **generalisability** of nalmefene findings to the wider population of gambling disorder patients

✅ **Combined two RCTs (meta-analytic approach)**
→ WHY: The two drug results were so similar that combining them increased statistical power
→ CONSEQUENCE: More reliable conclusions about predictors of treatment response

---

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Family history of alcoholism was self-reported**
→ WHY: Participants may not know all their relatives' medical histories (deceased relatives, adoption)
→ CONSEQUENCE: This key predictor variable may be **inaccurate**, which could over- or underestimate its importance; hard to replicate because semi-structured interviews vary between interviewers

❌ **No follow-up after the trial ended**
→ WHY: The study stopped measuring at week 16/18
→ CONSEQUENCE: We don't know if the gains lasted. Relapse is common in gambling disorder — if participants stopped taking medication after the trial, any improvement may have reversed. Long-term efficacy is unknown.

❌ **Naltrexone sample was small and non-diverse (N = 77, 90% Caucasian, one location: Minnesota)**
→ WHY: Smaller, less varied sample
→ CONSEQUENCE: The naltrexone-specific findings cannot be generalised as confidently as the nalmefene findings

❌ **Reductionist approach**
→ WHY: Focuses solely on blocking neurochemical reward; does not address underlying beliefs, coping skills, or emotional regulation
→ CONSEQUENCE: Without psychological therapy alongside medication, underpinning cognitive patterns remain unchanged. Once the drug stops, relapse is likely because the person hasn't developed alternative coping strategies.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Meta-analysis combining **two RCTs** (nalmefene 16-week + naltrexone 18-week)
- **Double-blind, placebo-controlled, independent measures**
- N = 284; diagnosed with DSM-IV pathological gambling + SOGS ≥ 5
- Main finding: **family history of alcoholism** = strongest predictor
- Secondary: **strong urges** (higher doses only) = mild predictor
- Placebo finding: **younger participants** more susceptible to placebo
- No follow-up = weakness
- Self-reported family history = validity weakness

📋 **Skeleton answer (6-mark describe):**
> Grant et al. (2008) combined data from two RCTs into a meta-analysis to investigate predictors of response to opiate antagonists in gambling disorder. 284 American outpatients diagnosed using DSM-IV were randomly allocated to placebo or low/medium/high doses of either nalmefene (16 weeks) or naltrexone (18 weeks). The study was double-blind and used the Y-BOCS to measure gambling symptom severity. A 35%+ reduction in Y-BOCS score was classified as a positive response. The strongest predictor of a positive drug response was family history of alcoholism. Strength of gambling urges also showed a mild association with positive response at higher doses.

📋 **Skeleton answer (eval — application to everyday life):**
> A strength of this study is its direct clinical application. By identifying family history of alcoholism as the key predictor, clinicians can ask a simple, low-cost screening question before prescribing. However, self-reporting of family history reduces validity, as participants may lack accurate knowledge of relatives' diagnoses. The lack of follow-up data is also a significant weakness, as relapse is common in gambling disorder and long-term efficacy cannot be established from this study.

## Study 2: Glover (1985) — Covert sensitisation for kleptomania

### [Glover (1985)] — Teaching a woman's brain to feel disgusted by the urge to steal

---

**1. Why this study exists**

By 1985, the dominant behavioural approach assumed that **all non-reflex behaviours are learned — and therefore can be unlearned**.

A woman had been stealing daily for 14 years. Standard psychiatric approaches (antidepressants, imagining arrest) had not worked.
Glover used a technique called **covert sensitisation** — pairing mental images of the unwanted behaviour with intensely unpleasant imagined consequences — to try to extinguish the impulse.

This case study was important because it demonstrated the technique could work even for a deeply entrenched, decades-long pattern of behaviour.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Meet the client: a **56-year-old married woman**. She had been stealing from supermarkets every single lunchtime for 14 years.

It started a year after her husband was convicted of embezzlement. She felt ashamed, her social circle disappeared, she took extra work to support them, and the resentment built. Somewhere in that shame spiral, a daily stealing habit took root.

She stole things she didn't need — baby shoes, random items. She described the urge as "overwhelming." She wished she were "chained to a wall" to stop herself.

She had already tried imagining being arrested and prosecuted. It hadn't worked.

**So Glover tried something different: vomiting imagery.**

**The therapy — 4 sessions, once every 2 weeks:**

| Session | What happened |
|---------|--------------|
| 1–2 | Muscle-relaxing medication used to deepen relaxation; therapist guided her to vividly imagine approaching items in a shop as if to steal, then imagine herself vomiting violently; other shoppers stare; she feels humiliated |
| 1–2 (crucial moment) | She imagines replacing the item. The vomiting stops. Relief floods in. The "not stealing" becomes associated with relief. |
| 3–4 | Self-hypnosis replaced the medication; she felt this made her visualisations more vivid and powerful |

> 💡 This is **classical conditioning** in action:
> - Unconditioned stimulus (vomiting image) → Unconditioned response (disgust/nausea)
> - Paired repeatedly with: thought of stealing (conditioned stimulus)
> - Result: thought of stealing → conditioned response (disgust) — the urge is now aversive

**Homework:** She practised the visualisation exercises **several times a day**.
**Practical rule:** She was told never to shop without a written list, and to leave the bag she used for stealing at home.

**Follow-up sessions:** Once every 3 months after the initial 2 months of treatment.

---

**3. What they found**

▼ **Progress over 19 months:**

| Timepoint | What happened |
|-----------|--------------|
| After 4 sessions (2 months) | Urges reduced; stole on 2 occasions (5 low-value items from 4 shops) — massively better than daily stealing |
| 3-month follow-up | Continuing daily homework; growing confidence to shop alone |
| 9-month follow-up | One lapse: stole a bar of soap; but it "no longer relieved tension as it once would have done" |
| 19-month follow-up | **No further relapses**; more cheerful, confident, sociable; rarely thought about stealing; if she did, the thought made her feel unwell rather than compelled |

> 🧠 The 9-month lapse is important: even after a relapse, the behaviour had lost its reinforcing power. The conditioned disgust had weakened the reward.

---

**4. Why it matters**

This case showed that a **14-year daily compulsion** could be significantly reduced in **just 4 sessions across 2 months** — and the gains lasted at least 19 months.

It also demonstrated that therapy must be personalised. When arrest imagery failed, vomiting imagery succeeded. The patient's own preference mattered.

It supports the **behaviourist position** that impulse control disorders are learned habits that can be extinguished using conditioning principles — without medication.

Kohn and Antonuccio (2002) later replicated success with a similar approach in a **39-year-old male** who had been stealing since age 6, showing the technique works across gender and age of onset.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Rich, detailed idiographic data**
→ WHY: The full case history (client's background, husband's crime, failed prior therapies) allows readers to understand why this approach worked for this specific client
→ CONSEQUENCE: Helps other therapists judge how transferable the findings are to their own clients — genuine clinical utility

✅ **Long follow-up period (19 months)**
→ WHY: Unlike many therapy studies, Glover tracked the client for over a year and a half post-treatment
→ CONSEQUENCE: Evidence of durable, not just short-term, behaviour change

✅ **No side effects**
→ WHY: Unlike opiate antagonists, covert sensitisation involves no medication
→ CONSEQUENCE: No risk of nausea, dizziness, dysphoria, or other drug side effects that cause patients to stop treatment

---

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **Single case study — N = 1**
→ WHY: Only one participant (a 56-year-old woman with a very specific life context)
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot generalise to the broader population of people with kleptomania — low **external validity**

❌ **Relies on vivid imagery ability**
→ WHY: The woman herself credited the therapy's success to her ability to vividly imagine the scenes
→ CONSEQUENCE: The therapy may be ineffective for people with **aphantasia** (inability to form mental images) or those with reduced imaginative capacity. Wicken et al. (2021) showed that people with aphantasia have flattened physiological responses to frightening scenarios — suggesting the conditioning mechanism would not work.

❌ **Cannot isolate the active ingredient**
→ WHY: The therapy combined vomiting imagery, muscle relaxation, self-hypnosis, practical strategies (shopping list, leaving the bag), and regular homework
→ CONSEQUENCE: Glover himself admits it is "impossible to know exactly which aspects were most effective" — the study lacks **internal validity** regarding mechanism

❌ **No control condition**
→ WHY: Case study design — no comparison group
→ CONSEQUENCE: We cannot rule out spontaneous remission or non-specific therapy effects (e.g., attention from a therapist). Without a control, causation cannot be established.

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Client: 56-year-old married woman; 14 years of daily stealing; husband's embezzlement was the trigger
- 4 sessions over 2 months, then follow-up every 3 months
- Sessions 1–2: muscle relaxant + vomiting imagery
- Sessions 3–4: self-hypnosis + vomiting imagery
- Vomiting stops when she imagines REPLACING the item → relief conditioned to NOT stealing
- Homework: practice imagery multiple times daily; strict shopping list; leave the stealing bag at home
- 19-month follow-up: only 1 lapse (bar of soap at 9 months); vast improvement overall
- Based on **classical conditioning** / **evaluative learning**
- Weakness: N = 1, relies on imagery ability (aphantasia concern), cannot isolate cause

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe, 6 marks):**
> Glover (1985) treated a 56-year-old woman who had stolen daily for 14 years using covert sensitisation. The therapist guided her through visualising approaching items in a supermarket as if to steal, then vividly imagining herself vomiting in front of other shoppers. When she imagined replacing the items, the vomiting would stop, creating a sense of relief — conditioning relief to non-stealing. Sessions 1–2 used muscle relaxants to aid relaxation; sessions 3–4 used self-hypnosis. Homework involved practising the imagery several times daily. At 19-month follow-up, she had only one minor lapse and reported the urge no longer felt overwhelming.

📋 **Eval point on idiographic vs nomothetic:**
> Glover (1985) used an **idiographic approach**, collecting detailed qualitative data specific to one individual. This allowed the therapy to be tailored to the client's own preferences (vomiting over arrest imagery) and her specific life context. However, because the approach is idiographic, it cannot generate nomothetic (universal) conclusions about the efficacy of covert sensitisation across all people with kleptomania.

## Study 3: Blaszczynski & Nower (2003) — Imaginal desensitisation for gambling disorder

### [Blaszczynski & Nower (2003)] — Using relaxation and guided imagination to defuse gambling urges

---

**1. Why this study exists**

Covert sensitisation works by making the target behaviour feel disgusting.
**Imaginal desensitisation works the opposite way**: instead of punishing the urge, it teaches the person to experience the full chain of events leading up to the behaviour — **but feel calm rather than aroused throughout**, and ultimately walk away.

The theory is McConaghy's (1980) **Behaviour Completion Mechanism**: compulsive behaviours keep recurring because the person never mentally "completes" the behavioural sequence. The incompleteness creates tension, which drives the urge again. Imaginal desensitisation provides a full completion — but a calm, non-harmful one.

Blaszczynski and Nower (2003) wrote a practical guide to the technique using a real case study to illustrate exactly how a therapist should implement it.

---

**2. The procedure — told as a story**

Meet **Mary Doe**: a 52-year-old divorced American woman, mother of two, working as a bookkeeper in Missouri. She had grown up watching her grandmother play cards. She started gambling for money in school. When gambling was legalised in Missouri, she started going to a local casino after work — every single day.

She lost **$25,000**. She began imagining stealing money to fund her gambling.

**Step 1 — The initial interview.**
The therapist sits down with Mary and uses open questions to map her **gambling trigger chain** — the precise sequence of environmental and emotional cues that lead from the beginning of her day to sitting at the poker table. Not just "you feel like gambling" — every step: the time of day, the feeling of loneliness at 5 pm, the drive past the casino, the smell of popcorn and vanilla, her favourite dealer's smile.

**Step 2 — Writing the personalised script.**
The therapist uses this information to write a detailed ~20-minute script divided into **6 scenes**:

| Scene | Purpose | Mary's example |
|-------|---------|----------------|
| 1 | Trigger the urge | 5 pm at work, colleagues leaving, loneliness builds, urge to visit casino rises |
| 2 | Planning to act | She decides to "just drive past" — packs up, feels excited |
| 3 | Arriving at venue | Approaches casino entrance, sees lights, hears music, smells popcorn and vanilla |
| 4 | Building arousal | Sees her favourite dealer smiling; feels confident; plans her bet |
| 5 | "Second thoughts" | Looks around — other players look discouraged; remembers past losses; debts flood her mind; excitement drains |
| 6 | Walking away | Leaves the table; walks out of the casino; feels **relieved and proud**; goes to a cinema instead |

> 💡 Scenes 1–4 are vivid and accurate — they must generate real arousal or the technique won't work.
> Scenes 5–6 are the "turn" — this is where the therapist engineers the calm alternative ending.

**Step 3 — The relaxation framework.**
Every session starts with **4–5 minutes of Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)**: tensing each muscle group as you breathe in, releasing as you breathe out.
Between each scene in the script, there are a few minutes of PMR to maintain deep relaxation.

> 💡 The logic: if you are deeply relaxed while imagining the trigger, your nervous system learns that the trigger does NOT mean arousal. The conditioned arousal response is extinguished.

**Step 4 — Homework.**
Mary is given an audio recording of the script.
She listens to it **2–3 times a day, for 5–7 days** — ideally at the same times each day (e.g., morning, late afternoon, before bed).
She tracks her sessions, urge strength, and any gambling incidents on handouts.

**Step 5 — Follow-up sessions.**
She may only have **2–3 face-to-face sessions** with the therapist:
1. Interview to gather information for the script
2. Practice PMR + run through the script; explain homework
3. Check progress; modify script if needed

---

**3. What they found**

> Important note: Blaszczynski and Nower (2003) used this case primarily as an **illustrative example** of how to conduct the therapy. They do **not report outcome data for Mary specifically**.

However, supporting evidence from other research:

| Study | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| **Grant et al. (2011)** | 80% of gambling disorder patients achieved abstinence after 6 sessions of **imaginal desensitisation + motivational interviewing (IDMI)**; 77% still abstinent at 6-month follow-up |
| **Grant et al. (2011) vs Gamblers Anonymous** | IDMI: 63.6% achieved abstinence; Gamblers Anonymous: only 17.1% |
| **McConaghy et al. (1991) long-term** | Imaginal desensitisation showed superior outcomes compared to aversion therapy at 2–9 year follow-up |
| **Small increase at 6-month follow-up** | Slight symptom return compared to immediate post-treatment — suggests maintenance sessions help |

---

**4. Why it matters**

Unlike medication, this therapy gives the client **transferable skills**.

The client learns PMR, which they can use in real life whenever a trigger appears — e.g., a radio advert for the lottery makes them feel the urge, and they can consciously deploy PMR to neutralise the arousal within minutes.

Unlike covert sensitisation (which creates aversion through disgust), imaginal desensitisation is less distressing — the client ends the imagined sequence feeling **calm and competent**, not disgusted. This may increase engagement and reduce dropout.

From a **cost perspective**: because sessions can be audio-recorded, the therapist only needs to spend significant time in the interview and script-writing phase. This makes it more accessible and cost-effective than extended 1-to-1 therapy.

---

**5. Strengths and Weaknesses**

**Strengths:**

✅ **Empirically supported by controlled trials (Grant et al., 2011)**
→ WHY: The technique has been tested in an RCT, not just case studies
→ CONSEQUENCE: 80% abstinence rate provides strong evidence of efficacy; comparison with Gamblers Anonymous shows it is significantly more effective than a common alternative (63.6% vs 17.1%)

✅ **Cost-effective and scalable**
→ WHY: Sessions can be recorded; therapist involvement is front-loaded in script writing
→ CONSEQUENCE: More patients can access therapy; reduces financial barriers; suitable for health services with limited budgets

✅ **Teaches transferable coping skills (PMR)**
→ WHY: PMR can be used by the client independently in triggering real-world situations
→ CONSEQUENCE: The client develops **self-efficacy** — a sense of personal control — rather than just avoiding triggers or relying on medication

✅ **Highly personalised**
→ WHY: Scripts are tailored to the individual's specific triggers, venues, emotional patterns
→ CONSEQUENCE: Higher ecological validity; the therapy mirrors the client's actual experience rather than a generic scenario

---

**Weaknesses:**

❌ **No outcome data for the Mary Doe case**
→ WHY: The case is used as a teaching illustration, not a formal study
→ CONSEQUENCE: Other therapists reading Blaszczynski and Nower (2003) learn how to conduct the technique but have no evidence it actually worked for Mary — limiting confidence in the specific application described

❌ **Requires deep relaxation ability**
→ WHY: If a client cannot achieve a state of deep relaxation (e.g., high anxiety, hyperarousal), the imagery sessions will not work effectively
→ CONSEQUENCE: Some clients may need anti-anxiety medication to participate effectively; this adds complexity and reduces accessibility

❌ **May not suit all client profiles**
→ WHY: Blaszczynski and Nower (2003) note it may be less effective for people with **attention deficit disorder** or **comorbid depression**
→ CONSEQUENCE: Clients who struggle with concentration or motivation may fail to complete daily homework sessions, reducing efficacy; that said, Grant et al. (2012) successfully used it with a 17-year-old with autism spectrum disorder and OCD, showing flexibility is possible

❌ **Requires high motivation and organisation**
→ WHY: Clients must complete 2–3 sessions of guided imagery per day for at least a week
→ CONSEQUENCE: Unmotivated or chaotic clients are unlikely to maintain the practice; this limits the population for whom the therapy is suitable

❌ **N = 1 case study (no control, no quantitative outcome for Mary)**
→ WHY: Case study methodology
→ CONSEQUENCE: Cannot establish causality; no comparison group; findings may not generalise — confounds (e.g., Mary stopping gambling for financial rather than therapeutic reasons) cannot be ruled out

---

**6. Exam tips**

⚡ **Must-knows:**
- Based on McConaghy's (1980) **Behaviour Completion Mechanism** — tension from incomplete sequences drives the urge
- 6-scene script structure: trigger → planning → arrival → arousal → second thoughts → walking away calmly
- Key components: **PMR** (progressive muscle relaxation) + **guided imagery** + **personalised script** + **homework** (2–3 times/day, 5–7 days)
- Only 2–3 face-to-face sessions; rest is audio-recorded homework
- No outcome data for Mary — weakness
- Grant et al. (2011): 80% abstinence, 77% at 6-month follow-up
- Grant et al. (2011) vs GA: 63.6% vs 17.1% abstinence
- Unlike covert sensitisation: ends with calm/relief, not disgust
- Weakness: requires motivation, relaxation ability, imagery capacity

📋 **Skeleton answer (describe, 6 marks):**
> Blaszczynski and Nower (2003) describe imaginal desensitisation as a relaxation-based psychological therapy for impulse control disorders, illustrated through the case of Mary Doe, a 52-year-old woman with gambling disorder. The therapist conducted an in-depth interview to identify the behavioural sequence leading to gambling, then wrote a personalised 6-scene guided imagery script. Sessions began with progressive muscle relaxation. The script guided Mary through her typical trigger chain, but ended with her leaving the casino calmly and choosing an alternative activity. Sessions were recorded and Mary practised the imagery 2–3 times daily as homework. The therapy aimed to extinguish conditioned arousal by pairing the trigger chain with deep relaxation rather than excitement.

📋 **Comparing biological vs psychological treatment (4 marks):**
> Both opiate antagonists (Grant et al., 2008) and imaginal desensitisation (Blaszczynski & Nower, 2003) aim to reduce the rewarding effects of impulse control behaviours. However, opiate antagonists achieve this biologically by blocking opioid receptors to prevent dopamine release, while imaginal desensitisation achieves it psychologically by extinguishing conditioned arousal through relaxation. Opiate antagonists require minimal daily effort (taking a pill) but do not build coping skills and may cause side effects. Imaginal desensitisation teaches transferable skills (PMR) but demands high motivation and daily practice.

## Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Opiate antagonist** | Drug that blocks opioid receptors, preventing endorphin-driven dopamine release and reducing euphoria |
| **Nalmefene / Naltrexone** | Two specific opiate antagonists used in gambling disorder treatment |
| **Double-blind** | Neither participant nor researcher knows who is in the drug or placebo condition |
| **Placebo** | Inactive pill identical in appearance to the real drug; controls for expectation effects |
| **Y-BOCS** | Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale — used here to measure gambling symptom severity |
| **Covert sensitisation** | Therapy pairing mental images of unwanted behaviour with aversive imagery (e.g., vomiting) to create conditioned avoidance |
| **Imaginal desensitisation** | Therapy using relaxation + personalised scripts to extinguish conditioned arousal to gambling/stealing triggers |
| **Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)** | Tensing then releasing muscle groups to achieve deep physiological relaxation |
| **Behaviour Completion Mechanism** | McConaghy's theory: incomplete behavioural sequences create tension that drives compulsive repetition |
| **Aphantasia** | Inability to form mental images — makes imagery-based therapies less effective |
| **Comorbid** | Having two or more diagnosable conditions at the same time |
| **Idiographic** | Focused on the individual; not aiming to generalise |
| **Nomothetic** | Focused on drawing universal conclusions that apply across a population |
| **SOGS** | South Oaks Gambling Screen — diagnostic/severity tool for gambling disorder |
